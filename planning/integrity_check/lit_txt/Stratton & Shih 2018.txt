Identifying Weaknesses in Eddy-Viscosity Models for Predicting
Film Cooling via Large-Eddy Simulations

Downloaded by UNIVERSITY OF VIRGINIA on August 14, 2018 | http://arc.aiaa.org | DOI: 10.2514/6.2018-4734

Zachary T. Stratton∗ and Tom I-P. Shih†
Purdue University, West Lafayette, IN, 47906
Large eddy (LES) and Reynolds-Averaged Navier-Stokes (RANS) simulations were performed to investigate modeling weaknesses in two popular RANS models, realizable k- and
shear-stress transport (SST), in predicting film cooling of a flat plate. The cooling jets issue
from a plenum through one row of circular holes of diameter D and length 4.7D that are
inclined at 35o relative to the plate. The following parameters were studied: blowing ratio (BR
= 0.5 and 1.0) and density ratio (DR = 1.1 and 1.6). The LES and RANS solutions generated
were validated by comparing with data from PIV and thermal measurements. The LES results
agreed well, while RANS results showed large errors. Results obtained show the turbulent and
thermal structure of the jets predited by the two RANS models to differ cconsiderably. However,
both models are consistent in under predicting the spread of the film-cooling jet. The counterrotating vortex pair (CRVP) dominates the interaction of the jet and crossflow in the near-wall
region, and neither RANS model could predict the strength and structure of the interactions.
The gradient-diffusion and Boussinesq hypotheses in the two RANS models were evaluated by
using the LES data; regions of counter-gradient diffusion and stress-strain misalignment were
identified. Comparing LES and RANS results show regions near the film-cooling hole where
the realizable k- and SST models predict eddy-viscosities that lead to incorrect Reynold’s
stresses.

II. Introduction
as turbine engines can achieve greater thermal efficiency by increasing the temperature of the gas that enter its
turbine component. The temperatures sought far exceed the melting point of the turbine’s material. Therefore, the
turbine must be cooled to keep the thermal loads on the material at acceptable levels for structural integrity and service
life. One widely used technique to cool the turbine material is film cooling, where some of the cooler air that has been
bled from the compressor is passed through small holes to form an insulating layer or film between the hot gas and the
turbine material. These holes are often angled or shaped in a way that maximizes the coverage of the insulating film.
Film-cooling is one class of jet-in-crossflow (JICF) where the jet is angled, typically around 35◦ with respect to the
crossflow, such that the wall has a strong influence on the resulting flow physics. JICF has been a difficult and intriguing
problem in fluid dynamics due to the large diversity of different flow structures present in the flow at any one time, with
the most significant structure being the counter-rotating vortex pair (CRVP) [1–3]. An extensive amount of research has
been done to better understand film-cooling flows [4, 5] . Of particular interest is how film-cooling efficiency can be
increased by changing parameters such as geometry, blowing ratio, density ratio, and upstream boundary layer.
Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) has been useful in providing understanding of film-cooling flows and how
design and operating parameters affect the film cooling. However, many early studies have shown RANS to be ineffective
in predicting trends in film cooling. This is because RANS based on eddy viscosity models are unable to predict the
high degree of anisotropy in film-cooling flows. In particular, they are unable to capture the correct lateral spreading of
the cooling jet [6–8]. Nevertheless, RANS continues to be used because of its efficient and fast turn-around time in
generating solutions. Thus, there is considerable interest to improve turbulence models used in RANS for film cooling,
models that could account for anisotropy in some way. Bergeles et al. [9] was one of the first to apply an anisotropic
turbulence model to a jet-in-crossflow by multiplying the eddy-viscosity in the lateral direction by a correction factor
that varied based on wall-normal distance. This correction was based on pipe-flow data and was only applicable in the
log-law region and above; nevertheless, the model predicted higher lateral spreading. Lakehal et al. [10] extended
Bergeles’ model to the viscous sublayer by increasing the correction factor. While this improved the lateral spreading,
the peak values of adiabatic effectiveness were underestimated. Azzi and Lakehal [11] used an explicit algebraic stress
model to calculate each Reynolds stress in combination with a two-layer model to account for near-wall anisotropy.
While this model showed some improvement over isotropic turbulence models, it was still unable to capture the lateral
spreading. Lakehal [12] further extended the anisotropic model of [9, 10] by basing the near-wall velocity scale (v 0 v 0)
and dissipation () on channel-flow DNS data; additionally turbulent Prandtl number (Prt ) is based on channel-flow
DNS data and varies with Rey . The anisotropic model in combination with the variable Prt significantly improved
cooling predictions for film-cooling of a flat plate, but underestimated cooling over a turbine blade. Jones et al. [13]
employs the v 2 f − kω model for film-cooling flows, but little improvement is seen over isotropic models; however,
when combined with a variable Prt formulation, better lateral spreading of the coolant is observed. Liu et al. [14, 15]
studies the effect of Prt using isotropic eddy-viscosity models and finds that allowing the Prt to vary laterally or as a
function of non-dimensional temperature can improve cooling predictions for round holes at both low and high BRs.
However, it was found that solely varying Prt has little affect on the cooling predictions for a slot jet, where the wall has
significant influence on the flow field. Li et al. [16, 17] devised an algebraic anisotropic eddy-viscosity model and
scalar flux model where they multiply µt by correction factors derived from an algebraic Reynolds stress model to get
µt,i j . This model improves cooling predictions by better capturing lateral spreading and cooling magnitude; however
the separation and reattachment near the hole does not match experimental data.

High resolution LES data can provide insight for turbulence-model development. A limitation of some of the
previous studies [18–20] is that the analysis is limited to one set of parameters (e.g. BR = 1, DR = 1). Film cooling
performance is very sensitive to the flow parameters, so it is desirable to analyze the RANS assumptions over a range of
parameters. The objective of the current study is to compare the film-cooling LES results of Stratton and Shih [21] to
two RANS models (k- and SST k-ω) for a range of parameters (BR = 0.5 and 1.0 and DR = 1.1 and 1.6) in order to
find regions where the RANS assumptions are weak and can be improved.

III. Numerical Method
IV. Problem Description and Numerical Setup
The problem studied is shown in Fig. 1. It consists of a flat plate to be film cooled with cooling jets issuing from a
plenum through a row of cylindrical holes of diameter, D. The cooling holes are spaced 3D apart, have an aspect ratio
of L/D = 4.7, and an injection angle of 35◦ . The domain extends 20D downstream of the hole.
The operating conditions are chosen to yield a Mach number of M = 0.1, DR = 1.1 and 1.6, and BR = 0.5 and 1.0.
These parameters give rise to four different velocity ratios ranging from 0.313 to 0.909. The Reynolds numbers for DR
= 1.1 and 1.6, based on hole diameter and freestream velocity, are Re = 5135 and Re = 6160, respectively.
The turbulent boundary layer, measured 5D upstream of the hole, has a boundary layer thickness of δ = 1.2D and
momentum thickness of δmom = 0.13D, which corresponds to a Reδmo m = 670. A summary of cases is presented in
Table 1. The chosen flow conditions correspond to those studied in the experiment by Eberly and Thole [22]; the current
results while be validated with these measurements.

A. LES Setup
The LES simulations were performed with the Navier-Stokes solver, FDL3DI [23, 24]. It solves the unfiltered,
compressible, three-dimensional Navier-Stokes equations using a finite-difference approach utilizing sixth-order
compact operators. To eliminate spurious modes, an eighth-order low-pass filter is applied to filter high-frequency and
high-wavenumber components of the solution.
The computational domain consists of 16 overset structured blocks. The mesh consists of grid spacings of ∆x + = 9,
0.4 < ∆y + < 8, ∆z+ = 5 upstream of the hole and similar values downstream of the hole; the total mesh size consists of
35 × 106 grid points.
In the LES simulations the inflow boundary condition is a laminar boundary layer profile with δ = 0.4D. Transition
to turbulence is controlled via a body-force trip placed 1.5D downstream of the inlet, and it is positioned such that
Reδmo m = 670 5D upstream of the hole. Additional details regarding LES setup can be found in Stratton and Shih [21].
B. RANS Setup
The RANS simulations were performed by using the commercial software FLUENT Version 17.0. In this study, the
flow is modeled by the ensemble-averaged continuity, momentum (compressible Navier-Stokes), and energy equations
with Sutherland’s model for viscosity. Two turbulence models for closing the ensemble-averaged equations were studied.
The first is the shear-stress transport (SST) version of k-ω. With this model, all equations can be integrated to the wall,
through the viscous sub-layer. The second model is realizable k- with the two-layer model of Chen and Patel [25] used
in the near-wall region. The curvature correction of Spalart and Shur [26] was applied to both models.
A pressure-based segregated solver, SIMPLE, was used to generate solutions. The fluxes for all equations at the cell
faces were interpolated using the second-order upwind scheme. Pressure was computed using second-order accuracy.
For all computations, iterations were continued until all residuals plateaued below 10−5 for continuity and 10−6 for
momentum, energy, and turbulence quantities, ensuring convergence to a steady-state. An unstructured mesh consisting
of 2.5 × 106 hexahedral cells.
In the RANS simulations a turbulent boundary layer profile with Reδmo m = 670 is directly specified at the inlet
along with k and  (or ω) profiles. Only half of the domain is simulated with a symmetry boundary condition specified
through the hole and at the lateral boundary. An atmospheric back pressure is enforced on the hot-gas outlet. At the
plenum inlet a mass-flow rate yielding the desired blowing ratio and density ratio is specified.

VI. Conclusion
A computational study based on LES and RANS were performed to examine the limitations of two RANS turbulence
models, realizable k −  and SST, for predicting film-cooling flows. In general, RANS under predicted adiabatic
effectiveness by failing to predict the correct lateral spreading. Realizable k −  tended to under predict the CRVP
strength, while SST over predicted the strength. Realizable k −  is better able to capture the trend and magnitude of the
turbulent kinetic energy, but k near the wall is under predicted, where as SST under predicts k throughout the entire jet
region.
LES data was also used to examine the Boussinesq hypothesis. There is some evidence of misalignment of the stress
and strain at higher V R, suggesting that a curvature correction should be utilized. However, in general, the assumed
linear relationship between stress and strain fields is satisfactory. Using the method of least-squares an “ideal” scalar
eddy-viscosity can be solved for using the LES data. This eddy-viscosity showed that realizable k −  over predicted the
eddy-viscosity while SST under predicted it. This result correlates with the relative strengths of the CRVP predicted by
each model. However, the eddy viscosity from LES approaches zero too far from the wall. Further analysis reveals that
