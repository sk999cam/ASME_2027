ABSTRACT

All modern gas turbines, from a small business jet to a large
civil engine, make use of film cooling within the HP turbine.
Transition on the blade surfaces has a large impact on blade heat
transfer and profile loss. Many studies have considered the role
of freestream turbulence and surface roughness on transition, yet
no systematic study of the role of film cooling on transition has
been reported.
This paper shows that film cooling is as potent at causing
transition as roughness typically found on the suction surface
of end-of-life engines, and more so than freestream turbulence.
For the blades tested, film cooling induced transition begins at
the rear of the suction surface, the transition point moving forward to the film cooling hole with a doubling in Reynolds number.
Averaging the acceleration parameter over a fixed distance downstream of the cooling hole collapses the transitional behaviour
for varied film ejection locations. A model is proposed to capture
the Reynolds sensitivity of film cooling induced transition.
The simultaneous presence of freestream turbulence and
roughness as well as film cooling, are shown to enhance transition
compared to any mechanism in isolation, despite the localised nature of the film cooling holes. It is likely that the combined effect
of the mechanisms is maximised when each mechanism in isolation would result in transition at a similar Reynolds number.
Therefore a method of mapping each transition mechanism in
isolation is demonstrated to identify which mechanism is dominant, the proximity of other mechanisms and hence their likely
enhancement of transition.
By testing combinations of film cooling, roughness and
freestream turbulence typical of a cooled HP rotor the transitional
region is found to cover the range 300, 000 < 𝑅𝑒 𝑥 < 1, 000, 000,
where transition will be primarily dependent on surface roughness and film cooling. Below 𝑅𝑒 𝑥 = 300, 000 transition is likely
via a laminar separation bubble and above 𝑅𝑒 𝑥 = 1, 000, 000 the
suction surface boundary layers will be fully turbulent. Large

1. INTRODUCTION

The transitional boundary layer behaviour within the HP
turbine has implications for both aerodynamic loss and local heat
loads. It is well accepted that natural transition rarely occurs in
this environment with either transition via a laminar separation
bubble or bypass transition being the most common routes to
turbulence. Mayle [1] provided a review of transitional effects in
gas turbines that covers the majority of work prior to 1991. From
this review five transition mechanisms can be extracted that are
relevant for the HP turbine:

All data presented is from blading representative of current
state-of-the-art HP turbine rotors. Most notably it is designed
using aerodynamically driven design methods ensuring smooth
acceleration and no un-necessary diffusion [7].
The cascade features a variable pitch cassette system with
either 5 or 6 blades depending on the pitch-to-chord. The aspect
ratio, 𝐴𝑅 = 𝐻/𝐶 𝑥 , is 2.42, giving a 2D region of over 70% span.
Figure 2 shows a schematic of the cascade cross section. Flow
moves from left to right, passing an interchangeable turbulence
grid before entering the blade cassette, which is inclined to the
blade inlet angle. Periodicity of the cascade is obtained with truncated streamlines, labelled in Fig.2. The shape of these surfaces
are generated using streamlines from within the passage extracted
from 2D CFD of the blade row. The use of streamlines as the
solid boundaries mimics an infinite cascade. Streamlines from
within the passage are advantageous over the stagnation streamline as they avoid the diffusion up to the stagnation point, keeping
boundary layers thin. This method was found to maintain the periodicity in exit Mach number to within ±0.3% across the central
two passages.
The blades are manufactured with an aluminium core and
a polycarbonate suction surface. This composite construction
avoids trailing edge deflection, whilst the low thermal conductivity of the polycarbonate enables IR imaging of recovery temperature. The two part construction also enables simplified manufacture of an internal plenum to feed the cooling holes.

2. RESEARCH AIMS

This paper aims to characterise the transitional behaviour of
a typical HP rotor under engine representative freestream turbulence, surface roughness and film cooling. The three research
questions that are answered are:
1. Over which ranges of Reynolds number does transition
change from that via a laminar separation to bypass transition
for cooled HP turbine rotor blades with realistic roughness
and turbulence levels?
2. What is the behaviour of transition due to film cooling with
Reynolds number and does it interact with both freestream
turbulence and roughness?

Film Cooling

Laid back fan shaped cooling holes are investigated at a range
of surface positions typical of the last row on a HP rotor suction
surface. In engine this final row is limited to the early suction
surface before Mach numbers become high, this is required to
avoid significant mixing losses [8, 9] and excessive coolant flow
rates for a given blowing ratio. The most downstream location
tested ejects at an isentropic Mach number that is 93% of the
trailing edge value, 𝑀ej /𝑀TE = 0.93, this being later than most
films in engine.
The coolant is fed to an internal plenum from both ends of
the blade each metered individually via ISO standard venturis.
All cooled data shown uses engine scaled cooling holes, matched
velocity ratios and a temperature ratio 𝑇0,coolant /𝑇0,fs = 1.

3. What is the relative importance of each transition mechanism
across the range of engine typical Reynolds numbers?

Separated flow transition occurs in the shear layer of a separation bubble, such as downstream of a laminar separation, this
mechanism being common in LP turbine blades [2]. The next
3 mechanisms are all bypass transition as in each case a perturbation is provided, by the film ejection, roughness elements or
freestream turbulence respectively, bypassing the initial stages of
natural transition and directly producing turbulent spots. Film
bypass is the most unique as the perturbations are discrete and
localised on the blade surface. Reverse transition occurs due to
streamwise acceleration, which above a certain level appears to
damp perturbations returning the boundary layer to a laminar like
state.
Previous work has explored the effects of freestream turbulence and roughness, both in isolation and combination. Some
experiments make use of flat plate tests with imposed pressure
distributions [3, 4], others make use of low speed turbine profiles
[5]. The studies of Stripf et al. [5] and Gramspacher et al. [4]
demonstrate both the role of freestream turbulence and surface
roughness as well as their combined effect when applied together.
Work such as Teng et al. [6] have also evidenced laminar like behaviour downstream of a film cooling hole under accelerating
flow. What is not available in the literature is a study that looks
systematically at the transitional behaviour of a film cooled blade
or the relative and combined impact of film cooling, roughness
and freestream turbulence.

Surface Roughness

The IR imaging is taken with a FLIR A615 camera, which
has a thermal sensitivity of 0.05 ◦ C and an image resolution of
640 × 480 pixels. Approximately 280 pixels are captured in the
streamwise direction per blade, with both the central blades being
captured. In this work no attempt is made to calculate the recovery
temperature, the images and differences between them are used
as a non-intrusive flow visualisation method, with only spatial
quantification. Turbulent and laminar regions can be identified
through changes between images at varied Reynolds numbers.
Figure 1 shows an example IR image of a film cooled blade
viewed axially from downstream, to its right is a schematic displaying the topology of the boundary layer states. On the left of
the image the contours are dark, cold and laminar. Passing left to
right there is a brief lightening as Mach numbers decrease post
peak suction. This is followed by a dark stripe caused by the air in
the laminar separation bubble approaching the static temperature
of the passing flow. Reattachment causes a turbulent boundary
layer and increased recovery factor, resulting in the light region
extending to the trailing edge on the right of the image.

Freestream Turbulence

Freestream turbulence varies by orders of magnitude within
the HP turbine. At combustor exit 𝑇𝑢 > 10% is typical [10].
Within the blade rows this value reduces due to both spatial decay and the acceleration of the bulk flow. Between rows the frame
of reference change reduces the bulk velocity, increasing the turbulence intensity. At inlet to HP rotors, where the inlet Mach
numbers are higher than the nozzle guide vane, inlet turbulence
up to 𝑇𝑢 1 ≤ 8% is typical.
Three levels of turbulence (3%, 6%, 8%) were generated using bar grids designed using the correlations of Roach [11]. These
were located ∼ 9𝐶 𝑥 upstream of the cascade, the values of 𝑇𝑢 1
quoted are those expected at the middle blade, these having been
validated by hot-wire measurements at the appropriate distance.

Boundary layer modelling

Comparisons of transitional behaviour to existing models
from the literature are made through boundary layer modelling.
The midspan of the cascade is representative of 2D flow making
it suitably modelled by a 1D boundary layer solver. In particular
for this study it is the properties of the laminar boundary layer
prior to transition that are of interest and hence Thwaites’ method
is used [12] to make predictions of the boundary layer thickness
and local momentum thickness Reynolds number 𝑅𝑒 𝜃 . When
discussing the transitional behaviour it is often useful to recast
the global Reynolds number in terms of the suction side length
as in Eq. (3), this value being ∼ 2 times higher than 𝑅𝑒 𝑥 for the
tested blade.

Loss Traverses

Loss measurements are taken behind the central two blades
through pneumatic traverses at 0.5𝐶 𝑥 downstream of the trailing
edge, labelled in Fig. 2. A 3-hole pneumatic probe with separate
static sting is used, enabling full measurement of the midspan
flowfield. The 3-hole probe has each hole stacked in the spanwise
direction, to reduce the pitchwise probe size to ∼ 15% of the wake
width at the traverse plane. The angled faces are at 45◦ from the
oncoming flow.
The loss values, 𝑌𝑝 , presented are mixed out to a pitchwise
uniform condition under constant area mixing and thus capture
any subsequent mixing of the wake. Where film cooling is present
the loss is calculated using a control volume featuring two inlet
streams; mainstream and coolant.

In the previous section it was shown that the role of film cooling in HP turbine transition is as significant as levels of roughness
expected in engine and greater than that of free stream turbulence.
However, it remains the least studied of the 3 transition sources.
The data presented in Fig. 7 showed that film induced transition
begins at a range of Reynolds numbers depending on the ejection
location.
The presence of laminar flow downstream of a cooling hole
has previously been attributed to the process of reverse transition1 . Mayle proposes reverse transition is best predicted by the
acceleration parameter, 𝐾, with values above 3×10−6 resulting in
reverse transition. This variable can be expanded to understand
the scaling with global Reynolds number, the result being an inverse dependence on blade Reynolds number for a given loading
distribution.

6. MAPPING MULTI-SOURCE TRANSITION

It was shown that film induced transition also acts in a combined way with roughness and free stream turbulence. On the
understanding that mechanisms provide the maximum combined
effect if in isolation they would both provide a similar transitional
behaviour, or that they would provide little effect if one were well
in advance of the other, it is useful to track the independent effect
of each over a blade surface for various Reynolds numbers.
Figure 13 shows a "transition map" with axes of suction
side surface fraction against global Reynolds number, in this
case 𝑅𝑒 𝑥 . Plotted on it are lines of transition onset location for
each of the three transition mechanisms in isolation, roughness,
freestream turbulence and film cooling. The curves for roughness and freestream turbulence are generated using correlations
from the literature. The curve for film cooling induced transition
makes use of the previously proposed linear variation in transition
location.
There are many correlations for the role of freestream turbulence in transition, with the models of Mayle [1], Abu-Ghannam
and Shaw [13] and Langtry and Menter [14] being popular examples. In the current study the correlation of Langtry and Menter
is used. The local turbulence value is calculated as per the correlation. The importance of the use of local values is discussed by
Praisner and Clark [15], who suggest a frozen turbulence model
is acceptable for the suction side of an aerofoil. In this study it
was found this overpredicted the role of freestream turbulence
compared to the data, with better agreement adopting the relation
proposed by Steelant [16].

The use of such maps comes firstly in evaluating which mechanism is likely to be dominant, resulting in earliest transition, for
differing configurations of roughness, freestream turbulence and
cooling for a given Reynolds number. Secondly, it enables a
comparison of the relative magnitudes of effects from the nondominant mechanisms. If they are similar to that of the primary
mechanism, mutual transition enhancement will be larger than if
the other mechanisms would not produce substantial transition in
isolation.

3. Transition resulting from film cooling works in a combined
way with that caused by surface roughness, resulting in transition at a lower Reynolds number than either in isolation. It
is proposed that maximum superposition of different mechanisms occurs when each in isolation would result in transition at a similar Reynolds number.
4. To evaluate and compare the role of each mechanism in
isolation a transition map is proposed plotting transition
onset location as a fraction of surface length against global
Reynolds number for multiple mechanisms simultaneously.

7. CONCLUSION

An experimental study of bypass transition mechanisms on
a representative HP turbine profile has been undertaken in a variable density linear cascade. The levels of freestream turbulence
and surface roughness were varied over values typical of engine
conditions. Film cooling was included and the ejection location moved over typical locations for the last row on a HP rotor.
Conclusions are drawn that answer the previously noted research
questions:

cooling ejection location typical of a HP rotor, below
𝑅𝑒 𝑥 = 300, 000 transition is likely via a laminar separation
bubble. A typically film cooled blade is likely fully turbulent
by 𝑅𝑒 𝑥 = 1, 000, 000 regardless of freestream turbulence or
roughness levels. This upper bound is dependent on ejection
