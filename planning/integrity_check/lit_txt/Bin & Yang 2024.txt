ABSTRACT
Film cooling is essential for protecting substrate materials from the hot gases in turbomachines. Recent
experiments have explored the effects of compound angles for the 7-7-7 film cooling hole, a baseline shaped
film cooling hole developed at the START lab. However, differences in facility conditions, manufacturing
process uncertainties, uncertainties in flow conditions, and the absence of internal cooling hole measurements
necessitate further investigation to fully understand the impact of compound angles and the associated flow
physics. This study presents direct numerical simulations (DNS) for the baseline 7-7-7 shaped film cooling hole,
examining a range of compound angles and flow conditions paralleling those in prior lab experiments. The
data gleaned offers insights into the flow dynamics within and in the vicinity of the cooling hole, particularly
in areas proximate to the wall. Our findings indicate that increasing the compound angle adversely affects
adiabatic cooling efficiency. A detailed analysis reveals that this decline in efficiency is primarily due to hot
gas intrusion into the cooling hole, the elevation of the cooling jet from the wall, and intensified secondary
flows at higher compound angles. Additionally, the DNS data reveals a logarithmic scaling in the laterally
averaged mean velocity profile and a self-similarity in temperature profiles beyond a certain distance from the
cooling hole.

1. Introduction
Film cooling involves the ejection of coolant through drilled cooling holes on the surface. In contemporary gas turbine systems, it is
anticipated that the internal gas temperature will exceed 2000 𝐾, a
level considerably surpassing the maximum operational temperature
tolerated by the majority of substrate materials without undergoing
substantial degradation. In this context, film cooling serves as the
primary and foremost defense mechanism safeguarding the hot gas path
surfaces against the formidable onslaught of extremely hot gas [1]. The
technology finds widespread application in critical components within
the turbine hot-section, including guide vanes [2,3], blades [4,5],
turbine casings [6], combustor liner [7,8], exhaust nozzle [9,10], and
others.
The inception of film cooling research can be traced back to the
1970s, with a seminal contribution made by Goldstein et al. [11],
who pioneered the demonstration and quantification of film cooling
effects. The recent investigations have further clarified the many factors that impact the efficacy of film cooling. These factors include
mainstream turbulence intensity [12,13], compressibility [14], coolant

blowing ratio [15], inclination angle [16,17], hole length [18], pitchwise spacing [19–21], arrangement [22], pressure gradient [23,24],
coverage and area ratios [25], as well as the expansion angle of the
diffused outlet [26]. Detailed reviews of literature can be found in Refs.
[1,27–29] and are not repeated here for brevity.
This study focuses on shaped cooling holes at a range of compound
angles. Shaped film cooling holes have a diffused outlet that decelerates
the coolant jet and spreads it laterally across protected surfaces. Such
designs have demonstrated superior cooling performance compared
to cylindrical ones. The 7-7-7 film cooling hole serves as a baseline
for shaped film cooling holes [30]. It consists of a meter section and
a diffuser section and has an inclination angle of 30◦ . The property
of the 7-7-7 film cooling hole has been thoroughly investigated, including the effects of in-hole roughness [31], mainstream turbulent
intensity [13,32], blockage [33], meter-diffuser offset [34]. This study
focuses on the effect of the compound angle [35,36]. The compound
angle refers to the angle between the direction of the coolant jet and the
mainstream flow direction. This design parameter is known to impact
the cooling effectiveness [37–39]. Experimental studies have suggested

DNS directly solves the Navier–Stokes equation and is often regarded as the truth [44]. Despite its high cost [45,46], DNS has
become an accessible tool for analyzing film cooling problems, and
many DNSs have been reported, see, e.g., Refs. [47–52]. For instance,
Muldoon et al. [47] employed DNS to determine the optimal pulsing
frequency for maximizing cooling effectiveness in pulsed film cooling
systems. In another study, Fu et al. [50] conducted DNS of film cooling
under varying mainstream Reynolds numbers, discovering a correlation
between increased Reynolds numbers and reduced vortex scale in the
wake. The high spatial resolution and accuracy of DNS are instrumental
in providing detailed insights into vortex structures and the underlying
flow physics [53,54]. However, DNSs of the 7-7-7 hole film cooling configuration, which are more relevant to practical engineering scenarios,
are still lacking.

2.3. Grid and code
A DNS must resolve all eddies within a flow. Given that the Kolmogorov length scale represents the smallest scale in turbulence, the
DNS grid spacing, denoted as 𝛥, should scale with the Kolmogorov
length scale 𝜂, i.e., 𝛥 ∼ 𝜂. The precise grid resolution requirement, however, varies depending on whether the flow is bounded or unbounded.
Ref. [55] set forth the grid resolution requirement in wall-bounded
flows: 𝛥𝑥+ ≈ 12, 𝛥𝑧+ ≈ 7, and 𝛥𝑦+ to be less than 1 in the sublayer
and approximately 4.4 away from the wall. Given that the Kolmogorov
length scale, 𝜂 + , is approximately 1 in the sublayer and increases with
distance from the wall, the grid resolution requirements from Ref. [55]
imply that 𝛥𝑥 ≲ 12𝜂, 𝛥𝑧+ ≲ 7𝜂, and 𝛥𝑦 ≲ 𝜂. Coarser grids have also been
used. For instance, Ref. [56] utilized a grid resolution of 𝛥𝑥+ = 17.8,
𝛥𝑧+ = 8.9, and 𝛥𝑦+ = 0.6 and 8 at the wall and away from the wall,
respectively, which proves to be sufficient to capture the mean flow.
If a DNS meets the established grid resolution requirement, conducting a grid convergence study is often deemed unnecessary, as such
studies are expensive and yield minimal additional insights. Indeed,
recent DNS investigations, see, Refs. [57–65], to name a few, did not
include grid convergence studies.
Ref. [46] seems to be the first grid refinement study that exceeded
the established grid resolution requirements and revealed inadequacies
of the grid resolution requirement in Ref. [55]. While affirming the
adequacy of the established grid resolution requirement for first-order
statistics such as the mean flow, the findings in Ref. [46] indicated that
the standard grid resolution is insufficient for capturing flow intermittency. Further, Ref. [66] demonstrated that first-order statistics, such as
mean flow, achieve grid convergence before variables like velocity rms
and turbulence dissipation. This aligns well with the current practice
of implicit LES. Most implicit LES, if properly done, can obtain grid
convergence for the mean flow, but they are LES rather than DNS

5. Conclusions
This paper presents the first set of direct numerical simulation (DNS)
results for the 7-7-7 film cooling hole at three distinct compound angles: 0◦ , 30◦ , and 60◦ . We detail both instantaneous and time-averaged
12

International Journal of Heat and Mass Transfer 231 (2024) 125816

Y. Bin and X.I.A. Yang

observations of wall shear stress, velocity, and temperature above the
adiabatic wall, near the wall surface, and inside the cooling hole. Given
the challenges of acquiring such data in a laboratory setting, these DNS
results provide new insights into the flow dynamics.
Our findings indicate that an increase in the compound angle correlates with a decrease in laterally averaged cooling effectiveness. This
reduced effectiveness can be attributed to several factors. Firstly, a
larger compound angle facilitates the intrusion of hot fluid into the
cooling hole. Secondly, at higher compound angles, the cooling jet
is observed to lift away from the wall, in contrast to its attachment
to the wall at smaller compound angles. Thirdly, while an increased
compound angle enhances the lateral spread of the coolant – a desirable outcome – it also inadvertently promotes turbulence mixing. This
mixing, in turn, draws hotter fluid towards the wall, adversely affecting
cooling effectiveness.
Last but not least, our analysis of the DNS data yields practical
parameterizations for the laterally averaged mean velocity and temperature profiles. Notably, the laterally averaged temperature profiles
demonstrate self-similarity beyond a dimensionless distance of 𝑥∕𝐷 =
10.
