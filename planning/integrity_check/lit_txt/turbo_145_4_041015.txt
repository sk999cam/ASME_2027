The present work describes the coupled pressure–velocity instability in the suction side
boundary layer of a transonic high-pressure turbine blade. The application case is the
VKI LS89 turbine blade MUR237. Modal decomposition techniques such as the proper
orthogonal decomposition (POD) and the dynamic mode decomposition (DMD) are
applied to data obtained by large eddy simulations (LES) to inspect the existing correlation
between pressure ﬂuctuations induced by traveling pressure waves and the velocity disturbances growing into the boundary layer. To this end, POD and DMD were applied to both
velocity and pressure ﬁelds from the leading edge up to the end of the blade surface. DMD
was computed on a 2D slice oriented parallel to the blade surface inside the boundary layer.
Data were ordered along the curvilinear abscissas, so that the most spatially ampliﬁed
waves were obtained. Interestingly, unstable velocity modes were detected in the front
blade suction side where pressure waves dominate. The unstable velocity modes computed
upstream of the peak suction highlighted the occurrence of elongated streaky structures
showing spanwise ﬂuctuations which are typical of their instability. The comparison of
pressure and velocity modes highlighted the occurrence of pressure-related ﬂuctuations
in the velocity ﬁeld at the same time instants at which streak instability is observed. The
analysis carried out in this work suggests that pressure waves can provoke localized instability of boundary layer streaks, which then break up further downstream causing transition. [DOI: 10.1115/1.4055971]
Keywords: boundary layer development, ﬂuid dynamics and heat transfer phenomena in
compressor and turbine components of gas turbine engines

1 Introduction
It is well known that the unsteadiness occurring in turbomachinery environment strongly affects the evolution of the boundary layer
(BL) evolving over the pressure and especially on the suction side
(SS) of blade proﬁles. In order to further improve the efﬁciency of
such aerodynamic components, there is an increasing need for the
detailed description of the role of unsteady disturbances in promoting the boundary layer instability and transition. The unsteadiness
of the ﬂow forming in blade rows largely arises from the relative
motion of the upstream stator or rotor rows. In this context, the
dominant unsteadiness is constituted by the passing wakes that
impinge the suction side boundary layer and affect its laminarturbulent transition [1,2]. As described in the comprehensive
reviews of Sandberg and Michelassi [3] and Sieverding and
Manna [4], when trailing edge wakes develop in transonic conditions, such as for high-pressure turbines (HPTs) for instance, a
complex interaction of shocks, wakes, and vortex shedding
occurs just downstream of the trailing edge. The wake and vortices
not only cause losses themselves but also have a strong impact on
the downstream blade surface [5]. In this context, pressure waves
constitute an additional source of unsteadiness, whose role in the
transition process, heat transfer, and loss generation is of paramount
importance [6–9].
From the boundary layer point of view, a shock wave produces a
localized adverse pressure gradient, which may cause ﬂow separation. Additionally, adverse pressure gradients provoke the instability of boundary layer structures [10], thus impinging waves may act
promoting the laminar-turbulent transition. The shock BL

fully understood, such as the origins and sources of unsteadiness
associated with the interaction region. In this sense, the increasing
availability of unprecedented amounts of data coming from experiments and high ﬁdelity simulations makes modal decomposition
techniques essential tools for the characterization of the complex
ﬂows evolving in compressor and turbine blade rows under

orthogonal decomposition (POD) and the dynamic mode decomposition (DMD) have been widely applied in previous literature works
dealing with aerodynamic components in several engineering applications (see, e.g., Refs. [16,17]). POD allows ranking the main ﬂow

Refs. [18,19]). On the other hand, DMD provides the most ampliﬁed disturbances in the analyzed ﬂows (see, e.g., Refs. [20,21]).

2 Flow Case
The LES simulations refer to the MUR237 test case. This particular ﬂow case is part of a campaign of measurements (MUR132,
MUR218, and MUR237), performed in the VKI LS89 turbine
linear cascade test facility, where the inﬂuence of turbulence intensity on boundary layer transition and heat transfer was investigated
[25]. The cascade counts a total of ﬁve blades, where the central one
was equipped for both static pressure and heat transfer measurement
devices. Chord length, blade height, and blade pitch were equal to
67.647 mm, 100 mm, and 57.5 mm, respectively. The MUR237
ﬂow case employs a higher inﬂow turbulence level (6%) compared
to the MUR218 (4%) and MUR132 (0.8%) ﬂow cases and therefore, an earlier laminar-to-turbulence transition of the boundary
layer at the blade suction side was found.

3 Large Eddy Simulations
3.1 Numerical Method. In the measurements, the ﬂow was
proven to be reasonably periodic in the pitchwise direction for the

central blade. Furthermore, at mid-span, the inﬂuence of the hub
and shroud vortices was found negligible. Thus, only one blade
channel and only a part of the entire blade height (10%) were considered when generating the computational grid for LES. Periodicity was then used in pitchwise and spanwise directions.
Regarding the LES grid, a conﬁguration with O-blocks around
the blade was used to guarantee higher accuracy close to the
blade wall (y + < 1.2, x + < 30, z + < 40) while maintaining a low
number of grid points in the freestream region where such grid resolution is not needed. As can be seen in the zoomed view in Fig. 1,
the number of grid points along the streamwise direction was
doubled moving towards the blade surface from one O-block to
the consecutive one. The same was done in the spanwise direction.
Between the O-blocks around the blade, an interpolation of
second-order was used to connect the faces [26]. With the
O-blocks approach, the numerical grid counts around 20% fewer
grid points than when a more traditional approach is used, resulting
in 38M points for the current investigation. Regarding the resolution of the numerical grid in the freestream region, it was shown
in Ref. [27] that the grid could accurately resolve the turbulence
decay.
At the inlet and outlet, the boundary conditions were set accordingly to Table 1. Since the synthetic eddy method (SEM) of Jarrin
et al. [28] was used in order to reproduce the incoming turbulent
ﬂow, static pressure and static temperature were imposed instead
of the total values. The average inlet velocity was also set at the
inlet boundary. In Ref. [26], the inﬂuence of different subgridscale (SGS) models and inﬂow boundary conditions on the boundary layer transition for the MUR237 ﬂow case was investigated.
Regarding the synthetic inﬂow turbulence, different combinations
of turbulent length scale, number of incoming eddies, and turbulence intensity were tested. The results showed that the turbulence
length scale is the parameter that mainly inﬂuences transition, and
particularly, the best results could be achieved for a length scale
of 3 mm and a value of inlet turbulence of 6%. Regarding the
SGS model, the wall-adapting local eddy-viscosity (WALE)
model performed the best compared to the standard Smagorinsky
model and the one-equation subgrid-scale kinetic energy model,
and for this reason, it was also used in this work. At the outlet, a
non-reﬂecting boundary condition [29] is used to further increase
the stability of the simulations. For the time iteration, the fourthorder explicit Runge–Kutta method was used with a time-step
equal to 4 * 10−9 s and a maximum value of CFL < 1, whereas for
the computation of the cell ﬂuxes, a weighted essentially nonoscillatory scheme was employed. For the simulations, the
in-house code LINARS was used. More details about the LES validation and the numerical method used for this investigation can be
found in Ref. [26].

The optimum threshold value is estimated directly from the Otsu’s
method by minimizing the intra-class variance [31]. Thus, the intermittency function was obtained by averaging along the spanwise
direction and in time.

the turbulent inﬂow.

proper orthogonal decomposition and the dynamic mode decomposition to make the reader familiar with these techniques. The variants and the algorithm adopted in this work are also discussed.
Proper orthogonal decomposition is a modal decomposition
technique that was proposed ﬁrst by Lumley [33] for the analysis
of turbulent ﬂows. Given a certain velocity ﬁeld which is a function
of space and time (i.e., u(x, t)), the POD procedure provides spatial

In the present work, the DMD algorithm is computed initially from
the leading edge up to the trailing edge of the blade. More precisely,
the extension of the DMD domain is gradually increased in the
streamwise direction to investigate the overall ampliﬁcation of
velocity ﬂuctuations from the laminar up to the turbulent regions
of the BL. For each spatial region considered, the most unstable
mode is recorded based on the real part of the corresponding eigenvalue, thus providing the spatial distribution of the most ampliﬁed
disturbances for the entire blade surface (see also the previous
authors’ work [36]). Then, based on the global response of the
DMD procedure, we focused on speciﬁc regions of interest
located in the front and the rear suction side of the blade. This
allowed us to better highlight the similarities observed between
the most unstable pressure and velocity ﬂuctuations, together with
the main disturbances driving the laminar-turbulent transition of
the boundary layer.

5 Conclusions
The present work describes the application of the extended
proper orthogonal decomposition and dynamic mode decomposition on data obtained from the large eddy simulation of a transonic
HPT blade. The application case is the VKI LS89 turbine blade
MUR237. Numerical simulations were performed with elevated
inlet turbulence that was shown to promote the formation of elongated streaky structures. These latter break down at the rear
suction side of the blade, thus leading to the laminar-turbulent transition of the boundary layer.
The application of POD to the unsteady pressure ﬁeld highlighted
the occurrence of traveling waves, whose signatures were found
also in the extended POD modes of the streamwise velocity component. Particularly, 2D spanwise oriented structures were detected in
the extended modes of the streamwise ﬂuctuating velocity together
with the signatures of BL streaks and their breakdown events. This
clearly indicates that pressure unsteadiness and streaks related ﬂuctuations are correlated in a statistical sense. Additionally, the
present EPOD procedure was shown to be suitable for the construction of reduced order models of pressure–velocity interaction terms
in Reynolds stress transport equations.
The analysis of the DMD eigenavalues showed the occurrence of
localized peaks of the spatial growth rate of streamwise ﬂuctuations
in the front part of the blade. Interestingly, these peaks have the
same periodicity of the traveling waves captured by the pressure
POD modes and the corresponding extended modes of the streamwise velocity.

Downstream of the peak suction position, the effects due to pressure unsteadiness on the growth of velocity ﬂuctuations were found
to reduce, despite being still recognizable. Indeed, the comparison
of DMD modes of the pressure and velocity ﬁelds showed that
the inception of turbulent spots, occurring as a consequence of
streak breakdown, may be forced by the localized pressure gradients
associated with traveling waves. Therefore, the present study indicates that for transonic HPT proﬁles, the laminar-to-turbulent transition occurs due to the combined forcing of the freestream vortices
and traveling pressure waves, with these latter playing an important
role in the instability process of streaky structures.
