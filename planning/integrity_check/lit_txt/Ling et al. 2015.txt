It has been previously demonstrated that Reynolds-averaged Navier–Stokes (RANS) simulations do not accurately capture the mixing between the coolant flow and the main flow
in trailing edge slot film cooling configurations. Most RANS simulations use a fixed turbulent Schmidt number of either 0.7 or 0.85 to determine the turbulent scalar flux, based on
the values for canonical flows. This paper explores the extent to which RANS predictions
can be improved by modifying the value of the turbulent Schmidt number. Experimental
mean 3D velocity and coolant concentration data obtained using magnetic resonance
imaging techniques are used to evaluate the accuracy of RANS simulations. A range of
turbulent Schmidt numbers from 0.05 to 1.05 is evaluated and the optimal turbulent
Schmidt number for each case is determined using an integral error metric which accounts
for the difference between RANS and experiment throughout a three-dimensional region
of interest (ROI). The resulting concentration distribution is compared in detail with the
experimentally measured coolant concentration distribution to reveal where the fixed turbulent Schmidt number assumption fails. It is shown that the commonly used turbulent
Schmidt number of 0.85 overpredicts the surface effectiveness in all cases, particularly
when the k-omega shear stress transport (SST) model is employed, and that a lower value
of the turbulent Schmidt number can improve predictions. [DOI: 10.1115/1.4029206]

Introduction
Trailing edge slot film cooling is a commonly used active cooling scheme for the thin trailing edges of turbine blades. Modeling
the turbulent mixing in this flow has proven to be challenging.
Because of the three-dimensionality of the flow and the inherent
unsteadiness of vortex shedding off of the slot lip, RANS simulations struggle to predict the mixing of the coolant flow with the
main flow [1–6]. Large eddy simulations (LES) [7,8] and detached
eddy simulations (DES) [3] can give more accurate film cooling
predictions than RANS in film cooling flows. However, because
of the computational cost of LES and DES, their sensitivity to
inlet boundary conditions and their reliance on wall models for
high Reynolds number cases, it is worthwhile to investigate methods for improving RANS predictions.
In many commercial RANS software packages, the turbulent
diffusivity is determined by a fixed turbulent Schmidt number as
shown in Eq. (1), with a default value of Sct ¼ 0.85. This model
involves the Reynolds analogy between momentum and scalar

models, have been shown to yield improved accuracy in flows

popular model both because of its simplicity and because of its
numerical stability-enhancing properties. Therefore, this paper

simulations of trailing edge film cooling configurations by comparing the simulation results to new velocity and concentration
data.
Two different eddy viscosity models are implemented in conjunction with a range of turbulent Schmidt numbers from 0.05 to
1.05, seeking optimal agreement with experimental data. The
computations are compared to multiple data sets to assess the generality of the conclusions. To facilitate this, new data sets were
acquired for a typical trailing edge configuration operated at three
different blowing ratios (BRs). Comparisons are also made to a
previously reported experiment that included an internal blockage
in each slot [13].
The experiments provided time-averaged three-component velocity data measured throughout the test volume using magnetic
resonance velocimetry (MRV) [14] and three-dimensional coolant
concentration measured using magnetic resonance concentration
(MRC) [15]. The coolant concentration represents temperature in
the actual application by the passive scalar analogy. This is appropriate because the flow field is highly three-dimensional with large
scale mixing dominating over molecular and thermal diffusion.
Therefore, the turbulent Schmidt number should be nearly identical to the turbulent Prandtl number.
In order to make a quantitative comparison between the RANS
simulations and experimental results, an integral error metric is
used. Using this metric, optimal Sct values are determined for
each of the four cases. The corresponding optimal concentration
distributions are then compared to the experimental concentration
distributions to reveal where the model breaks down. The purpose
of this paper is not to recommend the usage of RANS or a fixed
turbulent Schmidt number. Rather, the goal of this paper is to
examine the effect of varying Sct, to determine how far off the
optimal Sct is from the common default value of 0.85, and to see
in which regions of the flow the Reynolds analogy is least
appropriate.

The experimental apparatus has been well-documented in the
previous studies [13,15,16]. The inlet consists of a series of three
diffusers equipped with grids, followed by a 2:1 contraction for
flow conditioning. The test section, shown in Fig. 1, holds an
uncambered airfoil. Contraction inserts provide a favorable pressure gradient over the airfoil trailing edge region, as would be the
case for a realistic turbine blade. The airfoil is interchangeable, so
that airfoils with different trailing edge film cooling configurations
can be tested.
Schematics of the two airfoils of interest are shown in Fig. 2.
Both airfoils are based on a NACA-0012 with a nominal chord
length of 300 mm, but with the trailing edge blunted to give an
actual length of 283 mm. The airfoil aspect ratio was 0.27.
Because the airfoil was symmetric, the main flow was relatively
spanwise uniform, with weaker secondary flow patterns than
would be expected for a highly cambered turbine blade. The airfoils each have an internal radially fed coolant plenum that leads
into a bank of pin fins and then through three slots, separated by
lands. Two trailing edge configurations are investigated in this
study. The baseline case has tapered lands 9 mm wide at the slot
exit and a slot width of 15 mm. The case with a blockage in the
slot, which will be called the “island airfoil,” has straight lands
7 mm wide and a slot width of 17 mm. Both airfoils have slot
heights h of 5 mm. These and other key airfoil dimensions are
summarized in Table 1. The island airfoil was shown to have a
dramatically different mean velocity field and concentration distribution [13], so it is used to determine the sensitivity of the optimal
turbulent Schmidt number to geometric variation.

Experimental Methods
The experimental measurements were obtained on a 3.0 T
GE magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scanner with a transmit–
receive head coil. The use of MRI techniques necessitated that the
working fluid be water. Nevertheless, these experiments have

relevance to air flows. The turbulent mixing dominates over the
molecular mixing, making the effects of varying fluid properties
negligible. Furthermore, while the absolute Mach number in film
cooling flows can be near 1.0, the convective Mach number,
which is a measure of the relative velocity of the coolant and
main flow, is usually less than 0.5. It is this convective Mach number that is most important in determining the mixing properties of
the flow [17], and for convective Mach numbers less than 0.5, the
turbulent structures responsible for most of the mixing should be
well-represented by incompressible flow [17–19].

MRV
The 3D mean velocity fields were obtained using MRV. This technique gives the time-averaged velocity field on a Cartesian grid using
a phase contrast sequence detailed by Elkins et al. [14]. The working
fluid is a weak copper sulfate solution (0.06 M) in water. The copper
sulfate serves to increase the signal to noise ratio with respect to
using pure water but has a negligible impact on the fluid properties.
The field of view and resolution are summarized in Table 2.
The velocity field was acquired through a series of 12 “flowon” scans and four “flow-off” scans. The flow-off scans were used
for background subtraction and were spaced such that each flowoff scan was followed by three flow-on scans. The flow-off scans
from directly before and after each set of flow-on scans were averaged and then subtracted from those flow-on scans. The resulting
velocity fields were averaged over the 12 flow-on scans. The
uncertainty in the velocity field, based on the analysis of Pelc
et al. [20] and validated by Elkins et al. [14,21], was 5.1% at one
standard deviation.

MRC
The 3D mean coolant concentration distribution was measured
using MRC. This procedure has been documented in several previous studies [13,15]. Two different fluids were used: pure water and
a 0.015 M copper sulfate solution in water. Because the copper sulfate solution gives a much stronger signal in comparison to the
pure water, the copper sulfate solution could be used to differentiate the main flow from the coolant flow. The molarity of the copper
sulfate solution was chosen to ensure that the signal intensity would
vary linearly with concentration in this range. The resolution and
field of view for these experiments are summarized in Table 2.
Four different types of scans are used. In background scans,
which are used for background subtraction, both the coolant flow
and the main flow consist of pure water. Reference scans are used
to normalize the maximum signal intensity, so both the coolant
flow and main flow consist of copper sulfate solution. In standard
runs, the main flow is water and the coolant flow is copper sulfate,

One important quantity that can be determined based on the
coolant concentration distribution is the adiabatic surface effectiveness. Because the passive scalar analogy for temperature is being
employed, the surface effectiveness is given by the coolant concentration at the cutback surface. The analogous condition for adiabaticity is no penetration, which is preserved perfectly. Because of
the finite resolution of MRC, partial volume effects, in which a volumetric pixel (“voxel”) lies partially in the surface and partially in
the flow, thereby giving an artificially lower concentration value,
make it difficult to measure the concentration right at the surface.
To mitigate partial volume effects, the surface effectiveness was
taken to be the maximum concentration within 1 mm (0.2 h) of the
surface. The uncertainty of the surface effectiveness measurements
was calculated by finding the variance of each of the four scan
types over the region relevant to determining the surface effectiveness and then using the Student-t distribution to calculate the 95%
confidence interval for each scan type. The uncertainty in the concentration was then determined using the rule of partial derivatives
based on the uncertainty in each scan type. An additional 3%
uncertainty was combined with this based on the partial volume
effect, yielding a total uncertainty of 5.9% at 95% confidence.

Conclusions
RANS simulations of coolant/main-flow mixing in a trailing
edge slot film cooling configuration were compared to 3D
experimental data obtained using MRI techniques. A range of
turbulent Schmidt numbers were tested to determine which
value gave the best match to experimental data and where the
Reynolds analogy breaks down. It was shown that k-omega SST
Journal of Engineering for Gas Turbines and Power

did not accurately capture either the velocity field or the concentration distribution and required very low turbulent Schmidt
numbers to give even a rough estimate of the surface effectiveness. RKE not only did an overall better job of predicting the
velocity field and concentration distribution, but also required a
value of Sct lower than 0.85 to give the best match for the surface effectiveness. While the optimal value of Sct varied
between BRs and film cooling configurations, Sct ¼ 0.45 gave
improved surface effectiveness predictions in all cases in comparison to Sct ¼ 0.85. However, this value of Sct did not necessarily accurately capture the coolant concentration distribution
elsewhere in the flow. Because MRI techniques provide the full
3D concentration distribution, it was also possible to make comparisons along the slot centerplane. This analysis revealed that
by tuning the turbulent Schmidt number to get a more accurate
surface effectiveness, the coolant distribution elsewhere became
less accurate.
Our main conclusions are enumerated below:
(1) k-omega SST underpredicts the turbulent viscosity in the
pin fin array and in the wake of the slot lip, leading to inaccurate velocity and concentration distributions.
(2) The default value of Sct ¼ 0.85 overpredicts the surface
effectiveness in all cases.
(3) A fixed turbulent Schmidt number is not able to accurately
predict the coolant concentration distribution everywhere in
the cutback region, demonstrating that the Reynolds analogy is not sufficient in this flow field.
It is therefore suggested that more realistic models for the turbulent scalar flux are required in order to accurately model this
flow. One possible avenue for improving RANS modeling of trailing edge configurations would be to use values of turbulent diffusivity or Sct determined from high fidelity LES simulations of
such flows. However, it remains an open question whether such
extrapolations can generalize across variations in cutback geometries and flow parameters.

lower for k-omega SST than for RKE. This is not unexpected
given that Fig. 7 showed that k-omega SST predicted much lower
levels of turbulent viscosity. The optimal values of Sct for
k-omega SST are approximately ten times smaller than the default
value of 0.85. Most likely, this is due at least in part to underprediction of the turbulent viscosity, not just to differences in
momentum transport versus scalar transport. Since the integral
error metric is the average error magnitude over the ROI, it is also
apparent that in ROI-1, RKE more accurately predicts the concentration distribution, leading to lower error levels at the optimal Sct
than in the k-omega SST simulations. Furthermore, in ROI-1,
Sct ¼ 0.85 does not seem like an unreasonable assumption for
RKE at a BR of 0.7. However, at the higher BRs, lower values of
Sct are required for both ROIs and both trailing edge configurations. The optimal Sct value for the island airfoil is relatively consistent with the baseline case at BR ¼ 1.3, indicating that the
optimal Sct is not highly sensitive to the presence of internal structures in the slot or to the land geometry.
However, in ROI-2, which is more relevant to surface effectiveness predictions, RKE requires a lower Sct than it did in ROI-1,
nearer to half of the default value of 0.85. This is because RKE
predicts low levels of turbulent viscosity in the boundary layer
near the breakout surface. The fact that a different Sct value is
required to accurately calculate the concentration in different
regions of the flow undermines the validity of the Reynolds analogy for this flow.
It is useful to compare the concentration distribution from the
optimal RANS simulations to the experimental values. Figure 10
shows contours of concentration for the baseline configuration at
BR ¼ 1.3 for the values of Sct that were optimal for ROI-2, the
near-surface ROI. The contours of concentration from the experiment are shown for comparison.
Figures 10(a), 10(c), and 10(e) show contours of surface
effectiveness. Based on these plots, RKE seems to do a reasonably good job of predicting both the centerline surface effectiveness and its spanwise variation. In contrast, k-omega SST does
not capture the spanwise variation correctly, perhaps because it
did not accurately predict the spanwise variation of the streamwise velocity. Figures 10(b), 10(d), and 10(f) show contours of
coolant concentration on the slot symmetry plane. From these
contours, it becomes clear that neither RKE nor k-omega SST is
accurately capturing the full 3D concentration distribution.
While Sct ¼ 0.25 was the optimal value for RKE in ROI-2, it
was lower than the optimal value in ROI-1. Accordingly,
Sct ¼ 0.25 gives a good match for the surface effectiveness, but
overestimates the mixing in the wake of the slot lip. In fact,
only a very thin layer of high concentration persists near the surface, preserved by the layer of low turbulent viscosity near the
breakout surface. The symmetry plane contours for k-omega
SST are also inaccurate, overestimating the mixing on the coolant side of the slot lip and underestimating the mixing on the
main flow side.
Based on the surface effectiveness contours and velocity
field, it seems as if RKE is substantially more accurate than
k-omega SST in these configurations. However, by comparing
centerplane concentration contours, it becomes clear that in
order to get the right surface effectiveness, the turbulent
Schmidt number must be tuned in such a way that it gives the
wrong concentration distribution elsewhere. Such a fix cannot
be expected to be reliable.
