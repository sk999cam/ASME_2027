ABSTRACT
Film cooling introduces discrete perturbations on the suction surface boundary layer of high-pressure turbine blades,
acting as trip sites for transition. However, the strong favourable pressure gradient present within turbines can reverse this
effect through acceleration induced reverse transition Mayle (1991). This paper uses direct numerical simulations (DNS)
to demonstrate how perturbation location, size and spacing control the transition mechanism, specifically whether bypass
or separated flow transition occurs, through the acceleration parameter (K) and its control of turbulent kinetic energy tke.
Simulations are conducted on a flat palate with conditions representative of a Reynolds number based on surface length
(ReL ) of ∼ 400, 000, approximately equivalent to a Reynolds number based on axial chord Rex of ∼ 250, 000. Two parameters are varied in parallel: First, the location of the film ejection, in this study modelled as hemispherical trips, is
varied between the leading edge and peak suction. Second, the size of the hemispherical trip is varied to simulate various
film cooling configurations. Key findings reveal that acceleration suppresses tke production when K > Kcrit = 3e−6, with
differences in tke between cases arising primarily from wall normal and lateral velocity fluctuations (v′ and w′ ). The predominant turbulence length-scales decay differentially downstream of the trip, with smaller scales dissipating more rapidly
in strongly accelerating regions. Larger trips exhibit reverse transitional behaviour controlled by local Ktrip relative to Kcrit ,
while smaller trips are suppressed by the near wall shear-layer regardless of acceleration. Optimally-spaced trips excite
maximum non-modal transient growth and achieve complete bypass transition independent of acceleration, though this
mechanism is highly spanwise spacing sensitive with ∼ 12% reduction in spacing removing this effect.

INTRODUCTION
Mayle (1991) summarises various bypass transition mechanisms possible over a HPT blade categorising them into
primary mechanisms: free stream turbulence (FST), periodic wakes and laminar separation bubbles and, secondary mechanisms: roughness and film cooling ejection. The success of these mechanisms are governed by the local value of acceleration
parameter (K), see Equation 1, at their point of action. Film cooling and roughness were said to be a secondary mechanism
with transition mainly being a result the unsteady interactions of all of the other mechanisms. Multiple studies have found
differing behaviours as a result of the the impact of film cooling ejection.

Teekaram et al. (1991) and Schmidt and Bogard (1995) did initial studies on film cooling flows in regions of high mainstream
acceleration at large Rex ∼ 2e6. Both papers used moderate accelerations, far below the relaminerisation limit, and measure
cooling effectiveness which can be interpreted as a proxy for local boundary layer state. The results found a quicker decay
in effectiveness, when compared to zero pressure gradient cases, caused by an increase in heat transfer between the gas
and the blade likely due to a more turbulent boundary layer. For an initially laminar boundary layer which is subsequently
perturbed by coolant ejection Launder and York (1974) found that for Ke j ∼ 2e − 6 there was a 30% improvement in centreline cooling effectiveness due to the maintenance of a laminar/transitional boundary layer. Kanani et al. (2018) showed an
increase in effectiveness immediately following coolant ejection from a slot indicating that a more laminar like boundary
layer is sustained. Using the previously cited papers Coletti et al. (2013) attributed the varying effect of acceleration on
span-averaged effectiveness to four competing mechanisms. The jet deflection towards the boundary layer and likelihood of
laminar-turbulent transition being reduced by strong acceleration improved effectiveness. Conversely, the counterrotating
vortex pair, caused by the ejected coolant, is strengthened and the spanwise spread of ejected coolant is prevented both
deteriorated effectiveness. Overall the disarrangement in the affect of acceleration on film cooling induced transition and
the various competing near wall mechanisms lead to the consensus that the particular arrangement of film cooling both in
terms of location and strength is important in determining boundary layer state.
The simplest model of reverse transition used by Mayle (1991), described above, is still the most widespread model used in
design methods currently. In simulations the k − ε turbulence model is used to predict relaminerization in Jones and Launder
(1972) and the k − ω SST turbulence model in conjunction with the γ − Reθ transition model in Langtry and Menter (2009)
are used. The capability of the k − ω SST turbulence model is assessed Bader et al. (2017) which shows that general trends
are captured and it is well tuned to Kcrit = 3e − 6 correctly predicting that reverse transition will not occur below the critical
value. Although the predictions can be reliable, the models depend heavily on empirical transition correlations for generating Reθ ,transition derived from flat plate experiments that do not consider disturbances such as film cooling, roughness or FST.
The location of reverse transition onset and completion are key parameters to define to be used in low order model solvers
particularly in the design stage. Ranjan and Narasimha (2017) argues that since K is based purely on external variables
and takes no account of the dynamics of the boundary layer, such as the size or perturbation or sustained acceleration, it
2

cannot be a strict criterion for the onset of reverse transition. Clark et al. (2024) showed that averaging the value of Ke j
for 10 hole diameters downstream of the point of ejection collapsed the behaviour of the boundary layer into the more
binary turbulent/laminar behaviour as described by Mayle (1991) indicating that integral effects of acceleration also play
a role. The completion of reverse transition is defined as the point at which the Reynolds stresses in the boundary layer
have become negligible and do not effect the flow as described in Narasimha and Sreenivasan (1973) and Narasimha and
Sreenivasan (1979). Different values of Kcrit have been proposed for different types of boundary layer flows ranging from
2e − 6 < Kcrit < 3.6e − 6 with 3e − 6 emerging as the widely agreed upon threshold value. This range in Kcrit however shows
that K ≥ Kcrit is not a complete description of onset. Further this K > Kcrit model also ignores the size of the perturbation
experienced by the boundary layer and multimodal interactions with other pathways which could enhances or impede film
cooling induced transition. The length required for a boundary layer to fully achieve this state is dependent on the initial
level of disturbance within the boundary layer, the onset location, and the profile of the acceleration and no current model
exists to predict this without simulation.

RESEARCH AIMS
This paper aims to capture the behaviour of the HPT SS boundary layer when acted upon by discrete perturbations introduced by film cooling under various idealised film-cooling configurations represented by hemispherical trips of different
sizes. The research aims are:
1. Capture the variety of behaviours introduced by film cooling both transition seeded by turbulent structures introduced
by film ejection and their absence resulting in a separation bubble
2. Understand the effect of typical HPT sustained, post coolant ejection, acceleration on the tke and its length scale.
3. Show that local value of K is not the only governing parameter in the bifurcated behaviour but that the size and spacing
of the trip are also key parameters.
3 METHODS
3.1 Computational Setup
The present study uses 3DNS presented in Wheeler (2023), Wheeler et al. (2016) to run Direct Numerical Simulation
(DNS) over a flat wall to model disturbances introduced by film cooling ejection under an idealised HPT blade loading.
The flat wall approximation is minor, as suction-side curvature is concentrated near the leading edge and decreases substantially downstream of peak suction, where the trips and region of interest in the present study are located. As convex
curvature is known to stabilise boundary layer turbulence via centrifugal force Ranjan et al. (2017), any residual curvature
in this region would act in the same stabilising sense, making the present flat-plate results conservative relative to the true
curved geometry. The solver uses a 4th order differencing scheme, following the method of Tam and Webb (1993), with
a 7 point stencil. Skew split differencing is applied to inviscid fluxes using the method of Kennedy and Gruber (2008)
with characteristic interfaces applied at inlet and outlet from Kim and Lee (2000) and Kim and Lee (2003). A four step
Runge-Kutta loop is used to perform time marching. The region of interest of the simulations were restricted to the suction
surface with an idealised loading profile imposed on the domain through a freestream boundary condition following the
method of Jagger et al. (2025). The full domain is shown in Figure 2 with the flow direction from left to right. At the inlet
free stream turbulence length-scale is specified and a Blasius boundary layer profile with a chosen Reθ ,in is imposed at the
wall. The flow is allowed to develop undisturbed though the inlet buffer region after which the boundary layer is accelerated
following an idealised blade Mach distribution. The freestream boundary condition imposes the static pressure and velocity
through a buffer layer to replicate the acceleration and diffusion experienced in a HPT blade. The domain is extruded in
the span-wise direction (into the page) but is periodic in span to replicate cascade conditions while still allowing for 3D
spanwise instabilities to develop. The boundary layer is perturbed by trips in the region between the leading edge and peak
suction to replicate the perturbation of film cooling.
Periodic repeating hemispherical trips were used to simulate the aerodynamic impact of coolant ejection on the incoming
laminar boundary layer. Trips were chosen over direct film cooling configurations as film cooling has a wide parameter
space so no single configuration would be fully representative. Hemispherical trips allow for an easily parametrisable and
reproducible way to replicate the aerodynamic impact of the ejecting flow on the boundary layer capturing similar flow
physics. The trip radii, rtrip , were defined as fractions of the inlet boundary layer thickness, δ99,in , spanning a range of
perturbation sizes. The trips were space d 4 radii apart as shown in Figure 3. The total number of trips used across the
domain varied between 12 trips for the largest trips and 24 for the smallest and were implemented using an immersed
boundary method by enforcing zero momentum on mesh points within the trip in all directions.The inlet boundary layer
momentum thickness was set to Reθ ,in ∼ 100 to replicate conditions found in a HPT blade at Rex ∼ 250, 000 matching the
Rex regime where reverse transition is expected to occur reliably. This is nominally equivalent to a Reynolds number based

4.1 Energetics of Relaminarizing Flow
Within the high-pressure turbine there exists competing transition mechanisms through which turbulent transition is
triggered. Bypass transition is seeded at the film cooling injection site, leading to an attached fully developed turbulent
boundary layer in the diffusing region. If there is sufficient flow acceleration transition is delayed and occurs via laminar

turbulence.

The overall effect of the various sizes of trips placed at different streamwise locations can be summarised probabilistically
through calculating the occurrence of streaks in the space-time diagram from convecting turbulent spots and the separation
bubble. This is done through defining intermittences for both parameters (γstreak , γbubble ) the calculations of which are shown
in shown in Equation 3 and Equation 4. The streak intermittency is computed as the fraction of time the span averaged surface tke exceeds the threshold of tke∗ . This is calculated using the method described in Otsu (1979) to the pooled data
from all cases, which maximises the inter-class variance to separate the laminar background from intermittent streak events.
Bubble intermittency was computed similarly using a threshold of C∗f = 1e − 3, as separated regions are characterised by
very low friction coefficients. The standard C f < 0 criterion was not met in the mean flow because it occurs instantaneously,
these were transient events that did not appear as negative values once averaged over the span.
The results for these calculation are shown on Figure 16 separated by the various sizes of trip. The black line in all graphs
in Figure 16a and Equation 4 shows the behaviour of the streaks and bubble un-tripped case. The left most graphs shows
the trip of size 0.25δ99,in which due to the shear sheltering effect fails to cause bypass transition. As the trips are so small
there is little difference in γstreak when compared to the no trip case. γstreak starts at ∼ 0.8 decaying to near zero through
the accelerating region. This lack of streaks at peak suction means no spots are being seeded leading to γbubble ∼ 1 for all
locations of trip. The next image to the right shows the trip of size 0.29δ99,in these are of a sufficient size, hence initially
at the location of the trips at x̂ = 0.2, 0.3, 0.45, there is an elevated level of γstreak which rapidly decays for all locations.
There is little difference from the no trip case by peak suction for the case with Ktrip > Kcrit (blue) and only slightly elevated γstreak ∼ 0.1 for Ktrip ≤ Kcrit (red, green) when compared to the no trip case. The presence of these turbulent streaks
destabilises the bubble but does not eliminate it completely at any location. The destabilisation is in order of Ktrip with the
blue (Ktrip = 4.46e-6) most stable with γbubble ∼ 0.90 and the green (Ktrip = 2.30e − 6) the most unstable with γbubble ∼ 0.70.
The trip of size 0.33δ99,in is the identified optimal spacing and is seen to eliminate the bubble at all locations. For this
case γstreak remains elevated at for all streamwise locations downstream of the trip eventually ending at γstreak > 0.8 for all
cases. This strong presence of streaks seeds very strong and frequent turbulent spot generation which eliminates the bubble
as seen by the very low γbubble ∼ 0 in the corresponding graph. Finally, the right most graph is showing trips of size 0.5δ99,in
which experiences reverse transition behaviour. Trips where Ktrip ≥ Kcrit (blue,red) have an initial γstreak ∼ 1 but due to
strong acceleration it rapidly decays to a lower level downstream of the trip resulting in γstreak = 0.09, 0.17 respectively at
peak suction. A similar patter is seen for the case where Ktrip < Kcrit (green) but the decay is arrested at a much higher
level resulting in γstreak = 0.38. This difference in γstreak between the cases later causes the difference observed in γbubble
with Ktrip ≥ Kcrit cases not being able to seed many turbulent spots having a peak γbubble ∼ 0.8. Conversely for the case
with Ktrip < Kcrit more frequent spots are present and these coalesce with each other closing the separation bubble, having
a peak γbubble ∼ 0.18.

CONCLUSIONS
In this research DNS over a flat wall with an imposed loading distribution was conducted to simulate a HPT SS
boundary layer . The impact of coolant ejection on the boundary layer was modelled using discrete span-wise repeating
hemispherical trips with free-stream turbulence and inlet laminar boundary layer properties matching a blade Reynolds
number of 280, 000. The trips were placed at three streamwise locations depending on the local value of K with two placed
at Ktrip = 4.46e − 6, 3.32e − 6 > Kcrit and one placed at Ktrip = 2.30e − 6 < Kcrit . Four different sizes of trip were also used
to simulate the varying impact on the boundary layer of different film cooling arrangements. The the key outcomes of the
research are listed below:
1. The variety of different transitional behaviours of the suction surface boundary layer within a cooled high pressure
turbine were captured
2. Acceleration suppresses the absolute amount of tke if K > Kcrit beyond which tke grows for all trip locations. tke
relative to boundary layer edge velocity shrinks for cases with Ktrip > Kcrit as production cannot keep pace with the
mean flow.
3. The differences in the tke at peak suction come mainly from v′ and w′ which acts as a better indicator for the effect
of acceleration.
4. The main differentiator between cases where trips were placed at Ktrip > Kcrit or Ktrip < Kcrit was the predominant
length-scales contained in the turbulence.
5. Different length-scales of turbulence decay at different rates, with earlier trips losing small length-scale content more
rapidly. The magnitude and structure of tke remaining at peak suction governs the latter transitional behaviour.
6. The frequency and coalescence of turbulent spots in the diffusing region determines whether separation or bypass
transition occurs. Large trips show reverse transitional behaviour depending on Ktrip versus Kcrit , while small trips
are suppressed.
13

7. Trips optimally spaced to match a critical spanwise wavenumber excite maximum non-modal transient growth, driving bypass transition independent of Ktrip . This mechanism is highly spanwise-sensitive: a ∼ 12% spacing reduction
reverts behaviour to that of completely sheltered trips.
