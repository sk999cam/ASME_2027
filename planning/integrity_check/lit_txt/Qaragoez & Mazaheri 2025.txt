ABSTRACT
This study presents an integration of machine learning (ML) into Reynolds-Averaged Navier–Stokes (RANS)
simulations to enhance thermal and hydrodynamic predictions in film cooling applications. Recognizing the
limitations of traditional turbulence models — particularly their inability to accurately model Reynolds stresses
and turbulent heat flux — this work introduces a data-driven correction framework that improves both velocity
and temperature fields. A critical aspect of the study is the a posteriori estimation of turbulent viscosity and
turbulent Prandtl number, two key parameters influencing thermal and flow accuracy. These quantities are
predicted using neural networks trained on high-fidelity Large Eddy Simulation (LES) data. From a dataset of
2.6 million entries, correlation analysis was conducted to identify six of the most influential features, enabling
a compact and interpretable model. The trained model was integrated into the RANS solver via user-defined
functions (UDFs), allowing for in-solver correction of turbulence parameters. The ML model, trained solely on a
flat plate film cooling case with blowing ratio (BR) = 1, was applied without retraining to four additional cases,
including a leading-edge geometry and a turbine blade with suction-side cooling. The framework showed strong
generalization and stability, achieving up to 80% improvement in flat plate cooling cases and approximately
62% improvement for the gas turbine leading-edge case. This dual-field, a posteriori correction approach offers
a practical and computationally efficient enhancement to RANS modeling, advancing predictive capabilities
for complex industrial cooling flows.

1. Introduction
Due to global warming and efforts to reduce CO2 emissions, there
is a need to increase the inlet temperature of gas turbines to enhance
efficiency, as well as to use hydrogen — a fuel with a high adiabatic
flame temperature — as a fuel source. Given the limitations in the
manufacturing and materials of turbine blades, the use of cooling
techniques for turbine blades is essential. Among these, film cooling
is one of the most important and effective methods. Therefore, the
accurate simulation of film cooling becomes crucial [1,2].
On the other hand, in today’s fast-paced world, the ability to simulate and design with minimal computational costs, high accuracy, and
within a short timeframe is considered a competitive advantage. While
the Reynolds-Averaged Navier–Stokes (RANS) method often provides
satisfactory results for simple and industrial flows at a reasonable
cost [3], it faces challenges when dealing with flows containing large
eddies and anisotropic vortex structures, often leading to inaccurate
results. In contrast, high-fidelity methods such as Large Eddy Simulation (LES) and Direct Numerical Simulation (DNS) perform better
due to their ability to resolve smaller vortex structures and reduce

uncertainties in turbulence modeling. However, these methods come
with significantly higher computational costs, often ranging from 10 to
100 times greater than RANS, presenting computational challenges [4,
5]. The complex nature of applied and industrial turbulent flows,
characterized by a wide range of time and length scales and high
Reynolds numbers, renders DNS impractical. Consequently, LES and
RANS emerge as the primary approaches for simulating such flows.
Recognizing the high computational cost of LES and the lower accuracy
of RANS, there is a growing interest in enhancing RANS models by
leveraging insights from LES and employing machine learning techniques. This integration aims to achieve higher accuracy in RANS
predictions while maintaining computational efficiency, thus offering
a promising avenue for improving turbulence modeling in practical
engineering applications. Considering the above, there is a critical
need to enhance the accuracy of the low-cost RANS methods using
high-fidelity data.

stem from turbulence modeling, specifically the modeling of Reynolds
stresses and turbulent heat flux [6–8]. An effective and affordable

The inaccuracy of RANS in hydrodynamic predictions often stems
from Reynolds stress modeling. The following section discusses studies
that have addressed this issue.
Tracey et al. (2015) introduced a data-driven framework for turbulence modeling, investigating whether RANS solutions could be reconstructed without prior knowledge of model structures. While promising,
their approach faced challenges in generalization, complexity, and
computational costs [17].
Deep learning techniques have also been explored to improve turbulence modeling. Ling et al. (2016) developed a Tensor Basis Neural
Network (TBNN) to predict Reynolds stress anisotropy while embedding Galilean invariance, ensuring observer-independent predictions.
Their model, trained on LES data, outperformed traditional RANS
models in predicting anisotropy and velocity fields in canonical flows
but required further validation in complex turbulent systems [18].
More recently, Ellis and Xia (2023) applied TBNN to Reynolds
stress closure in two-equation RANS models, improving predictions
of anisotropic states and adiabatic cooling effectiveness. While GDH
and HOGGDH models enhanced cooling effectiveness distributions,
challenges in predicting lateral coolant spread persisted, indicating the
need for further refinements in turbulent heat flux modeling [1].
Beyond turbulence modeling, numerous studies have investigated
film cooling flows using experimental and numerical approaches. Below, we review some key contributions.
Experimental and numerical studies have played a critical role in
understanding film cooling effectiveness. Pietrzyk et al. (1990) [19]
and Sinha et al. (1991) [20] examined the effects of density and
momentum flux ratios on lateral cooling effectiveness. They found that
lower density ratios and higher momentum flux ratios reduced lateral
coolant spreading, significantly impacting cooling performance. Subsequent LES studies, including Leedom and Acharya (2008), validated
these experimental findings and emphasized the influence of plenum
conditions on cooling effectiveness [21].
Cruse et al. (1997) extended film cooling research to turbine leading
edges, showing that high free-stream turbulence reduced effectiveness
at low blowing ratios but had minimal impact at higher ratios [22].
LES studies, such as those by Kalghatgi and Acharya (2014) [23] and
Fan et al. (2019) [24], further confirmed the significant influence of
coherent structures — such as counter-rotating vortex pairs (CVP) and
hairpin vortices — on cooling effectiveness.

trenched film cooling, demonstrating improved effectiveness compared

One of the key parameters influencing film cooling effectiveness is
the turbulent Prandtl number (𝑃 𝑟𝑡 ), which governs heat transport in
turbulent flows. Accurate modeling of 𝑃 𝑟𝑡 is essential for predicting
temperature distributions in film cooling, yet conventional approaches
often assume a constant value, leading to inaccuracies in complex flow
conditions. Ling et al. (2015) evaluated three algebraic closures for
turbulent scalar fluxes in film cooling using LES data. Their study
identified a breakdown in the Reynolds analogy, emphasizing the need
for lower turbulent Prandtl numbers. Among the tested models, the
Generalized Gradient Diffusion Hypothesis (GGDH) and High-Order
Generalized Gradient Diffusion Hypothesis (HOGGDH) provided better
scalar flux anisotropy predictions at the jet’s top but struggled near
the bottom wall. They also found that tuning coefficients in RANS
models had a more significant impact on improving predictions than
transitioning to higher-order models [9].
Building on this, Ling et al. (2016) highlighted the failure of RANS
models to capture Reynolds stress anisotropy, particularly in jet-incrossflow and film cooling applications. To address this, they proposed machine learning-based turbulence models trained on diverse
flow datasets, demonstrating substantial improvements over traditional
RANS predictions. Future research aims to integrate these models into
RANS simulations to improve convergence and better predict wall
stresses and heat fluxes [10].

While machine learning has shown promise, two limitations persist:
(1) many models are trained and validated only on simplified flat-plate
film cooling configurations, limiting generalization; and (2) existing ML
models often rely on large and complex input feature spaces (17–19+
variables), reducing interpretability and increasing computational burden [1,14,15]. Our study addresses these limitations by training a
compact, physically interpretable ML model on one flat-plate case (BR
= 1.0), and testing it across four unseen configurations — including a
leading-edge film cooling case and C3X turbine blade geometry — without retraining. This study leverages LES to compute flow-dependent
𝜈𝑡 and 𝑃 𝑟𝑡 , and uses correlation analysis to identify the most relevant
features. After training a compact neural network model using six input
features, we integrate it into the 𝑘 − 𝜔 SST model via user-defined
functions, enabling a posteriori dual-field correction inside the RANS
solver. The novelty of this study lies in the a posteriori integration of the
ML model into the CFD solver, enabling simultaneous correction of both
turbulent viscosity and turbulent Prandtl number. Additionally, the
model demonstrates strong generalization across diverse geometries —
including leading-edge and turbine blade cooling — without retraining,
highlighting its practical value for industrial applications. The novel
contributions of this study are summarized as follows: (i) performing
simultaneous a posteriori correction of both thermal and velocity fields,
(ii) improving generalization across geometries, (iii) enhancing RANS
performance with a compact and computationally efficient ML model,
(iv) testing the model on cases with realistic operating conditions, such
as leading-edge and turbine blade film cooling, in contrast to most
previous studies that relied on idealized flat-plate configurations with
unity blowing and density ratios. These contributions demonstrate a
practically viable path toward enhancing the predictive accuracy of
RANS models in real-world, high-Reynolds-number cooling applications. The remainder of the paper is structured as follows: Section 2
outlines the numerical setup and governing equations. Section 3 details
data preprocessing and ML model development. Section 4 presents
the validation and performance of the integrated model across cases.
Section 5 concludes with key findings, implications, and future research
directions.

2.1. Governing equations and numerical method
The accuracy of turbulence simulations relies heavily on robust
numerical methodologies and properly defined governing equations.
This section presents the governing equations, numerical methods, and
solution strategies used in this study, including the LES and RANS
methodologies, subgrid-scale modeling, and grid independence studies.
2.1.1. LES method
The LES simulations were performed using ANSYS Fluent 2024 R1
with its GPU-based solver, which employs the finite volume method
and executes on graphical processors. Given that the simulated flows
have Mach numbers less than 0.3 and are considered incompressible
from a hydrodynamic perspective, a pressure-based solver was used
to couple the continuity and momentum equations. Since film cooling
involves significant temperature variations, leading to density changes
in the cooling layer, the energy equation was included in the numerical formulation. The variable density in the discretized domain was
computed using the ideal gas law, ensuring a consistent representation
of temperature-dependent density effects. The governing equations for

Flat plate with film cooling configuration
The studied configuration for single-row film cooling consists of a

3.2. Feature selection
The performance and generalizability of a machine learning model
strongly depend on feature selection. A large number of input features
can increase model complexity, lead to overfitting, and significantly
raise computational costs. By selecting only the most relevant features,
we ensure a simpler yet accurate neural network model, improving both
prediction accuracy and computational efficiency.
The concept of correlation, which measures the relationship between input features and the target variable, is used to identify and
select the most relevant features for model training. This approach
focuses on features that have a greater impact on predictions, reducing redundancy and overfitting while improving model performance,
interpretability, and efficiency.
In this study, 30 candidate features were extracted from the LES
flow field for use in machine learning model training. Feature selection
was based on correlation analysis and physical relevance. The complete
list of extracted features is provided in the Supplementary Material
(Section S2).

of traditional turbulence models, which assume constant transport
parameters and lack generalization across flow geometries.
A dataset consisting of 2.6 million samples with 30 features and two
target variables was derived from the LES data. Correlation analysis
identified the most relevant input features, optimizing accuracy while
maintaining computational efficiency. Neural network models were developed with hyperparameter tuning, using a compact neural network
architecture optimized for accuracy and efficiency.
The trained models were integrated into the RANS method and
tested across five different cases. The enhanced RANS model demonstrated substantial improvements over baseline simulations, particularly in predicting turbulent heat fluxes, temperature fields, and overall
hydrodynamic behavior. The improvements were most pronounced in
the three flat plate cooling cases, with enhancements of 80%, 67%, and
51%, respectively, while for the gas turbine leading edge case, the improvement was approximately 60%. Comparative analysis with existing
data-driven models further demonstrated the superior performance of
the present approach. Despite these advancements, challenges remain
in achieving broader generalization. The performance of the models
declined for cases that significantly deviated from the training dataset,
as observed in the turbine blade cooling case (C3X), which showed
the largest deviation from the training data. Expanding the dataset to
include a wider range of flow conditions is necessary to enhance model
robustness.
Key achievements of this study include the development of machine
learning models to predict the turbulent Prandtl number and turbulent viscosity, significantly improving RANS accuracy for film cooling
simulations. The models were successfully integrated into the 𝑘 − 𝜔
SST turbulence model, demonstrating their effectiveness in real-world
applications such as turbine blade cooling. The study also achieved
a balance between model complexity and computational efficiency,
ensuring that high accuracy was maintained while keeping resource
demands reasonable.
For future research, the generalization of the developed models
should be improved by expanding the training dataset to encompass
a more diverse range of flow conditions. Instead of relying on turbulent Prandtl number estimation, direct heat flux estimation using
data-driven models should be explored. Additionally, machine learning
models can be extended to predict all six Reynolds stress components,
replacing the need for turbulent viscosity models. The use of PhysicsInformed Neural Networks (PINNs) and Tensor-Basis Neural Networks
(TBNNs) could enhance turbulence modeling accuracy. Dimensionality
reduction techniques such as Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and
reduced-order models like Proper Orthogonal Decomposition (POD)
could further optimize computational efficiency. Future work may also
involve classifying and segmenting different flow regimes to develop
specialized models for distinct categories.
Overall, this study demonstrates the potential of integrating machine learning with RANS models to significantly enhance turbulence
modeling and film cooling predictions. The findings highlight the advantages of leveraging high-fidelity data, advanced neural networks,
and GPU computing to develop more accurate and efficient turbulence
models. Further advancements in data-driven turbulence modeling will
continue to refine predictive capabilities and expand the applicability
of these approaches to complex engineering challenges. This integrated approach paves the way for developing reliable, generalizable,
and interpretable ML-enhanced turbulence models for industrial-scale
applications.

5. Conclusion
This study investigated the integration of data-driven and machine
learning models with RANS simulations, focusing on film cooling applications. The primary goal was to enhance the predictive capability of
the 𝑘−𝜔 SST turbulence model by estimating turbulent Prandtl number
and turbulent viscosity. These estimates were achieved using machine
learning models trained on high-fidelity data obtained from Large Eddy
Simulations.
A key novelty of this study lies in the a posteriori correction of
both turbulent viscosity and turbulent Prandtl number using a compact,
interpretable neural network trained on LES data and integrated into
the RANS solver.
Five LES cases were conducted to generate high-fidelity data for
training (one case) and validation purposes, including four flat plate
film cooling simulations and one turbine blade cooling case (C3X).
