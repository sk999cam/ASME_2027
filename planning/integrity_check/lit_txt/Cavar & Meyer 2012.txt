The paper presents results of a Proper Orthogonal Decomposition (POD) investigation of the LES based
numerical simulation of the jet-in-crossﬂow (JICF) ﬂowﬁeld with Reynolds number based on the cross
ﬂow velocity and jet diameter Re = 2400 and the velocity ratio of R = 3.3. LES results are validated against
pointwise time averaged Laser Doppler Anemometry (LDA) measurements in PART1 of this study. In
PART2 of the presented study – a planar (2D) LES based snapshot POD analysis is ﬁrst conducted on
two mutually perpendicular planes located in the jet-to-crossﬂow entrance zone. The obtained results
are directly compared and found to be in close agreement with results of a Particle Image Velocimetry
(PIV) based planar (2D) snapshot POD analysis by Meyer et al. (JFM 583, p. 199–227, 2007), indicating
that LES is able to predict the same large scale ﬂow dynamics as that captured by PIV. Some differences
are also observed, but appear to be directly connected to the differences in levels of the resolved turbulent kinetic energy (TKE) between LES and PIV datasets. Those differences proved to be linked to the process of ﬁltering out the small-scale ﬂuctuations implicit to the PIV measurement technique. Comparisons
of TKE captured by the ﬁrst POD modes showed that they are not affected by this implicit ﬁltering.
The LES based POD analysis was also conducted in 3D. The 3D POD analysis results based on the ﬁrst
two POD modes show a full ability to directly visualize details of the relationship between the counterrotating vortex pair (CVP), the hanging vortex and the wake vortices. POD reconstruction shows that the
CVP originates from the hanging vortex formed at the lateral sides of the jet. It also shows that the shedding process involving oscillation of the jet core is responsible for the creation of wake vortices and that
the wake vortex originates from the hanging vortex, but grows quickly by ‘‘sucking up’’ the wall boundary
layer ﬂuid and vorticity.

1. Introduction
The Jet in Cross Flow (JICF) represents a basic ﬂow conﬁguration
that was investigated quite extensively in the past, primarily due
to its relevance to numerous engineering applications. The evolution of vortical ﬂow in and around JICF has been subjected to
several experimental and numerical investigations aimed towards
better understanding of the complex interaction mechanism between the jet and the cross-stream. Most experimental studies of
the ﬂow dynamics are visualization studies. Good examples are
(Fric and Roshko, 1994; Kelso et al., 1996), who used laminar
oncoming ﬂows (at least on the cross-stream side) making it possible to directly trace the vorticity deformation and creation of vortical structures throughout the ﬂow ﬁeld. However, in most
practical applications, where JICF occurs, both inﬂows are turbulent. The inherent unsteadiness of turbulent ﬂow makes ﬂow
patterns highly irregular and makes their visualization much more

(snapshots) in practice never show identical patterns. The coherent
ﬂow structures are randomly perturbed by the turbulence. The
direct comparison and interpretation of snapshots from different
ﬂow realizations is therefore not usually feasible.
Proper Orthogonal Decomposition (POD) can be used to perform statistical analysis of ﬂow snapshots (see e.g. Lumley, 1967;
Sirovich, 1987). POD is today an established technique and many
investigators have successfully used POD to analyze complex ﬂow
ﬁelds. Podvin et al. (2006) used POD to investigate cavity ﬂows and
were able to relate the ﬁrst couple of most the energetic and dominant POD modes to cavity ﬂow shear layer instabilities. Meyer
et al. (2007) used PIV based POD to analyze ﬂow structures in
the JICF ﬂow conﬁguration. Moin and Moser (1989) used DNS
based POD analysis to study fully developed channel ﬂow, Kostas
et al. (2005) used POD to analyze both the instantaneous velocity
and vorticity ﬁelds of a backward-facing step, Orellano and Wengle
(2001) combined POD and LES to analyze the forced turbulent

transitional, spatially evolving boundary layer was performed by

Flows calculated with LES are usually compared to experiments
using time-averaged local variables such as mean velocities and
Reynolds stresses. For the present ﬂow case, we have presented
this type of comparison in PART1. Good agreement with measurements using Laser Doppler Anemometry (LDA) has been found
with the exception of a region on the side of the jet that faces
the crossﬂow. It is suggested in PART1 that this may be caused
by measurement errors. Therefore, one of the objectives of the
present study is to investigate how POD can be used as a statistical
tool to compare experimental data to the results calculated with
LES.
POD is based on energy considerations. If a ﬂow has large, energetic ﬂow structures that systematically appear they will be captured in the ﬁrst POD modes. POD can therefore be used to
identify dominant ﬂow structures. The dynamics of the structures
can be tracked if time-resolved data are available.
Particle Image Velocimetry (PIV) adds a new dimension to
experimental data by offering snapshots of a ﬂow. When compared
to LES, PIV data still have two important shortcomings: measurements are usually not time-resolved and only capture data in a
plane of the ﬂow. POD analysis does not require time-resolved data
and is therefore well-suited as a statistical tool for PIV data analysis. The fact that data only comes from a plane that cuts through
the ﬂow is more questionable. To represent three-dimensional
ﬂow structures, POD analysis should formally be based on threedimensional velocity ﬁelds. However, the experimental investigation presented in Meyer et al. (2007) suggests that POD analysis
of PIV data taken at different orthogonal planes that cut through
central parts of the JICF do represent the same ﬂow structures.
Therefore, another objective of the present study is to test this suggestion by comparing POD on LES data done both in planes similar
to PIV data and on a full three-dimensional dataset.
Finally, the three-dimensional POD analysis will be used to analyze details regarding the creation of the counter-rotation vortex
pair (CVP). The CVP is the largest ﬂow structure in the jet in cross
ﬂow, but the origin of the CVP is still debated in literature.
As mentioned, PART1 of this study compares LES results to
pointwise LDA measurements and establishes a ﬁrm and clear link
between computations and measurements on the considered jetin-cross-ﬂow set-up. This PART2 of the presented study describes
ﬁndings of the conducted POD analysis on available PIV (Meyer
et al., 2007) and LES datasets. For information regarding experimental and computational set-up, a review of previous experimental and numerical studies related to jet-in-cross-ﬂow, etc., the
reader is referred to PART1. It should be noted that all LES based
2D POD results presented in the following are based on case LES
6 (best numerical counterpart to LDA measurements) and all LES
based 3D POD analyses are based on case LES 7 (longest overall
computation (in time units) conducted during this investigation)
as described in PART1.
2. Proper Orthogonal Decomposition (POD) Basics
An objective and unbiased method, that can be used to identify
ﬂow structures in a given ﬂow, is Proper Orthogonal Decomposition – POD. In POD a series of ﬂow realizations is decomposed,
using some mathematical tools, into a set of orthonormal basis
functions, that span the given dataset in the most ‘‘optimal’’ way.
The deﬁnition of optimality in POD is based on energy considerations, as POD eigenvalues, which represent the relative contribution of a given POD mode to the overall resolved kinetic energy, can
be arranged in descending order, so the most energetic, i.e. largest
ﬂow structures are captured in the ﬁrst couple of POD modes.

Therefore in cases, where the ﬂow dynamics are dominated by
some distinct coherent structures, it should be possible to capture
them by considering a relatively small number of POD modes.

(1967). In the context of this work the method known as ‘‘snapshot
POD’’ of Sirovich (1987) will be used. In the following we present a
brief description of basic equations, that constitute a mathematical
foundation of the applied method. For a detailed description of the
general POD methodology, an interested reader may refer to the
book by Holmes et al. (1998).

3. Comparison of PIV-based and LES-based planar (2D) POD
analyses
Results presented in PART1 of the current LES study show that
despite substantial numerical efforts, a clear disagreement between results from LDA measurements and ﬁndings of all considered LES cases was observed in the immediate jet-to-crossﬂow
exhaustion zone. Therefore, it is interesting to consider a direct
POD based comparison between ﬂow dynamics revealed by available PIV measurements and LES, as it can serve as an additional
important validation step that indicates how well the LES is able
to represent not only the statistical properties of the measured JICF
ﬂow case, but also the dynamics of real ﬂow behavior. We ﬁnd it
additionally very important to establish a clear indication that

37

POD results based on PIV (previously published in Meyer et al.,
2007) and current LES based POD ﬁndings do in reality represent
the same ﬂow physics, since our intention is to utilize ﬁndings
from that study and bring it a considerable step further by introducing 3D POD analysis, which is presented and discussed in Section 5.
As mentioned in the introduction, PIV measurements provide
only snapshot based data in a plane. This does not guarantee that
the most dominant ﬂow structures are captured. However, ﬁndings
reported by Meyer et al. (2007) suggest that results from different
planes do agree and that they represent the same ﬂow phenomena.
Nevertheless, it is not unlikely that a POD analysis on the full
three-dimensional ﬂow ﬁeld can lead to a substantially different
outcome. Therefore, the ﬁrst POD analysis of the LES data is conducted using data that has been resampled to the same planar grid,
as is used for PIV. Since LES data has a signiﬁcantly higher spatial
resolution than the PIV data, this resampling can be done quite
accurately (a second order inverse distance interpolation technique was used). Doing the POD analysis in the exact same points
(same plane) means that deviations in results cannot be caused by
the selection of data points.
The POD analysis of PIV data is based on 1000 snapshots, which
were obtained with sufﬁcient time between samples to ensure statistical independence. To test statistical convergence, the POD
analysis was ﬁrst conducted with signiﬁcantly fewer samples.
Using, e.g. 200 samples showed the same qualitative results for
the ﬁrst app. 6 POD modes, but small irregularities and asymmetries were seen when compared to the results using 1000 samples.
For increasing POD mode number, large difference were seen. A
dataset comprising 1000 samples seemed to indicate that reasonable statistical convergence levels were reached and is therefore
chosen as the basis for the presented analysis. The same number
of independent samples in the LES case could not be provided for
the obvious reasons of extreme computational overhead connected
with it.
The current LES based POD study is conducted based on the results of case LES 6 from PART 1 of this study and datasets extracted
on two investigated planes during the overall time period that correspons to 30 Flow-Throught-Times (FLTs).1
The turbulence time scale is increasing with the downstream
distance in the JICF ﬂow. Assuming that the average integral time
scale in the near-ﬁeld ﬂow is 3D/U1 (obtained from autocorrelation function Ruu at point x/D = 2, y/D = 0 z/D = 3 – Fig. 8 in PART
1 of this study), the present LES dataset will only contain app. 90
statistically uncorrelated samples. The initial analysis was conducted on these 90 uncorrelated ﬂow realizations showing qualitatively similar results to those based on 1000 samples. The main
difference was that some structures were signiﬁcantly ‘‘blurred’’
and were not easily identiﬁable in the small dataset analysis.
Therefore, despite the fact that the LES dataset contained statistically correlated data, we decided to conduct the main LES analysis
on the full dataset in order to accommodate a comparison of PIV
and LES data up to a similar level of detail.
The POD analyses are compared on two mutually perpendicular
planes in the jet near-ﬁeld – y/D = 0 the symmetry plane and the x/
D = 1 plane.

Vortex-identiﬁcation methods such as Q-criterion (Hunt et al.,
1988) have long been used as vortex-identiﬁcation tools. In
Fig. 11c and d results of an application of the Q-criterion on the snapshot from Fig. 11a and its reconstruction from Fig. 11b are presented.
As seen from Fig. 11c, various vortices on the upstream side of the jet,
actually not directly seen in the snapshot itself (traces of shear-layer
vortices) as well as vortices in the wake region can be identiﬁed.
However, the most interesting part here is represented by three
vortices seen on both Fig. 11c and d positioned at (x/D, y/
D) = (0.45, 0.5), (x/D, y/D) = (0.4, 0.5) and (x/D, y/D) = (0.65, 0.2).
As hypothesized by Meyer et al. (2007), the ﬁrst two vortices should
represent traces of the standing (hanging) vortices of Yuan et al.
(1999), while the last one should represent traces of a wake vortex.
From Fig. 10 it is seen that the time variation of the A1 and A2
coefﬁcients closely follow a phase shifted pattern of sine and cosine functions. Small variations in the amplitude and period of both
coefﬁcients seen in Fig. 10 can probably be attributed to some random ﬂow variations. In order to diminish the effect of random variations on the present analysis, coefﬁcients are in the following part
assumed to follow the idealized sine and cosine patterns indicated
in Fig. 10.
In order to better analyze the vortical structures seen in Fig. 11c
and d the present investigation is continued in 3D – Fig. 12. Subﬁgures of Fig. 12 illustrate iso-surfaces, colored by xz, having the
same positive Q value, where the Q-criterion has been applied on
ﬂow reconstructions based on the ﬁrst two POD modes with idealized coefﬁcients (the modes are added to the mean ﬂow ﬁeld).
Moreover, the subﬁgures of Fig. 12 actually follow the development of structures depicted in Fig. 12a throughout approximately
one half of the periodical cycle of coefﬁcients A1 and A2 (180 snapshots – cf. Fig. 10).
Generally, in Fig. 12 three to four distinct vortical structures can
be identiﬁed. Two of them, which are positioned on the lateral
edges of the jet, are visible on all subﬁgures. They slightly change
their inclination angle with the z-axis (or jet trajectory) but always
appear at approximately the same domain position. According to
the deﬁnition of Yuan et al. (1999) they can be identiﬁed as hanging vortices.
By inspecting Fig. 12a in a more detailed manner two additional
structures, which are both attached to the corresponding hanging
vortex can be identiﬁed. The process of continuous and alternating
creation and destruction of these structures can be followed
throughout Fig. 12a–f. In Fig. 12a it is seen that one of the mentioned structures is attached to the left hanging vortex, while the
other is just about to be created in close proximity of the right
hanging vortex. Following the vortex dynamics further – Fig. 12b,
one can observe that the vortex connected to the left hanging vortex become detached from it, while the vortex connected to the
right hanging vortex has grown in size, while it is convected
downstream.

6. Conclusion

depicts a mirror image of Fig. 12a, indicating that a new analogous
process of creation and destruction of the attached/detached vortices from the hanging vortices takes place.
The process described above clearly elucidates the process of a
dynamical interaction of wake vortices and corresponding hanging
vortices in the JICF ﬂow. Furthermore, from Fig. 12a and f a clear
indication of the signiﬁcant role played by the hanging vortex in
the creation process of wake vortices is underlined. It is seen that
the wake vortex practically originates from the hanging vortex
and grows in size in a tornado vortex like manner by ‘‘sucking
up’’ the boundary layer ﬂuid and vorticity.
The spatial location at which they are identiﬁed, the size and
the fact that hanging vortices can clearly be visualized in all subﬁgures of Fig. 12 (representing app. one half of the periodical cycle of
A1 and A2 coefﬁcients from Fig. 10), indicate that they play a dominant role in the process of creation and evolvement of the CVP
vortical structure. The presented analysis thereby indicate that
the creation process of wake vortices is directly connected to,
controlled by and originates from the process of CVP creation in
the JICF ﬂow ﬁeld.

A clear link between present numerical LES study of the JICF
ﬂowﬁeld and LDA measurements on the corresponding experimental set-up with respect to the mean ﬂow statistics has been established in PART1 of this study. Further investigations aimed at
analyzing the ﬂow dynamics and understanding the complex ﬂow
patterns that are pertinent to the JICF ﬂow ﬁeld are conducted by
means of Proper Orthogonal Decomposition (POD) technique and
are presented in this PART2 of the current study.
The LES based POD analysis is directly compared to the corresponding POD analysis conducted based on measured PIV datasets,
previously reported in Meyer et al. (2007). The comparisons are
undertaken for two mutually perpendicular planes in the jetto-crossﬂow entrance zone (y/D = 0 and x/D = 1 planes), where
in- and out-of plane velocity components in POD modes 1–6 are
directly compared, showing a good quantitative agreement, underlining that LES predicts the same large scale ﬂow dynamics as is
captured by PIV. Some differences are also observed, but they appear to be directly connected to the differences in the levels of
the resolved turbulent kinetic energy in the LES computations
and PIV measurements of Meyer et al. (2007). Those differences
in the levels of the resolved turbulent kinetic energy seem however
to be directly linked to limited resolution capabilities and interrogation area averaging implicit to PIV measurement technique. The
well-known issue related to PIV measurements – the ﬁltering out
of small-scale ﬂuctuations can directly lead to underestimation of
the turbulent kinetic energy, seem to clearly be the case for the
measurements of Meyer et al. (2007). However, a comparison of
the kinetic energy captured by the ﬁrst POD modes shows that
the ﬁrst POD modes are not affected by this implicit ﬁltering.
The LES based POD analysis is extended to 3D. The results ﬁrst
show a clear connection between the dominant modes found in 2D
POD analyses, conﬁrming the ﬁndings of Meyer et al. (2007), who
claimed that structures revealed by the separately conducted 2D
POD analyses (in the y/D = 0 and x/D = 1 planes) do represent traces
of the same 3D coherent structures. The measurements by Meyer
et al. (2007) were not time-resolved, but the POD analysis suggested that a periodic shedding of wake vortices was the most
important dynamic mechanism involving also the core of the jet.
The 3D POD analysis of the LES results show that this is indeed
the case. The frequency f of the wake vortices shedding is found
to be f D/U1 = 0.09. This supports the idea put forward by Meyer
et al. (2007) that this type of periodic ﬂow structure can be indicated by observing patterns in the POD reconstruction coefﬁcients.
Dominant modes found from the POD analysis are added to the
mean ﬂow ﬁeld and are analyzed in the z/D = 0.55 plane in order to
isolate and directly visualize vortical structures in the wake region.
This POD based reconstruction of the JICF ﬂowﬁeld is subsequently
combined with the well-known Q-criterion vortex identiﬁcation
method and is fully extended to 3D. Studies of a 3D ﬂow reconstruction in time, based on the ﬁrst two POD modes, reveals details
of the relationship between the CVP, the hanging vortex and the
wake vortices. The reconstruction conﬁrms that the CVP originates
from the hanging vortex formed at the sides of the jet. Also, the
wake vortices are created in a shedding process that involves oscillation of the jet core. A wake vortex originates from the hanging
vortex, but grows quickly by ‘‘sucking up’’ the wall boundary layer
ﬂuid and vorticity. It should be noted that since the ﬂow is in a turbulent boundary layer, some ﬂow mechanisms may be different for
JICF in a laminar boundary layer.
