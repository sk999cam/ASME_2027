SIMULATION OF THE NEAR-FIELD REGION OF FILM COOLING JETS USING
RANS AND HYBRID URANS/LES MODELS
Hosein Foroutan
Savas Yavuzkurt
Department of Mechanical and Nuclear Engineering
The Pennsylvania State University
University Park, PA 16802

ABSTRACT
This paper investigates the flow field and heat transfer in
the near-field region of film cooling jets through numerical
simulations using RANS and hybrid URANS/LES models.
Detailed simulations of flow and thermal fields of a single row
of film cooling cylindrical holes with 30o inline injection on a
flat plate are obtained for low (M=0.5) and high (M=1.5)
blowing ratios under high free stream turbulence (10%). The
realizable k-ε model is used within the RANS framework and a
realizable k-ε-based detached eddy simulation (DES) is used as
a hybrid URANS/LES model. Both models are used together
with the two-layer zonal model for near-wall simulations.
Steady and time-averaged unsteady film cooling effectiveness
obtained using these models in ANSYS-FLUENT are compared
with available experimental data. It is shown that hybrid
URANS/LES models (DES in the present paper) predict more
mixing both in the wall-normal and spanwise directions
compared to RANS models, while unsteady asymmetric
vortical structures of the flow can also be captured. The
turbulent heat flux components predicted by the DES model are
higher than those obtained by the RANS simulations, resulting
in enhanced turbulent heat transfer between the jet and
mainstream, and consequently better predictions of the
effectiveness. Furthermore, the unsteady physics of jet and
crossflow interactions and the jet lift-off under high free stream
turbulence is studied using the present DES results.
INTRODUCTION
High turbine inlet temperatures are required to obtain high
efficiency in advanced gas turbines. Therefore, it is typical to
have turbine inlet temperatures as high as 1400oC [1]. However,
enhancing the thermal performance of gas turbines by
increasing the turbine inlet temperature may cause irreparable
damage to the blades. Therefore, a reasonable lifetime of
turbine blades can only be achieved by an efficient surface
cooling mechanism such as film cooling. In film cooling, a
secondary fluid (coolant) is injected through several holes on a

surface, which forms an air film with lower temperature
between the surface and hot mainstream, and protects the
surface from overheating. The full-coverage film cooling [2]
incorporates several rows of holes over the entire surface,
resulting in not only more uniform heat transfer rates on the
surface also protection of the whole surface which is desirable
[3]. This, however, gives rise to the importance of the study of
the near-field region in the film cooling since in the full
coverage film cooling, which is more widely used nowadays,
the whole surface is in the near-field of the cooling jets.
Making significant advances in the cooling technology
requires a fundamental understanding of the physical
mechanisms involved in film cooling flow fields. The low-cost
and availability of detailed set of data make computational fluid
dynamics (CFD) an important tool for film cooling
investigations. Most previous film cooling flow predictions
have been carried out employing the steady Reynolds-Averaged
Navier-Stokes (RANS) equations with two-equation turbulence
models [4-8]. Although the RANS simulations were generally
able to predict the gross flow and heat transfer behavior, they
fail to correctly describe the anisotropic turbulence associated
with the jet-crossflow interactions. Therefore, as one of their
known deficiencies, RANS simulations underpredict the lateral
spreading and mixing of the coolant jet [9]. Furthermore, steady
RANS simulations are unable to capture unsteady phenomena
associated with mixing of the jet and the crossflow in film
cooling. Experimental studies [10] show that the film cooling
flow field is highly unsteady and complex with secondary flows
and various types of vortical structures which dominate the
mixing process in the near-field region. Therefore, unsteady
simulations are necessary in order to properly capture
inherently unsteady features of the film cooling flow.
In the unsteady simulation framework, Large Eddy
Simulation (LES), which directly resolves the more influential
turbulence scales and models only the small (subgrid) scales,

has been widely used. Tyagi and Acharya [11] performed LES
of film cooling flow from inclined cylindrical jets. They were
able to identify various vortical structures, i.e., counter-rotating
vortex pair (CRVP), roller vortices, and upright wake vortices,
within the film cooling flow field and present them in terms of
basic hairpin coherent structures. The time-averaged results
obtained from their LES show good agreement with
experimental data, although a few discrepancies were seen at
stations farther downstream. Large Eddy Simulation of film
cooling flow was carried out by Peet and Lele [12] by including
the supply plenum in the computational domain. Unsteady flow
above the test surface, as well as inside the plenum and the film
hole was investigated. The laterally-averaged film cooling
effectiveness was slightly overpredicted, while good agreement
for the centerline effectiveness was obtained for the blowing
ratio of 0.5. Johnson and Kapat [13] used LES with dynamic
Smagorinsky subgrid scale model for simulation of film
cooling flow and heat transfer. Their results showed that LES
predicts the development and mixing of the jet well, except in
areas close to the injection hole and near the wall. The initial
mixing of the coolant and the crossflow was underpredicted by
30-50% right after the hole. Furthermore, the coolant coverage
was underpredicted near the wall even further downstream.
They argued that under-resolution of the near-wall and nearfield mixing layers or failure of the simplistic Smagorinsky
subgrid model result in underestimation of the mixing. In fact,
the near-wall difficulty is well known in the context of LES,
which is mainly due to the anisotropy of all scales (even the
small ones) near the wall (more details are given in the next
section).
An alternative solution is to apply hybrid URANS/LES
models. They are intended to combine the advantages of LES
and URANS models. However, comparing to LES, there are
considerably fewer studies attempting on investigating
unsteady features of the film cooling flow using hybrid
URANS/LES models. Detached Eddy Simulation (DES) is the
most commonly used hybrid URANS/LES model. The first
attempt on simulation of film cooling flow with DES was
reported by Roy et al. [14] who applied a Spalart-Allamaras
based DES [15] model to a film-cooled flat plate with blowing
ratio of unity. It was shown that DES greatly enhances the
realistic description of the dynamic anisotropic mixing
processes comparing to RANS simulations. However, DES
predictions of the laterally-averaged film cooling effectiveness
showed no more accuracy than that of the RANS model, which
were partly attributed to forcing a symmetry boundary
condition at the hole centerline inhibiting the growth of threedimensional asymmetric instabilities. Kim and Hassan [16]
performed unsteady simulations of flat plate film cooling using
URANS, LES, and DES models. They showed that results of
the URANS simulations become almost the same as those of
the steady RANS simulations, while no unsteady vortical
structure can be captured. LES and DES results, on the other
hand, showed the coherent vortical structures of the film
cooling flow field. Both LES and DES models, however,

underpredicted the time-averaged film cooling effectiveness in
the near-field region, and overpredicted it far downstream.
This paper investigates the flow field and heat transfer in
film cooling with a single row of cylindrical holes (30o inline
injection) on a flat plate applying RANS and hybrid
URANS/LES models. Some of the distinctive features of the
present simulations are:
1. Considering a test case with high free stream
turbulence, closer to actual conditions of gas turbines.
2. Applying time-dependent, perturbed inlet boundary
conditions in order to take the effects of inlet turbulent
fluctuations into account.
3. Considering both low and high blowing ratios.
HYBRID URANS/LES MODELING
In the LES approach, the large, energy carrying,
dynamically important, and flow-dependent eddies are solved
directly, leaving only smallest scale of turbulence with very low
energy and supposedly universal behavior (assumed isotropic)
to be modeled. The application of LES has been particularly
successful in non-equilibrium flows in free shear layers, and in
massively separated flows in which the accurate simulation of
regions near the wall is not of primary importance [17].
However, one of the major obstacles to the use of LES in
complex industrial flows, such as film cooling, is the modeling
of the near-wall region [18]. The dynamics of the flow near the
wall are strongly anisotropic even at small scales and
turbulence production in this region is associated with an upscale energy cascade that is largely dominant over the
commonly assumed down-scale energy cascade, presenting
elsewhere. Also, the large eddies that must be captured on the
computational grid to perform an accurate LES shrink in size
and are not isotropic as one approaches the wall, leading to
excessive computational costs [17]. In fact the small eddies are
not isotropic near a wall either. Current LES modeling
approaches require that either the near-wall region be
adequately resolved (using a DNS-like grid near the wall which
makes it inapplicable for industrial flows), or that an LES wallmodel be used, which to date has not provided accurate results
in relatively complex flows. Contrary to the LES, the weakness
in the RANS simulations is that the resolved large scale
unsteady motions are often damped out by the turbulence
model. The near-wall modeling, on the other hand, is often
quite accurate since all these models are developed and
calibrated for near-wall flows.
Hybrid URANS/LES methods aim at combining the best of
RANS and LES turbulence models. In this approach, the
unsteady RANS (URANS) models are employed in the
boundary layer, while LES treatment is applied to regions away
from the wall. The most common type of hybrid URANS/LES
models is the Detached Eddy Simulation (DES) [15]. The term
―Detached Eddy Simulation‖ comes from the fact that this
model is meant to resolve the large eddies far from any
boundary, i.e., ―detached eddies‖, in an LES-like manner.

Test Case Description
The majority of the previous experimental [28, 29] and
numerical [4-6, 11-14, 16] studies on the film cooling flow
have been performed under low (~1%) Free Stream Turbulence
(FST). However, the flow in a gas turbine engine typically has
FST on the order of 10-20% in the regions where film cooling
is employed, and turbulence in the free stream is highly
anisotropic. FST introduces additional length scales,
compounding an already complex problem. It is shown that
correctly predicting flows under high FST is a challenge even
in relatively simple geometries [30]. Therefore, a test case with
more realistic level of FST (10%) is chosen for this study.
The three-dimensional test case for the present study is
chosen according to the experiments of Mayhew et al. [31],
which has been numerically investigated by Yavuzkurt and
Hassan [7] and Yavuzkurt and Habte [8] (It is worthy to
mention here that there are very few studies in the literature
where enough information is supplied for a good simulation,
especially under high FST). It consists of a single-row of three
jet holes on a flat surface, with a 30o inline injection angle. The
diameter of the film cooling hole is D=12.5 mm, and holes have
a length-to-diameter ratio of 4.6 and a pitch-to-diameter ratio of
3. Experiments were performed in a wind tunnel with a free
stream flow velocity and temperature of 25 m/s and 298 K
respectively. The FST intensity was 10% at the upstream edge
of the film cooling holes. The ―coolant‖ fluid which is actually
heated air in these experiments was injected from a supply
plenum. Experimental measurements of the film cooling
adiabatic effectiveness 𝜂 were documented by Mayhew et al.
[31] for three blowing ratios of 0.5, 1.0, and 1.5 with injection
jet temperatures of 323K (typical) and 307K (only for the case
of M=0.5).

extends from the inflow plane located one diameter upstream of
the leading edge of the hole to outflow plane located 29

In General, in hybrid URANS/LES simulations, more cells
expectedly may lead to a better solution, however, the required
computing resource and time significantly increases. The final
computational grid is fully structured and consists of 1,427,640
hexahedral cells. The cells are arranged in such a way that they
are aligned with the anticipated flow direction to reduce
numerical diffusion. This will greatly reduce computation time
and prevent solution instabilities. A close-up of the mid-plane
view of the grid in the near-field region is depicted in Fig. 2.
The grid is refined in areas of large variable gradients. Since
turbulence models utilized in this study are used with two-layer
zonal model for near wall treatments, it is important that the y+
values of the grid points closest to the wall be of the order of
unity (similar to the low Reynolds number models). Here, the
grid generated is fine enough to reach y+≈0.1 in the wall
adjacent cells.
Boundary Conditions and Numerical Methodology
The velocity distributions were reported by Mayhew et al.
[31] 12D upstream of the test section. In order to save CPUtime, a separate test case of turbulent boundary layer starting
from that section (with known inlet conditions from [31]) is
simulated first. Then, the profiles of the mean velocity
components and turbulence quantities are extracted on a section
11D downstream (the location associated with the main domain
inlet), and implemented as inlet boundary conditions for the
main simulations. This reduction in upstream length which
significantly decreases the required time and computational
resources is investigated by Johnson et al. [33], and is shown to
have minimal effect on the results.
The influence of inflow turbulent fluctuations is taken into
account by applying perturbed inlet boundary conditions in
unsteady simulations. A practical approach to obtain perturbed
inlet conditions is to superimpose random noise perturbations
on the mean velocity profiles. Imposing fluctuations as close as
possible to those present in the real flow is crucial. Unphysical
randomness will be damped rapidly, hence inhibiting the
purpose of the method. To generate a time-dependent, perturbed
inlet condition, a random 2-D vortex method developed by
Mathey et al. [34] is used in this study. In this method, a
perturbation is added to the mean velocity profiles (here,
obtained from the above-mentioned method) via a fluctuating
vorticity field. This vorticity field is two-dimensional in the
plane normal to the mean flow direction. In the first time step,
the vortex positions are given randomly at the inlet plane. The
vortices are then given a random rotation sense such that the
total Turbulent Kinetic Energy (TKE) of vortices becomes the
same as the mean value specified by the inlet TKE profile.
These vortices then induce a fluctuating velocity based on the
Biot-Savart law. The use of this method requires the
specification of the mean turbulent kinetic energy and
dissipation rate profiles at the inlet of the computational domain
which are available in the present case. The accuracy of this
method has been investigated using several test cases [34, 35].

In order to compare the ability of RANS and hybrid
URAN/LES models in turbulent heat transport (velocitytemperature correlations) predictions, turbulent heat flux vector
𝑢𝑖 𝜃 is investigated. Figures 10 to 12 show the predictions of the

streamwise, wall-normal, and spanwise distributions of nondimensional turbulent heat flux vector at the hole centerline
plane and for different streamwise locations, i.e., x/D. RANS
results are based on the reconstruction of 𝑢𝑖 𝜃 components using
Eq. (3), while DES results are the combinations of resolved and
unresolved (Eq.(3)) parts. As x/D increases, the temperature
fluctuations and gradients reduce due to the mixing between the
coolant air and the mainstream, thus, the turbulent heat flux
components decrease.
Figure 10 displays the distributions of streamwise
component of turbulent heat flux 𝑢𝜃. It has a positive sign near
the wall and a negative sign in the outer region. This is due to
the fact that the temperature gradient inside the boundary layer
in the streamwise direction is negative while it is positive
outside (Note the negative sign in Eq. (3) and the fact that in
the Mayhew et al.’s [31] case the jet has a higher temperature
than the mainstream). The RANS and DES models show
relatively similar trends for this component. However, the DES
model predicts considerably higher values. The wall-normal
component of the turbulent heat flux 𝑣𝜃 as represented in Fig.
11, has a negative sign near the wall where the jet is (partially)
detached and a positive sign farther away (between the jet core
and far-field away from the wall). The discrepancy between the
RANS and DES predictions are relatively small for this
component, confirming the fact that RANS simulations are
known to be adequate in predicting the mixing in the wallnormal direction. The distributions of the spanwise component
of the turbulent heat flux 𝑤𝜃 are illustrated in Fig. 12. Again,
DES predicts higher levels for this component compared to
RANS. It can be concluded, in general, that applying the hybrid
URANS/LES model causes an increase in the predicted
components of turbulent heat flux, resulting in enhancing the
heat transfer between the coolant air and mainstream.
The centerline effectiveness obtained using time-averaged
DES and steady RANS simulations are plotted in Fig. 13 in
comparison with experimental data [31]. Obviously, the
centerline effectiveness is reduced in comparison with low
blowing ratio (Fig. 6) due to the jet lift-off and stronger CRVP.
The RANS simulations not only cannot predict enough mixing
between the jet and crossflow in spanwise direction, they also
fail to capture the jet lift-off phenomenon in this case. This
results in overprediction of the centerline effectiveness.
Nevertheless, this overprediction remains even farther
downstream due to the higher momentum within the jet core
and RANS simulations being unable to model the correct
momentum transport of a jet in cross flow. The results obtained
using DES model show improvements in the near-field region
due to enhanced jet penetration simulations both in spanwise
and wall-normal directions. However, it seems that the extent of
the jet lift-off is overpredicted by DES, resulting in an
underprediction of 𝜂 between x/D=2 and x/D=6. Farther
downstream where the jet is predicted to be reattaching to the
surface, the centerline effectiveness is overpredicted, although
the results are improved compared to the steady RANS case.
