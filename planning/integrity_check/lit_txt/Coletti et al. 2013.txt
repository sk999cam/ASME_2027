The present study experimentally investigates a turbulent jet in crossﬂow relevant to ﬁlm cooling applications. The jet is inclined at 30°, and its mean velocity is the same as the crossﬂow. Magnetic resonance
imaging is used to obtain the full three-dimensional velocity and concentration ﬁelds, whereas Reynolds
stresses are obtained along selected planes by Particle Image Velocimetry. The critical role of the counterrotating vortex pair in the mixing process is apparent from both velocity and concentration ﬁelds. The jet
entrainment is not signiﬁcantly higher than in an axisymmetric jet without crossﬂow, because the proximity of the wall inhibits the turbulent transport. Reynolds shear stresses correlate with velocity and concentration gradients, consistent with the fundamental assumptions of simple turbulence models.
However the eddy viscosity is strongly anisotropic and non-homogeneous, being especially low along
the leeward side of the jet close to injection. Turbulent diffusion acts to decouple mean velocity and concentration ﬁelds, as demonstrated by the drop in concentration ﬂux within the streamtube issued from
the hole. Volume-averaged turbulent diffusivity is calculated using a mass–ﬂux balance across the
streamtube emanating from the jet hole, and it is found to vary slowly in the streamwise direction.
The data are compared with Reynolds-Averaged Navier–Stokes simulations with standard k  e closure
and an optimal turbulent Schmidt number. The computations underestimate the strength of the counter-rotating vortex pair, due to an overestimated eddy viscosity. On the other hand the entrainment is
increasingly underpredicted downstream of injection. To capture the correct macroscopic trends, eddy
viscosity and eddy diffusivity should vary spatially in different ways. Therefore a constant turbulent
Schmidt number formulation is inadequate for this ﬂow.

1. Introduction
The interaction of a jet with a crossﬂow is a widely studied conﬁguration in ﬂuids engineering, due to its numerous applications,
from pollutant plumes to ﬁlm cooling (Margason, 1993; Mahesh,
2013). The latter is a widely employed cooling technique for gas
turbines: internal cooling air bleeds through small holes machined
on the walls of the component, and creates a layer that protects the
surface from the incoming high temperature gas (Bogard and
Thole, 2006). Because of the relatively high Reynolds numbers of
the ﬂows involved, the ﬁlm cooling process is governed by threedimensional turbulent mixing.
In the open literature, fully resolved simulations of velocity and
scalar ﬁelds of turbulent jets in crossﬂow are mostly concerned
with canonical/simpliﬁed cases. For example Muppidi and Mahesh
(2008) investigated a normal jet issued from a long pipe into a laminar boundary layer, with a velocity ratio of 5.7. They obtained

Given the elevated cost of performing time-accurate simulations, most numerical models for three-dimensional turbulent
ﬂows in general, and for ﬁlm cooling in particular, are based on
the Reynolds-Averaged Navier–Stokes (RANS) approach. This is
true especially when the actual geometries (for example, details
of the combustion chamber and the airfoils in gas turbines) need
to be faithfully replicated, which would make Large Eddy Simulations (LESs) and Direct Numerical Simulations (DNSs) prohibitively
expensive. In the RANS turbulence closures, Reynolds stresses are
either determined via a turbulent viscosity model (Launder and
Sharma, 1974), or via solution of modeled Reynolds-stress transport equations (Speziale et al., 1991). The turbulent heat ﬂuxes
are typically determined via a gradient-diffusion assumption with
a scalar turbulent diffusivity and constant turbulent Prandtl number PrT = mT/aT, where mT and aT are the turbulent viscosity and diffusivity, respectively. One of the underlying assumptions is a direct
analogy between the momentum and thermal transport (Reynolds
analogy).

While the turbulence closures for the momentum transfer can
deliver acceptable results even for practical ﬂows, the constant
PrT model can be used to compute only relatively simple boundary-layer, channel or pipe ﬂows (Kays, 1994). As a result, commonly used solvers largely mispredict scalar distributions in
conﬁgurations different from the canonical ﬂows for which they
have been developed. This is often given as a reason why Reynolds
stress models do not perform substantially better than the simpler
eddy-viscosity models (Hoda and Acharya, 2000). Recently several
models have been devised in order to capture the physics of anisotropic dispersion. Abe (2005) proposed a hybrid approach connecting LES with RANS modeling in the near-wall region. Younis et al.
(2005) presented a functional relationship between the turbulent
scalar ﬂuxes and various tensorial properties of the velocity ﬁeld.
A speciﬁc model for ﬁlm cooling ﬂows was devised by Lakehal
(2002) coupling a standard two-equation model with a DNS-informed one-equation model resolving the near-wall layer. Liu
et al. (2008) incorporated a laterally varying turbulent PrT in a realizable k  e calculation and found more accurate prediction of ﬁlm
cooling effectiveness. However most of these models use ad hoc
assumptions, which do not guarantee broad applicability to a wide
range of conﬁgurations. Further progress in modeling of turbulent
mixing in three-dimensional ﬂows is hindered by the lack of detailed and accurate experimental data for non-canonical ﬂows. Indeed the constant PrT model remains the most widely used for the
prediction of ﬁlm cooling ﬂows, although its performance is poor,
especially near injection (Kohli and Bogard, 2005).
From the experimental standpoint, several attempts have been
made to investigate the jet in crossﬂow dynamics by means of advanced measurements techniques, especially for conﬁgurations
relevant to ﬁlm cooling. Kaszeta and Simon (2000) used triple sensor anemometry to capture the three instantaneous components of
velocity, pointing out the anisotropy of the eddy viscosity. Kohli
and Bogard (2005) used cold wire thermometry in combination
with Laser-Doppler velocimetry to obtain the turbulent scalar
ﬂuxes, and highlighted the limits of the Reynolds analogy. Jessen
et al. (2007) and Bernsdorf et al. (2006) studied ﬁlm cooling conﬁgurations by two-dimensional and stereoscopic Particle Image
Velocimetry (PIV), respectively. Issakhanian et al. (2012) used
Magnetic Resonance Velocimetry (MRV) to explore the details of
the in-hole and near-injection dynamics. None of these studies
presented full three-dimensional distributions of the scalar ﬁeld
downstream of injection. Benson et al. (2011) achieved this by
Magnetic Resonance Concentration measurements (MRC) in a
cut-back trailing edge ﬁlm cooling conﬁguration. However the latter differs signiﬁcantly from a discrete-hole conﬁguration, resembling more a wall-jet rather than a jet in crossﬂow.

With respect to single-point techniques, ﬁeld measurements
such as PIV and MRV/MRC offer the advantage of capturing the
large scale dynamics, which is of prime importance in the type of
ﬂow investigated here. PIV struggles with strong out-of-plane
components, whereas MRI-based techniques deliver full threedimensional ﬂow information and three-dimensional scalar ﬁelds.
The latter cannot be obtained by any other method, unless 1D or
2D techniques are used in conjunction with complex traversing
systems. On the other hand, the need of inserting the test apparatus in the MRI bore limits the maximum size of the test section,
which in the present case results in some conﬁnement on the jet
dynamics. This has to be taken into account when performing
quantitative comparisons with other studies. Finally, MRI-techniques do not provide easy access to the Reynolds stresses. PIV is
used here to obtain this information along selected planes.
The present study focuses on a single-hole ﬁlm-cooling conﬁguration, where a non-buoyant contaminant is injected into a channel ﬂow. The goal is to provide detailed data for a single parameter
set that will provide new understanding of turbulent mass and
momentum transfer. The full three-dimensional mean velocity
ﬁeld is obtained by means of MRV, and the three-dimensional scalar concentration ﬁeld is obtained by MRC. Particle Image Velocimetry (PIV) is used to measure the Reynolds stresses along
selected planes. Finally, RANS calculations using the commercial
software FLUENT are compared with the experimental data, and
the validity of the underlying assumptions is discussed.

2. Apparatus and procedures
2.1. Set-up and operating conditions
The set-up was presented in detail by Coletti et al. (submitted
for publication), and is only brieﬂy described here. The experimental model is sketched in Fig. 1a. It consists of several sections fabricated using stereolithography and assembled together in a
channel 1350 mm in length. It includes a diffusing section with
screens to prevent separation, a honeycomb to avoid secondary
ﬂows, and a contraction which provides a low-turbulence uniform
ﬂow to the 50 mm by 50 mm test section. The boundary layer is
tripped about 210 mm upstream of the hole location. The round
jet tube is D = 5.8 mm in diameter and L = 24 mm long. It is fed
from a plenum and enters the main ﬂow at an angle of 30°. The
boundary layer thickness upstream of injection (estimated as the
distance from the wall at which the velocity is 95% of the centerline
velocity) is about twice the hole diameter. An identical test section
is used for the PIV measurements, but with transparent Plexiglas

The closed-loop ﬂow system includes a holding tank, from
which two electric pumps extract the main ﬂow and jet ﬂow, passing through the channel and recirculating back to the tank. The elements are connected by 25.4 mm diameter plastic hoses. The main

and the bulk velocity (Ubulk = 0.5 m/s), is 25,000. The velocity ratio
is one (Uj = Ubulk) which results in a jet Reynolds number of 2900.
Since the density ratio (qj/qbulk) is one, the blowing ratio (qjUj/
qbulkUbulk) and the momentum ﬂux ratio (qj U 2j =qbulk U 2bulk ) are also
one. According to Thole et al. (1992) a ﬁlm cooling jet is fully detached for momentum ﬂux ratios higher than 0.8. Our measurements conﬁrm that the present jet is fully detached. The
Reynolds number regime is signiﬁcantly lower than in turbine ﬁlm
cooling applications. Much higher Reynolds numbers would likely
produce quantitative differences with respect to the present experiments. The scope of the study is however to explore fundamental
aspects of the phenomenon by the analysis of a well-deﬁned conﬁguration, rather than replicating engine-like conditions. Moreover, since the ﬂow is fully turbulent, the reported trends are not
expected to change at higher ﬂow regimes.
The working ﬂuid is water, which does not diminish the relevance of these experiments to gas turbine applications, because
in the present regimes the turbulent transport coefﬁcients are orders of magnitude higher than their molecular counterpart. The
heat-mass transfer analogy holds, and therefore the following results about the turbulent Schmidt number can be interpreted in
terms of turbulent Prandtl number.
2.2. Measurement methods
Three-dimensional mean velocity ﬁelds are obtained using the
method of Magnetic Resonance Velocimetry (MRV) described by
Elkins et al. (2003). MRV makes use of a technique very similar
to that used in conventional medical magnetic resonance imaging
(MRI). Quantitative assessment of the ﬂow is obtained thanks to
the sensitivity of the phase of the MR signal to motion. The procedure for the data acquisition technique is described by Pelc et al.
(1994). Experiments are performed at the Richard M. Lucas Center
for Imaging (Stanford, CA) with a 3 Tesla General Electric scanner,
with a transmit-and-receive radio frequency coil commonly used
for imaging human heads. Using the same MRI facility, the concentration of a passive scalar (copper sulfate) added to the ﬁlm cooling
ﬂow is measured following the MRC (Magnetic Resonance Concentration) procedure developed by Benson et al. (2010). Time-averaged velocity and concentration data are obtained on a uniform
grid at a resolution of 0.6 mm in all three directions. The uncertainty in the velocity ﬁelds is about 6% of the bulk velocity,
whereas uncertainty in the concentration is 5% of the maximum
concentration (both at 95% conﬁdence level). Details on the procedures are reported by Coletti et al. (submitted for publication).

Measurements of mean velocity and Reynolds stresses are performed by means of two-dimensional PIV along the symmetry
plane of the jet and along a wall-parallel plane at 0.5D from the
injection wall. The light source is a pulsed Spectra-Physics PIV400 Nd:YAG laser (532 nm). The laser beam is shaped into a
0.5 mm thick sheet by means of a convergent spherical lens of focal
length f = 1000 mm followed by a cylindrical lens of f = 60 mm.
The images are acquired by a 12-bit Princeton Instruments CCD
camera (1300 by 1030 pixels2). A magniﬁcation factor of about
112 pixel/mm is achieved using a 105 mm Canon objective. Five
slightly overlapping windows (about 12 by 9 mm2 in size) are
investigated along the symmetry plane, whereas four windows
are considered for the wall-parallel plane. The sampling frequency
is 10 Hz, which guarantees statistically independent realizations.
In the post-processing, the initial interrogation windows are 128
by 128 pixel2. Two reﬁnement steps lead to a ﬁnal resolution of
0.28 by 0.28 mm2. Mean velocity and rms of the velocity ﬂuctuations are obtained by averaging over 4000 image pairs.
2.3. RANS calculations
We use the experimental data obtained for the present ﬁlm
cooling conﬁguration in order to assess the performance and the
limitations of a commonly employed RANS model. The standard
k-e model with enhanced wall treatment and a coupled second-order solver was employed using the software FLUENT 13.0. This
choice is in accordance with the best practices developed through
several studies comparing different modeling strategies for these
types of ﬂows (Ferguson et al., 1998). Karvinen and Ahlstedt
(2005) compared the performance of various FLUENT turbulence
models in a jet in crossﬂow conﬁguration, and showed that standard k   gave the best agreement with experimental results in
terms of their prediction of the velocity proﬁles and turbulent kinetic energy proﬁles.
The numerical set-up fully replicates the experimental conﬁguration, including the main ﬂow channel and the supply plenum.
The inlet and outlet of the main channel are set 15D upstream
and 35D downstream of the hole. The grid is structured and consists of 2.5 million hexahedral cells. Boundary layer meshes were
applied to both the ﬁlm-cooled wall of the channel and the inside
of the hole, with the ﬁrst cell located at y+ <1. The computation was
run on single node with 36 GB of RAM and took 48 h to converge to
residuals of less than 106. Grid-independent convergence was
conﬁrmed by running the same simulation on a grid with 4.6 million cells and it was shown that the turbulent viscosity varied on
average by less than 1% in the region of interest near the jet.
The main inlet turbulence boundary conditions were prescribed
based on empirical relations from Kim et al. (1987) and an estimated boundary layer thickness based on the experimental velocity proﬁles. The turbulence conditions at the coolant plenum inlet
are prescribed as 1% turbulent intensity and 1 mm turbulent length
scale, which compare well with dedicated PIV measurements. For

3.1. Three-dimensional velocity and concentration ﬁelds
The main topological features of a jet in crossﬂow have long
been identiﬁed (see for example Fric and Roshko, 1994), although
their origin and dynamic role is still under debate (Marzouk and
Ghoniem, 2007). The present work deals with the mean ﬂow statistics and focuses on features that have a macroscopic impact on the
scalar transport. Therefore we will restrict our attention to the
counter-rotating vortex pair (CVP), which is the feature of highest
dynamical signiﬁcance, and the one that leaves the strongest footprint on the time-averaged quantities. The CVP is clearly visible in
Fig. 2, obtained from MRV. In particular, positive and negative isosurfaces of streamwise vorticity xZ = ±0.3Uj/D are shown in Fig. 2a.
Fig. 2b displays color contours of streamwise velocity along a number of transverse planes, which stress the velocity deﬁcit due to the
blockage imposed to the crossﬂow by the jet. Finally the in-plane
velocity vectors in Fig. 2c demonstrate how the CVP acts to bring
outer ﬂow close to the wall, also lifting the jet ﬂuid. It is clear
why this feature is often indicated as the major ﬂow structure
responsible for the mixing of jets in crossﬂow in general, and in
ﬁlm cooling conﬁgurations in particular.
It is well known that the CVP persists long after injection. Its
cumulative effect in stirring the ﬂow is well visualized by plotting
a streamtube found using the experimental mean velocity measurements (Fig. 3). A streamtube is deﬁned as the surface formed
by all the streamlines that pass through a given closed curve in
the ﬂuid (Batchelor, 1967). In the present case the curve is chosen
as the ellipsoidal intersection of the hole with the main channel
(offset towards the hole center by 0.1D in order to have non-zero

velocity vectors). The originating curve is highlighted in red (color
online) in Fig. 3, whereas successive cross-sections of the tube are
in black. One thousand, streamlines (calculated with the opensource software ParaView) are integrated from equally spaced
points along the originating curve. The streamlines are then used
as skeleton to build the streamtube (which is calculated with the
open-source visualization package VTK). The convoluted shape of
the streamtube is the result of the CVP acting over its whole length.
From a topological standpoint the CVP, by stretching and folding
the jet stream-tube, increases the exchange area offered to the
main ﬂow.
In the present ﬂow regime, the distribution of the scalar injected by the inclined jet is not only a function of the ﬂow topology,
but is also affected by the turbulent transport properties, which
cannot be inferred from the mean velocity alone. To illustrate the
three-dimensional structure of the coolant jet, iso-surfaces of mean
concentration are shown for nine levels between 80% and 20%
(Fig. 4a–i). The footprint of the CVP is visible in the scalar distribution up to about three hole diameters downstream of the injection
(a–d): the two vortices are recognizable in the double-lobed shape
of the iso-concentration surfaces. However, further downstream
(and therefore at lower concentration levels), the concentration
iso-surfaces are almost oval, indicating that the turbulent transport
is dominating over the advection.

ﬂux from the leeward side does not exceed 50% of the total. This
supports our conclusion that the proximity of the wall inhibits
the turbulent transport on this side, limiting its contribution to
the jet entrainment.

3.4. Evaluation of the turbulent diffusivity
In order to directly measure the turbulent diffusivity it is necessary to measure the turbulent scalar ﬂuxes (or equivalently the
turbulent heat ﬂuxes). However such measurements are very difﬁcult even in canonical ﬂows, and in three-dimensional ﬂows they
can be practically unfeasible. To our best knowledge, the only measurements of turbulent diffusivity in a ﬁlm cooling ﬂow were obtained by Kohli and Bogard (2005), using Laser Doppler
Velocimetry in conjunction with a cold-wire. Their results were
limited to the symmetry plane.

diffusivity should be adopted in the RANS model. However, to capture the persistence of the CVP, a lower (or streamwise decreasing)

turbulent Schmidt number of 0.85 was found to be optimal by
comparison with the present data set (Ling et al., submitted for

even in a relatively simple conﬁguration as the present one. For
the important ﬂow and scalar details to be properly reproduced,
both mT and aT should vary spatially (and independently) in the
correct manner.
4. Conclusions

Fig. 16. Mean concentration along Y/D = 0.5 plane, from MRC measurements (a)
and from RANS simulations (b).

An inclined jet in cross-ﬂow has been investigated in a water
tunnel by means of Magnetic Resonance Velocimetry, Magnetic
Resonance Concentration measurements, and Particle Image
Velocimetry. The ﬂow conﬁguration is relevant to ﬁlm cooling
technology, since the molecular properties of the ﬂuid are irrelevant in the fully turbulent regime. The heat-mass transfer analogy
is utilized, and the conclusions concerning the scalar concentration
can be also interpreted in terms of temperature.
The mean ﬂow is dominated by the CVP aligned in the streamwise direction, which brings crossﬂow ﬂuid into the vicinity of the
wall. The streamtube issued by the hole is stretched and folded by
the CVP, increasing the surface for mass exchange with the crossﬂow. The streamwise vorticity leaves its footprint on the scalar
ﬁeld close to injection, but turbulent diffusion acts to smear out
this pattern, marking a strong decoupling between mean velocity
and mean concentration ﬁelds. This consideration is used to quantify the eddy diffusivity, considering a mass ﬂux balance through

the streamtube issued by the hole. The eddy diffusivity is shown to
be fairly constant in the streamwise direction.
In the ﬁrst few diameters downstream of injection, the jet
entrainment is slightly enhanced (with respect to an axisymmetric
jet in quiescent environment) by the formation of the CVP. However, further downstream, the proximity to the wall inhibits this
process. As a consequence, and unlike perpendicular jets in crossﬂow, the leeward side and the windward side contribute almost
equally to the entrainment.
The intense turbulent ﬂuctuations along both sides of the jet
shear layers mark the envelope of the injected scalar. The Reynolds
shear stresses are found to correlate qualitatively with the primary
shear and concentration gradients, which seemingly supports the
use of the eddy-viscosity hypothesis, the gradient-diffusion
hypothesis and the Reynolds analogy. However the turbulent viscosity is strongly non-uniform and anisotropic, underscoring that
these concepts can hardly be used to make quantitative
predictions.
A direct comparison against a standard k-e calculation highlights the limitations of RANS modeling for the present ﬂow. The
formation mechanism of the CVP and its strength are not properly
captured. Close to injection, the local minimum of eddy viscosity in
between the counter-rotating vortices is not predicted. Consequently, RANS predicts an excess of mass and momentum transport radially from the CVP, resulting in over predicted coolant
concentrations in the vicinity of the wall. On the other hand, further from injection, jet entrainment is largely underpredicted. It
is concluded that, for an eddy viscosity model to capture the correct macroscopic trends, turbulent viscosity and diffusivity should
vary spatially in different ways. Therefore a constant ScT model is
clearly inadequate for this ﬂow.
