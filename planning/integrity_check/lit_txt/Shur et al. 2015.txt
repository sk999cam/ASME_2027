An Enhanced Version of DES with Rapid Transition
from RANS to LES in Separated Flows
Mikhail L. Shur1,2 · Philippe R. Spalart3 ·
Mikhail Kh. Strelets1,2 · Andrey K. Travin1,2

Received: 4 November 2014 / Accepted: 1 May 2015 / Published online: 10 June 2015
© Springer Science+Business Media Dordrecht 2015

Abstract The paper addresses the issue of the significant delay of transition from RANS
to LES in shear layers, which is known to affect the original version of Detached-Eddy
Simulation (DES) on typical anisotropic grids. A common remedy has been to disable the
subgrid scale model, leading to Implicit LES (ILES). Here, enhanced versions of DES
are proposed based on new definitions of the subgrid length-scale. Unlike the original
definition attached to DES (i.e., simply the maximum local grid spacing) the new ones
include solution-dependent kinematic measures which serve as indicators of the nearly 2D
grid-aligned flow regions which are typical of the initial region of free and separated shear
layers. This brings about a significant reduction of the subgrid viscosity in such regions.
This, in turn, unlocks the Kelvin-Helmholtz instability and drastically speeds-up transition
to 2D and then 3D flow structures in shear layers. At the same time, the proposed lengthscale is not influenced by the smallest grid dimension, unlike the cube root of the cell
volume and other recently proposed definitions, and we view this as a physically plausible,
safe and therefore preferable length-scale definition. The advantages of these enhanced versions are demonstrated on a set of numerical examples which include isotropic turbulence,
a mixing layer, a jet, a boundary layer, and a backward-facing step. The new definitions
are as successful as ILES in liberating the early instabilities, while being non-zonal and
compatible with later interactions of the turbulent region with solid bodies. The turbulence
statistics of the flows and the radiated noise of the jet are also considerably improved, especially with relatively coarse lateral grid spacings. The new definitions will also improve
LES, particularly with the Smagorinsky model.

1 Introduction
It is well known now that the original DES approach severely delays the Kelvin-Helmholtz
instability and transition from modeled to resolved turbulence in free and separated shear
layers [1]. This deficiency is explained by peculiarities of the grids typically used in simulations: in order to capture, e.g., jet shear-layers or trailing edges at a reasonable cost, these
grids are fine across the shear layer and maybe in the streamwise direction, but coarse in
the spanwise/azimuthal direction. This creates “book”, “pencil” or “ribbon” grid cells (see
Table 1) which are very different from the nearly isotropic cells assumed when LES and the
LES function within DES are applied with the cut-off in the inertial range [2].
This flaw which had been anticipated already in the first presentation of DES in Spalart
et al. [3], tangibly restricts its applicability, e.g., it makes it, in fact, non-applicable to the
effective prediction of the noise generated by high Reynolds number jets, and it also considerably decreases the accuracy of the prediction of aerodynamic flows with separation of
thin boundary layers. Primarily the issue is related to the measure of subgrid length scale,
, which enters the Subgrid Scale (SGS) branch of DES and all but very few other hybrid
and LES methods. In [3] this measure was defined as the maximum of the cell sizes in all
three directions, max ≡ max(x, y, z). This definition is actually independent of the
hybrid nature of DES, and we consider max as a more rational and safer definition of 
also for an LES, in general, compared to, e.g., the common
√ definition of the subgrid lengthscale as the cube root of the grid-cell volume vol = 3 xyz. In the archetypal LES
(and LES region in DES or similar hybrid RANS-LES methods), that is, with regular grids
which have essentially cubic cells of a size  falling within the Kolmogorov inertial range,
these definitions are nearly identical. However, LES and, especially DES are routinely
applied in unstructured and/or irregular and/or highly anisotropic structured grids, where
the definitions become far from equivalent. An important point is that, even in the normal
LES situation with statistically isotropic eddies near the cut-off, it is easy to see that the
smaller dimensions of a strongly anisotropic grid cell do not contribute to the grid resolution
capability, and therefore have no place in the subgrid length-scale supplied to the SGS

At the same time, in the shear-layer situation with the lateral grid step much larger than
the other two, the max definition becomes too “conservative”, i.e., it results in an excessive
generation of SGS eddy viscosity, thus resulting in effectively RANS functioning of the
model and naturally defeating the Kelvin-Helmholtz (KH) instability.1 This is “safe” since it
does not over-tax the grid resolution, but it is counter-productive in terms of the development
of turbulence farther downstream. In fact, with very large z it is conceptually correct
for DES to behave like a pure RANS, and to carry all the Reynolds stress as modelled.
However, if the intent is to liberate the Kelvin-Helmholtz instability, very low values of eddy
viscosity, enough to make the grid-cell Reynolds number based on that viscosity moderate,
are needed. Considering that the initial region of the separated shear layers is nearly 2D,
there is no vortex stretching or energy cascade in this region. Hence, an eddy-viscosity
aimed, as in the archetypal LES, at controlling small-scale energy and preventing Eulerequation singularities is not needed at all, and the calibration of the SGS model carried out
in the inertial range becomes irrelevant. Therefore, for such a flow the mild “abuse” of the
LES concept associated with lowering the eddy viscosity does not lead to singularities, and
may often improve the overall accuracy of the simulation, provided of course that this takes
place away from walls.
As a result, although for quite different reasons, neither the vol nor the max definition
of the subgrid length-scale is perfect.
Another aspect of the issue is that although these definitions depend only on the grid, in
order to produce an eddy viscosity they are combined with measures of the flow, such as
the vorticity and strain tensor, within SGS turbulence models, whether in the Smagorinsky
or any other model. Hence it does not seem to be inconsistent to look for improvements
in the definition of  that introduce a dependence on the solution, including specific features of the flow in question such as the nearly 2D character of the early shear layers.

3 Description of the Numerics
All the simulations are carried out with the use of the in-house NTS code [15, 16]. It
is a cell-vertex, finite-volume code accepting multi-block overset grids of Chimera type.
The incompressible branch of the code employs the Rogers-Kwak flux difference splitting
scheme [17], whereas in the compressible branch the Roe scheme [18] is used. In both
cases the viscous fluxes in the governing equations are approximated with second-order
central differences, whereas the spatial approximation of the inviscid fluxes is different in
the RANS and LES regions. In particular, in the RANS regions, a 3rd order upwind-biased
scheme is used, and in the LES zone a pure 4th -order central scheme or a weighted (4th
order central / 5th order upwind biased) scheme is employed [15, 19]. For the time integration, an implicit 2nd order backward Euler scheme with sub-iterations is used (a typical
number of sub-iterations, ensuring at least 2 orders of magnitude drop of the residuals, is
10).

5 Concluding Remarks
Two modifications are proposed to the original definition of the subgrid length-scale used in
DES and sometimes LES (namely,  = max ), aimed at an acceleration of the RANS-toLES transition in flows involving free and separated shear layers within DES and/or DDES.
A notable feature of the new definition is that it includes not only the grid spacing in each
direction but also kinematic, solution-dependent indicators of the nearly 2D flow regions

Our concern over definitions which are driven by the smallest spacing will be viewed as
more theoretical or philosophical than crucial in practice, because all simulations have some
measure of numerical dissipation. However, we predict that definitions which do not detect
situations in which the vorticity is not aligned with the long direction of “pencil” cells will
promote a KH instability that is not well resolved by the grid, which could have sequels
downstream.
The advantages of the newly proposed length-scale definition incorporated in DES and
DDES are convincingly demonstrated on a set of numerical examples including decaying
isotropic homogeneous turbulence, a plane spatially evolving shear layer, a round subsonic jet, and a backward-facing step flow. In all the cases a crucial acceleration of the
RANS-to-LES transition is achieved, which results in a drastic improvement of prediction
accuracy and much less sensitivity to the lateral grid-spacing. In particular, for the jet test
case, it is shown that the enhanced versions of DES (that is, combined with new definition of the length-scale) are quite competitive with the zonal approaches, e.g., the ZDES
or Implicit LES which has often been considered as the most accurate tool for jet-noise
predictions.

interactions of the proposed modification to the subgrid lengths-scale with empirical functions involved in DDES and, especially the IDDES formulations, which requires further

Note that an alternative, although much more complicated and computationally expensive approach aimed at resolving the issue of delayed RANS-to-LES transition relies upon
the injection of turbulent content at the inlet of an LES or WMLES area located downstream
of the RANS area. In principle, the methods of this category present an optimal compromise between efficiency and generality. They are computationally affordable today only for
small regions, and are capable of predicting not only separated flows and free shear layers
considered in the present work but also attached and mildly separated flows, in which “the
new instability” mechanism ensuring RANS-to-LES transition is absent. In such methods
the issue of delayed RANS-to-LES transition also exists but it has a different origin (it is
caused by the inevitable imperfection of the injected turbulence, which prevents an immediate transition to realistic mostly resolved turbulence in the WMLES region). In order to
decrease this delay or, in other words, to decrease the length of the “adaptation region”
required to establish “mature” turbulence downstream of the RANS-LES interface, it is
essential to improve the “quality” of the artificial turbulent content injected at the interface,
i.e., to make it as close to real turbulence as possible, which is currently an area of intensive
research in our group and many others.
