a b s t r a c t
Large eddy simulation (LES) is used to predict the turbulent ﬂow structures and ﬁlm-cooling characteristics of the unsteady jet/cross-ﬂow interactions in a fan-shaped hole. The coolant hole is located on a
ﬂat plate surface with a 35 degree angle with respect to the main ﬂow stream at a constant ﬁlm
density ratio D R = 2 and two different blowing ratios M = 1 and 2. The computational results under
the different blowing ratios are validated by measurements data for the laterally-averaged and timeaveraged ﬁlm-cooling effectiveness. In addition, two different hybrid RANS/LES turbulence models, SAS
(scale-adaptive simulation) and DES (detached-eddy simulation), are employed to show the inﬂuence
of the turbulence model on the ﬁlm-cooling effectiveness. The results reveal that the LES model is the
only turbulence model that can calculate the ﬁlm-cooling performance with an acceptable accuracy as
compared to the experimental results. Comprehensive analyses are performed on the time-averaged and
instantaneous turbulent ﬂows within the cooling hole and mainstream channel, and the results show that
the cooling jet ﬂow structures and forest of hairpin vortices on the ﬂat plate are signiﬁcantly changed
due to the blowing ratio. It is also observed that a pulsating behavior exists within the coolant hole,
which is convected toward the hole exit. The interaction between the cooling hole ﬂow and channel
mainstream ﬂow leads to unsteadiness at the interface mixing region (Kelvin-Helmholtz instability). In
addition, velocity disturbances with various convective velocities are observed in the time-series ﬂow
ﬁelds at different blowing ratios. The evaluation of pressure ﬂuctuation signals in the time and frequency
domains show that the dominant frequency of the fan-shaped hole is shifted by changing the blowing
ratio, and increasing the blowing ratio leads to the generation of more periodic vortical structures as well
as improved ﬁlm-cooling performance.

1. Introduction
Gas turbines are extensively used in various industrial applications particularly in the aero-propulsion of aircraft engines. In
order to enhance the power output and thermal performance of
modern gas turbines, a working ﬂuid gas with a higher temperature at the inlet of the gas turbine engine is required [1]. However,
the temperatures of hot main ﬂow gases, reaching about 1800 K,
often exceed the melting temperatures of the alloys currently used
in the turbine blades [2,3]. Therefore, in order to keep the turbine
components safe from high temperature gases, various effective
and appropriate cooling methods, both internally and externally,
are needed.

Film cooling as an external cooling method is one of the most
common and useful cooling approaches that is frequently used in
advanced gas turbine engines [4,5]. In the process of ﬁlm cooling, cooler air from the coolant chamber and within the cooling
holes located in the surfaces of turbine blades is injected into the
hot ﬂuid cross-ﬂow stream. The injection of this air into the external boundary layer creates a ﬁlm layer of coolant over the blades,
which protects the surface from the high temperature mainstream
ﬂow. In general, the coolant is injected into the mainstream at an
angle. This reduces both the lift-off of the jet and the mixing of
the coolant with the mainstream ﬂow at a larger distance from
the surface. Hence more colder ﬂuid is available to cool a larger
surface area. However, the interaction between the injected cooling hole ﬂow and the upstream hot ﬂuid ﬂow leads to complicated,
transient, and irregular vortical structures at the coolant hole exit.
These vortices are divided in various main groups, such as the
horseshoe and wake vortices, the jet shear-layer, and the counterrotating vortex pair (CRVP) [6]. It has been found that CRVPs, kidney vortices, have the potential to rotate the ﬂow near the cooling

ﬁlm-cooling effectiveness and optimize the amount of coolant, it is
essential to investigate in detail the accomplishments of the ﬂow

Recently, a large number of experimental studies have been
performed to further elucidate the fundamental governing features and complexity of the ﬁlm-cooling ﬂows [8–13]. Sinha et
al. [14] investigated the inﬂuences of variable density ratios on
the ﬁlm-cooling performance of a single row of holes located on
an adiabatic ﬂat plate. They applied different density and blowing
ratios of the cooling ﬂuid and measured the surface temperature
at different locations in the lateral and streamwise directions using a thermocouple arrangement. The results indicated that the
ﬁlm-cooling jet-covered area is dramatically reduced by increasing the momentum ratio and decreasing the density ratio, which
leads to reduced lateral ﬁlm-cooling performance. The unsteady
effects of ﬂow patterns on ﬁlm-cooling were experimentally investigated by Fawcett et al. [15] for fan-shaped and cylindrical
cooling holes. Different unsteadiness and coherent vortices are
found in the shear layer region between the coolant ﬂow and main
ﬂow for both types of cooling holes, which have a strong inﬂuence on the ﬁlm-cooling performance. The inﬂuence of upstream
turbulence intensity on the ﬁlm-cooling effectiveness of antivortex holes was experimentally investigated by Park et al. [8]. They
found that the coolant-covered area generally increased with increasing blowing ratio. Fraas et al. [16] experimentally investigated
the inlet geometry effects of the cooling hole on the cooling efﬁciency for a laidback fan-shaped hole. They found that the ﬁlmcooling performance was signiﬁcantly dependent on the hole inlet
geometry. Despite the development of modern and accurate experimental techniques and devices for measuring the ﬁlm-cooling
effectiveness, these techniques still have distinct limits, speciﬁcally
in terms of scale, time, and cost. Consequently, improvements in
high-performance computers and numerical methods have led to
computational ﬂuid dynamics (CFD) as a robust appliance for predicting the ﬁlm-cooling effectiveness and ﬂow structures.
Over the last decade, a variety of numerical methods have been
applied for ﬁlm-cooling simulations. Direct numerical simulation
(DNS) [17,18] numerically solves the Navier-Stokes equations and
resolves the full range of the turbulent length and temporal scales.
Muldoon and Acharya [18] performed a DNS simulation to determine the effects of cooling jet pulsation on the ﬁlm-cooling performance. They observed that the effectiveness of the overall cooling
process was enhanced under pulsating jet ﬂow as opposed to con-

DNS numerical model, making it expensive and not suitable for industrial utilization.
The most common numerical method used to predict ﬁlm-cooling effectiveness is the Reynolds-averaged Navier-Stokes (RANS)
approach. The RANS approach predicts reliable data regarding the
mean-ﬂow quantities [19–23]. Fan et al. [21] used the 3D viscous
steady RANS approach with the standard k − ω turbulence closure
to predict ﬁlm-cooling performance on the leading edge of the gas
turbine blade. It was found that, when the diameter ratio of the
cooling jet holes was increased to 0.1, the intensity of heat transfer reached its maximum value. Walters and Leylek [22] compared
their numerical simulations to the measurement results for a single row of 3D coolant jets, in which the values of the diameter
and length of cooling holes were the same as those of the exact
holes used in real applications. They found that CRVPs at the exit
of cooling holes are the primary inﬂuence on the ﬁlm-cooling effectiveness. The basic vortical structures are generated from the
coolant hole, and ﬁlm-cooling effectiveness could be greatly enhanced by controlling these vortices. A three-dimensional RANS
simulation based on k −  turbulence model was conducted in
order to show that the ﬁlm-cooling effectiveness of a diffusion
cooling hole was affected by changing the inclination/orientation
angle and the length of cooling hole [23]. It was found that the
k −  turbulence approach could provide precise information on
the overall ﬁlm-cooling performance; however, the lateral data exhibited differences as compared to the experimental results. While
the RANS approaches can mostly reproduce the ﬁlm-cooling effectiveness, the certainty of the cooling effectiveness predicted by
the RANS approach is dependent on the selection of turbulence
closure. Moreover, due to the transient and complex phenomenon
during the mixing process of jet and mainstream as well as secondary ﬂows and vortex interactions, the RANS approach cannot
resolve and capture the details of these kinds of ﬂows.
In recent years, the transient ﬂow characteristics of ﬁlm-cooling
have been employed using hybrid RANS/LES methods such as DES
or SAS. For example, Voigt et al. [24] conducted ﬁve different
steady and unsteady turbulence closures in order to simulate the
ﬁlm-cooling process within a cylinder-shaped hole located on a ﬂat
plate including conjugate heat transfer. They found that the unsteady numerical simulations generally predicted the ﬁlm-cooling
performance better than steady simulations, but that the SAS
model could resolve more turbulent structures than the URANSSST calculations. Lin et al. [25] applied DES and SAS approaches to
calculate the ﬁlm-cooling eﬃciency and aerodynamic performance

in a cylindrical cooling hole. They observed various conﬁgurations
in the cross-ﬂow jet region during the formation vortical structures. First, small vortices were generated from the cooling hole
exit, which then merged into each other to create a larger vortex.
These large vortices were then dissipated into heat and velocity
changes in the downstream of the main channel. The SAS approach
showed consistent ﬁlm-cooling performance as compared to that
of the experimental data only near the coolant hole exit. Although
hybrid methods can predict the ﬂow ﬁeld with reasonable accuracy, the ﬁlm-cooling performance and heat transfer characteristics
are still not consistent with the experimental data. Therefore, since
the jet/cross-ﬂow is inherently unsteady and the turbulence structures in the mixing region are highly anisotropic, a high-ﬁdelity
approach is necessary to simulate the ﬁlm-cooling process.
Over the last decade, LES has been extensively used for complex
ﬂow conﬁgurations, as it has been proven to precisely and reliably
predict highly unsteady vortical and anisotropic turbulence ﬂows
[26–29]. The high resolution predictions of the resolved length
and time scales of the ﬂow ﬁeld led to LES as a CFD tool for
more comprehensive investigations of the appropriate mean-ﬂow
quantities and instantaneous quantities for ﬁlm-cooling prediction
[30,31]. Wang et al. [30] performed LES simulation for heat transfer analysis of the ﬂow ﬁeld in the converging slot holes at various
blowing ratios. They observed that kidney and anti-kidney vortices
are the main vortical structures in which the kidney vortices deteriorate ﬁlm-cooling performance. Sakai and Takahashi [32] performed various LES simulations of coolant ﬂow through cylindrical
holes into a cross-ﬂow in order to ﬁgure out the ﬁlm-cooling process on a blade surface. They showed that the jet vortices change
signiﬁcantly with varying density ratios. At a low density ratio,
hairpin vortices are generated far from the coolant hole exit, while
at relatively large density ratios, the generations of these vortices
are prevented. While there have been various valuable researches
based on LES application in ﬁlm-cooling prediction, most of them
have been primarily focused on the cylindrical hole conﬁgurations. There have only been a few LES studies on different hole
geometries such as fan-shaped hole [29,33]. Since most numerical simulations have been focused on the cylindrical cooling hole,
studies on the ﬂow ﬁelds of fan-shaped cooling holes have been
very rare, therefore, comprehensive research elucidating the ﬂow
structures and their effects on the mixing process in the crossﬂow and ﬁlm-cooling performance of the shaped holes is needed.
Although, the transient density gradient, transient pressure ﬂuctuations, and velocity propagations in the time/space domains within
a fan-shape hole geometry are important factors in characterization of unsteady behaviors caused by interactions of jet and main
stream ﬂows, to the best of authors knowledge, there has been
almost no research to address them. Moreover, the authors have
introduced the unsteady ﬂow pulsations along the cooling hole as
the main source of the unsteadiness in the mixing region, which
was not found in the literature.
In this paper, the numerical simulation was conducted in order to predict the ﬁlm-cooling process of a cross-ﬂow through
a fan-shaped hole. Therefore, the three-dimensional compressible
LES approach in a fan-shape hole for a ﬂat plate was conducted
using commercial software ANSYS CFX V18.0. Additionally, to the
best of our knowledge, SAS and DES numerical simulations for a
fan-shaped hole were also carried out for the ﬁrst time in order to
evaluate the abilities of the SAS and DES methods to predict the
ﬁlm-cooling effectiveness and compared the results with the data
predicted by the LES. The computational results were validated by
the measurements in terms of the ﬁlm-cooling effectiveness on the
ﬂat plate, as well as the time-averaged ﬁlm-cooling performance
in the lateral direction for two different blowing ratios. Furthermore, the shape and conﬁguration of the CRVP and hairpin vortices
formed by the interaction of the jet and cross-ﬂow were predicted

2. Numerical method
2.1. Test setup
The test case is a 35-10-10 fan-shaped hole which is designed and tested by the Heat and Flow Control Laboratory, Korea
Aerospace University, Republic of Korea. Fig. 1 shows the details of
the laidback fan-shaped cooling holes used in the current study.
The cooling hole is inclined at an angle of 35 degrees with respect to the mainstream ﬂow, and the diameter of coolant hole, D,
is 1.5 mm. Table 1 summarizes the speciﬁcations and dimensions
of the fan-shaped hole. The forward expansion angle in each direction is 10 degrees, as is the lateral expansion angle. This expansion
reduces the momentum of the cooling jet, and the jet ﬂow is attached to the ﬂat plate surface, which leads to lower aerodynamic
losses and greater lateral coverage of the surface. The metering
section length is set at L m / D = 3 and the length of the forward
section is L f wd / D = 6. The ﬁlm-cooling effectiveness at the plate
surface is measured using the pressure-sensitive paint technique.

A high-resolution scheme is applied for discretization of the
convection terms [35] due to the reduction of the numerical diffusion error. For time discretization, the second-order backward
Euler scheme is adopted. A converged RANS solution is applied as
an initial data for the unsteady LES simulations in order to reduce the calculation cost. The k −  turbulence closure is chosen
to conduct steady simulation based on its performance and good

solution when the residual of the primary variables is less than
10−5 and the temperature ﬂuctuations at the monitoring points located in the main ﬂow after the coolant hole remain unchanged for
1000 iterations. For unsteady simulations, the proper time-step is
an essential factor for resolving an accurate solution of the turbulent scales, as well as considering the limitations of computational
resources. A time step of 1.5 × 10−6 (t = 0.02D /U ∞ ) is selected

numerical simulation for each LES simulation is about 550 h.
2.4. Turbulence model validation
The appropriate turbulence closure is a crucial factor in numerical simulations of the ﬁlm-cooling process, particularly for
the fan-shaped cooling holes. In order to obtain accurate and reasonable data from the computational simulations, various typical
turbulence models, speciﬁcally the SAS, DES, and LES models, are
used for blowing ratio M = 1. The signiﬁcance of the SAS approach
is found on the inclusion of the extra term in the turbulence model
equations, and this extra term is a function of the von Karman
length-scale [37,38]. Hence, the URANS SAS approach can be used
to resolve the turbulent structures in unsteady zones in the same
way as the LES model, and simultaneously behaves like the common RANS model in the steady zones.
DES is another modiﬁed hybrid RANS/LES turbulence model
which has recently been used in a wide range of industrial and
scientiﬁc applications [39]. In the DES approach, a RANS model is
activated within the stable and moderate separated ﬂows, and the
LES equations are applied wherever the turbulent length-scale is
larger than the grid spacing. Although the DES turbulence model
has in numerous merits, there are some issues regarding the identiﬁcation of various RANS and LES zones during the numerical
simulation. Fig. 5 compares the time-averaged ﬁlm-cooling effectiveness contour on the surface of the ﬂat plate for the computational solutions of three various turbulence closures and the
corresponding experimental results at M = 1. The adiabatic ﬁlmcooling effectiveness, an indicator of temperature reduction by the

4. Conclusions
The current study reveals detailed information on the ﬂow
structures and thermal characteristics of a fan-shaped laid-back
hole located on a ﬂat plate. Three-dimensional large eddy simulations using the WALE subgrid model were numerically conducted
in order to predict the unsteady ﬂow behaviors within the cooling
hole and main channel, as well as to analyze the ﬁlm-cooling effectiveness at a constant density ratio of 2, and two different blowing
ratios M = 1 and 2.
The LES model predicts the ﬁlm-cooling performance distributions on the surface of the ﬂat plate well as compared to
those of the experimental data measured by the pressure-sensitive
paint technique. Moreover, the results of the lateral-averaged ﬁlmcooling effectiveness investigated by the LES approach are consistent with the measurement results for two different blowing ratios,
which conﬁrmed the accuracy of the numerical model. However,
the ﬁlm-cooling effectiveness was over predicted by the other hybrid RANS/LES models, such as the SAS and DES approaches.
The time-averaged ﬁlm-cooling distributions showed that the
spread of cool air in the lateral directions was highly dependent
on the blowing ratio, and by increasing the blowing ratio, the lateral ﬁlm-coverage was wider in the spanwise direction and the
coolant was distributed more uniformly. The vorticity contour at
various cross-sectional planes along the cooling hole showed the
formation of contra-rotating vortices on the leeward side of the
coolant hole, and were developed and convected toward the exit
hole. These vortical structures are important, especially as a source
of CRVPs in the mixing region.
The instantaneous transient data conﬁrmed the ﬂow unsteadiness within the cooling hole and mainstream channel. The unsteady vorticity distribution in the streamwise direction showed
the formation of separated kidney and anti-kidney vortices near
the hole exit. The anti-kidney vortices are also observed at downstream in the main passage which are generated by the induced
ﬂow. As the blowing ratio increases, the sizes and magnitudes of
these vortical structures are changed and generate a highly complex and unsteady ﬂow behaviors. In addition, by increasing the
blowing ratio, the number of hairpin vortices in the spanwise direction was also increased, and they became tighter than those of
the lower blowing ratio.
The time sequence of the instantaneous iso-surface of Q cr
clearly displayed the formation, evolution, convection, and dissipation of vortical structures from the inlet of the cooling hole toward
the outlet of the mainstream channel. The time sequence of the
pressure gradient contour in the mid-span plane conﬁrmed the
ﬂow unsteadiness, which is periodically generated at the cooling

15

hole inlet and convected toward the cooling hole. These instances
of ﬂow unsteadiness at the hole exit are mixed with mainstream
ﬂow, and this interaction leads to a pulsating behavior due to
the Kelvin-Helmholtz instabilities in the shear layer. Moreover, for
higher blowing ratios, the period of this periodic ﬂow instability
was decreased due to the higher jet velocity.
The time/space distribution of transient velocity in the streamwise direction in the main channel near the ﬂat plate indicated
that the jet velocity waves mainly developed near the exit hole.
The slope of the jet velocity waves shows the convective velocity,
and these waves continuously travel to the downstream, and the
magnitude is reduced due to the diffusion process and the breakage of the hairpin vortices. Higher magnitudes of jet velocity waves
with smaller periods were observed for blowing ratio M = 2.
The pressure ﬂuctuation captured at various monitoring points
indicated periodic transient behavior. The spectrum of pressure
ﬂuctuation predicted dominant frequencies at 4.4 kHz and 8.8 kHz
for blowing ratios 1 and 2, respectively. In addition, comparing the
-7/3 Kolmogorov law and the computed pressure spectra yielded
that the present LES model can well predict the turbulence structures.
