USE OF THE ADIABATIC WALL TEMPERATURE IN FILM COOLING TO PREDICT
WALL HEAT FLUX AND TEMPERATURE
Katharine L. Harrison and David G. Bogard
Department of Mechanical Engineering
University of Texas at Austin, Austin, Texas 78712, USA
ABSTRACT
The adiabatic wall temperature is generally assumed to be the
driving temperature for heat transfer into conducting gas turbine
airfoils. This assumption was analyzed through a series of FLUENT
simulations using the standard k-ω turbulence model. Adiabatic
effectiveness and heat transfer experiments commonly documented in
literature were mimicked computationally. The results were then used
to predict both the heat flux and temperature distributions on a
conducting flat plate wall and the predictions were compared to the
heat flux and temperature distributions found through a flat plate
conjugate heat transfer simulation. The heat flux analysis was
compared to previously published work using the realizable k-ε
turbulence model. The same conclusions could be drawn for both
turbulence models despite differences in simulated adiabatic
effectiveness and heat transfer coefficient distributions. Agreement
between heat flux predictions and the heat flux from the conjugate
simulations correlated well with how closely the adiabatic wall
temperature approximated the over-riding gas driving temperature for
heat transfer into the wall. In general, the driving temperature for heat
transfer was represented well by the adiabatic wall temperature and the
heat flux was well predicted. However, in some locations, the heat flux
was over-predicted by up to 300%. Since wall temperature is
ultimately the parameter of interest for industrial gas turbine design,
the conducting flat plate temperature distribution was also predicted.
This was done by using the adiabatic effectiveness and heat transfer
coefficients found with the standard k-ω turbulence model as boundary
conditions in a three dimensional solid conduction simulation. Then
metal temperatures predicted in the solid conduction simulation were
compared to those found through conjugate analysis. Despite
deviations in predicted heat flux and the conjugate model heat flux of
up to 300%, deviations in the predicted and the conjugate model nondimensional metal temperatures were less than 10%. Thus, use of the
adiabatic wall temperature as the driving temperature for heat transfer
to predict temperature on the surface of a conducting wall results in
relatively small errors.
INTRODUCTION
Film cooling performance for gas turbine airfoils is typically
evaluated through measurements of the adiabatic effectiveness, η, and
heat transfer coefficient augmentation, hf /h0, in which hf is the heat
transfer coefficient with film cooling and h0 is the heat transfer
coefficient without film cooling. Near adiabatic models are typically
used in film cooling experiments to determine the temperature
distribution measured on an adiabatic wall, Taw. The adiabatic wall

temperature is generally presented in its normalized form, η, as
defined in the nomenclature. These parameters, η and hf/h0, are
important because it is commonly assumed that they can be used to
predict the heat flux into a film cooled airfoil wall, as expressed in the
following equation:

q′′ = h f (Taw − Tw )

(1)

where T w is the surface temperature for a conducting airfoil wall and
q” is heat flux. It is presumed in using Equation (1) that Taw is the
over-riding gas temperature that drives heat transfer into the surface.
The effects of this presumption on predicting the temperature and heat
flux for a conducting flat plate is analyzed in the current study through
a series of computational simulations.
Goldstein [1] proposed that Taw in Equation (1) is the appropriate
driving temperature potential for heat transfer, rather than the
mainstream temperature, T∞, which is used traditionally in convective
heat transfer. The rationale behind using Taw as the driving temperature
for heat transfer is that the heat transfer coefficient is then independent
of the coolant temperature, Tc. Consequently, hf is defined by Equation
(1). The heat flux could always be predicted correctly using Taw if hf
values were determined using measurements of the q˝, Taw, and Tw, for
a film cooled metal airfoil. However, hf values are generally measured
experimentally at unity density ratio, DR = 1, so that Tc is equal to T∞.
In this case, determination of hf is simplified because Taw is equal to T∞
or the recovery temperature, Tr, in the case of compressible flows. By
measuring hf at DR = 1, it is presumed that Equation (1) is insensitive
to Tc. In experiments to determine hf, the wall is generally heated with
a constant heat flux source and the surface temperature distribution is
measured. Then hf is calculated as indicated by Equation (1). At DR =
1 for which Taw = Tr, there are no variations in the thermal field due to
temperature difference between the coolant jet and T∞; variations in
the thermal field in the gas flow are due only to the heat transfer from
the wall. This situation is quite different from actual turbine conditions
in which the coolant jet is at a much lower temperature than T∞ which
leads to a highly non-uniform temperature field in the gas flow above
the wall.
It is important to recognize that a thermal boundary layer exists
over the wall in an actual turbine airfoil due to the conduction of heat
into the wall. The thickness of the thermal boundary layer may be thin
or thick relative to the coolant jet thickness depending on the
development length of the boundary layer. How this is relevant to the
use of Taw as the driving temperature potential for a film cooling flow
is demonstrated by three hypothetical temperature distributions for an

An analytical evaluation of different methods for determining the
heat flux into the wall was done by Choe et al. [2] using superposition
and hypothetical constant temperature test surfaces. They showed that
the normalized adiabatic wall temperature, η, determined using an
adiabatic wall would not be expected to be the same as that determined
using a constant temperature conducting test surface.
Harrison and Bogard [3] analyzed the assumption that Taw is the
driving temperature for heat transfer in film cooling experiments
through a series of simulations in FLUENT. The realizable k-ε (RKE)
turbulent model was used to simulate film cooling on a flat plate. The
RKE model was employed for its relative ease of use and its
prevalence in literature as a first step in analyzing whether Taw was the
correct driving temperature. Then a detailed turbulence model
comparison was conducted by Harrison and Bogard [4] to determine
which model could best predict experimental η and hf /h0. The RKE,
standard k-ω (SKW), and Reynold’s stress (RSM) turbulence models
were considered. The SKW model best simulated experimentally
measured laterally averaged adiabatic effectiveness, η , but resulted
in the worst centerline predictions. Conversely, the RKE model
provided the worst η predictions but the best predicted centerline η
and also matched correlations for h0 better than the RSM or SKW. Use

of the RSM model resulted in the best agreement with measured
centerline hf /h0. All three turbulence models predicted laterally
averaged hf / h0 with similar accuracy.
From the results in Harrison and Bogard [4], it is difficult to
choose the most appropriate model for studying film cooling. In the
current study, the SKW model was chosen because it showed the best
η agreement and equally as good agreement with experimental
measurements of laterally averaged hf /h0 values as the other turbulent
models. The current study aims to analyze whether Taw is the correct
driving temperature for heat transfer through simulations to find the η,
hf / h0, and the overall effectiveness, φ. When Taw and heat transfer
coefficient augmentation found through experiments are used as a
boundary condition for models of turbines, laterally averaged values
are most likely applied. Thus, it was deemed most important to
accurately simulate laterally averaged values and the SKW model was
selected for this reason to use in the current study.
In Harrison and Bogard [3], the validity of using Taw as the
driving temperature for heat transfer was analyzed by predicting the
heat flux into a film cooled wall using computed values for Taw and hf
/h0. The predicted heat fluxes were compared to heat fluxes found
from a conjugate heat transfer conducting model. Then thermal
profiles above the adiabatic and conducting walls were analyzed to
determine how closely the driving temperature for heat transfer was
approximated by Taw. Harrison and Bogard [3] determined that using
Taw as the driving temperature for heat transfer generally resulted in
good predictions of the heat flux into the conducting flat plate wall.
However, there were regions where the heat flux predictions were poor
and in these regions Taw was found to poorly approximate the driving
temperature for heat transfer. In the current study, the heat flux
analysis is repeated using the SKW turbulence model and the results
are compared to the results using the RKE model. As will be shown,
similar conclusions can be drawn using either turbulence model
despite the differences in predicted values of η and hf /h0 using the two
models, as shown in Harrison and Bogard [4]. It should be noted that
Harrison and Bogard [3] used a slightly different mesh and the effects
of viscous heating were not included in the study.
Harrison and Bogard [3] evaluated only the effect of using Taw as
the driving temperature for heat transfer on predicted heat flux
distributions on the flat plate. This is useful to an extent, but the
parameter of interest for turbine engineers is the wall temperature
distribution rather than the heat flux. Also, poor predictions of local
wall temperature do not necessarily follow from poor predictions of
heat flux. When analyzing conducting walls, large differences between
predicted and conducting wall heat flux are likely to be smeared out
when examining the predicted temperature distributions. Thus, even
where heat flux predictions are poor, temperature predictions may not
be poor. The main focus of the current study was to extend the heat
flux analysis to determine how using Taw as the driving temperature for
prediction of heat transfer affects the temperature distribution
predictions on a conducting flat plate. This was achieved through
simulations using a solid conducting model with Taw and hf /h0 as
boundary conditions to predict the overall effectiveness, φ (defined in
the nomenclature).

COMPUTATIONAL SIMULATIONS AND METHODS
A series of simulations were run to predict the heat flux and
temperature into a conducting, film cooled wall using Taw as the
driving temperature for heat transfer. The simulations were based on
the flat plate film cooling geometry in Baldauf et al. [5] and was
meshed using GAMBIT (part of the FLUENT package). The
dimensions and a schematic of the geometry are given in Fig. 2.
Pertinent flow variables are summarized in Table 1. In all simulations,
incompressible flow was simulated but the properties of air were set to
vary as either quadratic or linear functions of temperature since there
was considerable density variation in the simulations. At the inlet to
the tunnel, a velocity profile with a mainstream velocity of U = 60 m/s
was applied to match the displacement thickness of δ1/d = 0.1 at the
hole reported in Baldauf et al. [5]. At the tunnel inlet, the turbulence
intensity was matched to that measured in Baldauf et al. [5], but since
the integral length scale was not indicated, Λ was set to one hole
diameter. At the plenum inlet, the turbulence intensity was set to an
arbitrary value of 2%, and the length scale was again set to Λ = 1d.
A hexahedral mesh was created in which half of one hole was
meshed, and symmetry boundary conditions were placed on all
surfaces at planes defined by the centerline and half pitch distance
from the hole. To prevent a boundary layer from forming at the top of
the tunnel, a symmetry boundary condition was applied. In all
simulations, adiabatic wall boundary conditions were applied at the
upstream and downstream ends of the flat plate walls and the plenum

To mimic experiments to find η, another simulation was run with
all wall boundary conditions set to be adiabatic. This simulation was
used to determine the temperature on the film cooled adiabatic flat
plate wall, Taw, and will be referred to as the “AW” simulation.
Experiments to determine heat transfer coefficients were also
mimicked computationally by setting the boundary condition on the
surface of the flat plate to a constant heat flux of q” = 15 kW/m2. This
value was chosen because it was approximately equal to the average
q” found upstream of the holes into the flat plate wall in the CW
simulation. The heat transfer simulations were run with and without
film cooling to determine hf and h0 respectively, and these simulations
will be referred to as the “HF” and “HF0” simulations. Both hf and h0
simulations were run so that the hf /h0 augmentation factor could be
determined since hf /h0 is the parameter generally reported in film
cooling experiments. The heat transfer coefficients were calculated as
shown in the nomenclature.
The AW, HF, HF0, and CW simulations were run to determine
how well Taw and hf /h0, found by mimicking common experiments,
could be used to predict q”cond, the heat flux into the conducting wall.
In order to predict q” using Equation (1), an hf value must be
determined. However, heat transfer coefficient augmentation, hf /h0, is
the parameter available in literature rather than hf alone. Because of
this, to determine hf, hf /h0 must be multiplied by the heat transfer
coefficient that would exist on the actual airfoil without film cooling,
designated as h0,airfoil. The h0,airfoil distribution used in this study was
the heat transfer coefficient for the simulated conducting flat plate
without film cooling and will be designated h0,cond. The conducting
wall surface condition is neither a constant temperature nor a constant
heat flux. An additional conjugate analysis simulation without film
cooling, referred to as CW0, was therefore necessary to obtain these
h0,cond values. The boundary conditions applied for the CW0 simulation
were similar to those for the CW simulation except no film cooling
was simulated. The calculation to determine h0,cond is shown in the
nomenclature to be similar to that for h0, but the q” is found through
conjugate analysis in the CW0 simulation and is a constant applied
heat flux boundary condition in the HF0 simulation.

The validity of using Taw and hf /h0 to predict film cooling
performance was also evaluated by determining φ, sometimes referred
to as the “metal temperature.” A solid conduction simulation, denoted
“SC,” was run such that only the solid plate was modeled with the
fluid regions deleted from the model shown in Fig. 2. In the SC
simulation, Taw and (hf /h0)*h0,cond were used directly as boundary
conditions on the exterior surface of the solid flat plate. The internal
heat fluxes applied to the bottom of the flat plate and the heat fluxes
inside the hole were set equal to those in the CW simulation. The
boundary conditions for the SC simulation on the top of the plate were
applied in two ways. First, Taw and (hf /h0)*h0,cond were calculated at
each cell along the flat plate and these local Taw and (hf /h0)*h0,cond

averaged Taw and (hf/h0)*h0,cond values were calculated and the laterally
averaged values were applied as boundary conditions as a function of
position along the plate. This was done because laterally averaged
values are more commonly used by industrial designers to predict
metal temperature.
The temperature distribution on the flat plate, Tpredicted, was found
through the SC simulation and compared to the temperature
distribution, Tcond, found from the CW simulation. The solid
conducting model simulation evaluates the accuracy of using Taw and
hf /h0 data available in literature to predict φ, a normalized Tcond as
defined in the nomenclature. This is a more useful tool for determining
how well Taw can be used to predict the driving temperature for heat
transfer into a conducting wall because temperature is ultimately the
parameter of interest for industrial turbine designers.

The effects on hf /h0 of heating the region upstream of the hole are
also important to consider and have been examined experimentally.
Mayhew et al. [6] measured hf /h0 values with upstream heating that
were 5-10% higher than other values in literature and attributed this
effect to upstream heating. Coulthard et al. [7] compared flat plate hf
/h0 values with and without upstream heating and found upstream
heated hf /h0 values to be up to 25% higher than those without
upstream heating. The largest effects were seen at low M near the hole.
By x/d = 6, the hf /h0 distributions with and without upstream heating
differed by less than 10%. The effects of upstream heating have also
been studied on a model turbine vane by Harrison et al. [8]. For a
turbulent approach flow, hf /h0 was up to 15% higher with upstream
heating than without near the hole for all M. By x/d = 5, the hf /h0
results were less than 10% higher with upstream heating than without.
Because upstream heating can significantly affect hf /h0 and real
turbines have an established thermal boundary layer upstream of the
film cooling holes, only simulations with upstream heating are
discussed in this paper.

In film cooling literature, Taw is assumed to be the driving
temperature for heat transfer into a conducting wall, but other
assumptions are made as well that should be addressed. Specifically, hf
values are typically found using a constant heat flux, DR = 1, and no
upstream heating. This is not realistic because real airfoils are more
likely to be closer to constant temperature surfaces than constant heat
flux, operate at much higher DR, and a thermal boundary layer is
established upstream of the coolant holes. The error in determining hf
/h0 using a constant heat flux surface is most likely small, since
turbulent flow correlations for h0 on a flat plate with constant
temperature and constant heat flux boundary conditions are only about
5% different. This is reiterated by Fig. 5, in which h0 with upstream
heating is compared to h0,cond found through conjugate analysis, for

for the heat transfer simulations are fairly similar. The figure shows a
small increase in laterally averaged hf /h0 with density ratio.

HEAT FLUX AND TEMPERATURE PREDICTIONS USING Taw
The focus of this paper is to determine how well employing Taw
as the driving temperature for heat transfer into a conducting wall can
be used to predict the heat flux into and temperature of a conducting
wall. The assumption that Taw is the correct driving temperature is
dependent on the size of the thermal boundary layer in relation to that
of the coolant jet. Thus, it is useful to examine thermal profiles of the
static fluid temperature above the wall to analyze thermal boundary
layer development. Fig. 7 shows thermal profiles of non-dimensional
static temperature, θ, above the adiabatic and conducting walls at x/d =
5 for M = 0.5. The shapes of the thermal profiles above the adiabatic
and conducting walls are very similar. The conducting wall increases
the temperature of the core of the jet slightly (decreased θ). This is
because along the centerline and close to the holes, the conducting
wall is warmer than the coolant jet. Heat is being driven out of the wall

Taw approximates the over-riding gas temperature well, the heat flux is
well predicted by using Taw. Conversely, when Taw poorly
approximates the driving temperature for heat transfer, the heat flux is
poorly predicted. Furthermore, the fact that both the RKE and SKW
turbulence models can be used to reach the same conclusion shows
that the results of this study do not depend on the turbulence model.
Thus, it should be irrelevant that neither of these turbulence models
quantitatively predict η and hf /h0 distributions because the turbulent
models simply provide examples of different thermal fields that could
exist above the wall. What this analysis illustrates is that in regions
where the gas temperature varies strongly above the wall, Taw cannot
be used effectively as the driving temperature for heat transfer in
predictions of heat flux to a conducting wall. It is clear from Fig. 14
that the core of the jet is separated from the wall and the thermal field
above the wall varies significantly from Taw for M = 1 at this location.
Regardless of whether the turbulence models can predict the exact η
and hf/h0 values for M = 1, jet separation is a physical situation that
exists on real airfoils. This study suggests that in regions affected by
jet separation, Taw is not the driving temperature for heat transfer. The
same conclusion applies to any situation that leads to strong variation
in the thermal field above the wall.
It should be noted, however, that although the heat flux is not
well predicted at certain locations examined in this study, in general
Taw is found to be a very good approximation of the driving
temperature for heat transfer.

Analysis of the predicted heat flux is important for understanding
when Taw is a good approximation of the driving temperature for heat
transfer into a gas turbine airfoil. However, the heat flux analysis does
not give an indication of how assuming Taw to be the driving
temperature for heat transfer affects the temperature distribution on the
surface. For gas turbine airfoils, the surface temperature is really the
parameter of interest. Despite that the heat flux is not well predicted at
certain locations when using Taw as the driving temperature for heat
transfer, large over/under-predictions in heat flux do not necessarily
extend to large over/under-predictions in the temperature distribution.

CONCLUSIONS
In engines, even small differences in surface temperature can
significantly affect the life of airfoils, so even small differences in the
predicted surface temperature may be important. The adiabatic wall
temperature is generally assumed to be the driving temperature for
heat transfer into a conducting wall in film cooling literature. This
assumption has not been studied in open literature except in Harrison
and Bogard [3]. In contrast to the earlier study, which evaluated

10

Copyright © 2008 by ASME

performance in terms of the actual and predicted heat flux to the
surface, the focus of this study was to determine the accuracy of the
solid body temperature predicted with the standard process of using
adiabatic wall temperature and heat transfer coefficient as boundary
conditions for a solid body conduction analysis. This work also
complements the earlier study by showing that the use of the SKW
turbulence model in the current study leads to similar conclusions as
were found previously using the RKE model.
The assumption that the adiabatic wall temperature is the driving
temperature for heat transfer into a conducting flat plate was analyzed
through a series of simulations. These simulations computationally
mimicked standard experiments to find the adiabatic wall temperature
and heat transfer coefficient augmentation on a flat plate. The results
of the simulations were then used to predict the heat flux and wall
temperature of a conducting wall with the same geometry.
The predicted and actual heat fluxes were compared and
reasonable agreement was found. However, in some regions, generally
along the centerline, there was as much as a 300% difference between
the predicted and actual heat fluxes. In these regions of poor heat flux
predictions, the over-riding gas temperatures were invariably found to
be not well represented by the adiabatic wall temperature. When
agreement between the predicted and actual heat fluxes was found to
be good, the thermal profiles indicated that the adiabatic wall
temperature was a good approximation of the driving temperature for
heat transfer.
The temperature on the flat plate was also predicted by using the
adiabatic wall temperature and heat transfer coefficients as boundary
conditions for the top surface of the plate in a simulation of the
thermal conduction within a solid model. This was a simulation of
common design practice to estimate “metal temperature” using the
film cooling adiabatic effectiveness and heat transfer coefficients as
boundary conditions for finite element analysis of thermal conduction
within the solid body of a turbine airfoil. This simulation was done
with local and laterally averaged values of adiabatic effectiveness and
heat transfer coefficients. Results showed less than 10% error in the
predicted surface temperature even in regions where the heat flux
predictions were in error by as much as 300%. Not surprisingly, use of
laterally averaged adiabatic film effectiveness and heat transfer
coefficients resulted in laterally uniform temperature distributions, and
therefore did not reveal the actual lateral variations of wall
temperature. However, the actual lateral variations in wall temperature
were not large, so this did not result in large errors. Overall, use of the
adiabatic wall temperature as the driving temperature for heat transfer
resulted in reasonably accurate predictions of the surface heat flux and
surface temperature distributions on a conducting wall.
