Abstract
This paper presents a detailed analysis of the flow topologies and turbulence scales in the jet-in-cross-flow
experiment of Su and Mungal [Su and Mungal JFM 2004]. The analysis is performed using the Large
Eddy Simulation (LES) technique with a highly resolved grid and time-step, and well controlled boundary
conditions. This enables quantitative agreement with the first and second moments of turbulence statistics
measured in the experiment. LES is used to perform the analysis since experimental measurements of
time-resolved 3D fields is still in its infancy, and because sampling periods are generally limited with Direct
Numerical Simulation. A major focal point is the comprehensive characterization of the turbulence scales
and their evolution. Time-resolved probes are used with long sampling periods to obtain maps of the integral
scales, Taylor microscales, and turbulent kinetic energy spectra. Scalar-fluctuation scales are also quantified.
In the near-field, coherent structures are clearly identified, both in physical and spectral space. Along the
jet centerline, turbulence scales grow according to a classical one-third power law. However, the derived
maps of turbulence scales reveal strong inhomogeneities in the flow. From the modeling perspective, these
insights are useful to design optimized grids and improve numerical predictions in similar configurations.
Keywords: Jet-In-Cross-Flow, Large Eddy Simulation, Flow Structures, Turbulence Scales

1. Introduction
Achieving a detailed understanding of turbulent mixing in a Jet-In-Cross-Flow (JICF) is valuable in
many practical situations. Applications include plume dispersion, gas turbine combustor cooling, and fuel
injection. In each of these cases, the primary technical objective is to control and optimize transport and
mixing, all of which depends on having a refined understanding and knowledge of the related mechanisms of
turbulence. For this reason, JICF configurations have been extensively studied experimentally, theoretically,
and numerically. Comprehensive reviews can be found in papers by Margason et al. [1], Karagozian [2] and
Mahesh [3]. The details of turbulent processes leading to destabilization have been investigated over a
wide range of Reynolds numbers, momentum-flux ratios, density ratios, Mach numbers, and reactivities.
However, the complexity of turbulence and the large variety of instabilities present in such systems still
requires research to develop efficient control methods.
A key area that needs to be improved is the precise characterization of turbulence scales, the mechanism
of their formation, and their evolution. This is relevant at both technical and scientific levels for the
reasons mentioned above. This information is also valuable for industry since the predictive capabilities of
engineering simulation techniques critically depends on understanding how to best resolve the key processes.
There is currently no clear methodology to quantify what optimal local grid spacings should be used to make
calculations both affordable and accurate. Knowledge of the characteristic sizes and topology of turbulence
can be used to optimally distribute the computational cells where they are needed, enabling more accurate
predictions at a lower cost.
A complete characterization of turbulence scales has not been undertaken yet in a JICF for two reasons.
First, a precise measure of scales can be achieved in experiments by placing probe clusters in the flow and
analyzing the spatial correlations of temporal signals of given quantities. However, the characterization of
the three-dimensional spatial evolution of turbulence scales is not practical using this method. Experimental
methods such as high-speed tomographic Particle Image Velocimetry (PIV) can be used, as recently detailed
in Scarano [4]. But, despite the significant progress, this method has spatial and temporal resolution
challenges and cannot be employed yet to characterize the small scales of turbulence. Second, even though
Direct Numerical Simulation (DNS) can be carried out in some JICF configurations [3], the characterization
of scales requires large computational domains and long integration times, which are still prohibitively
expensive with the computational power of today.
In the present work we have characterized the different turbulence scales present in the JICF studied
experimentally by Su and Mungal [5] employing highly-resolved Large Eddy Simulation (LES). Using the
“high-fidelity” approach, assumptions associated with boundary conditions and physics are minimized and
the spatial/temporal resolutions are selected precisely to ensure grid-independent predictions of the first
moments of turbulence. The fidelity in the solution allows one to characterize the distribution of turbulence
scales throughout the flow at a level of detail, which to-date, has not been achieved.
LES has been applied to the JICF configuration by a number of researchers. Most have been aimed at
predicting film-cooling in gas turbines [6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. These studies have improved our understanding
of turbulent mixing processes associated with a JICF. In general, good qualitative agreement between LES
results and time-averaged experimental data can be obtained. Our approach differs from previous works
through the use of fine and uniform mesh spacing in all spatial directions. This approach does not require
any a priori knowledge of the jet trajectory, and enables one to control the simulation accuracy with a
single scalar parameter. The parametric study in Section 4 then facilitates setting the grid spacing to a
value that provides grid-independent predictions of the turbulence statistics. The level of resolution applied
is significantly higher than in previous studies (e.g. see Appendix. B). Other studies have also had to deal
with many degrees of freedom related to local cell-size distribution and local stretching in complex threedimensional flow fields. In these studies, the mesh is generally designed through trial-and-error, until a
sufficient agreement with experimental results is obtained. However it is not clear if the local resolutions
applied are adequate in the spatially evolving field downstream of the jet.
The paper is organized as follows. The target JICF configuration is described in Section 2 along with
respective operating and boundary conditions. In Section 3, a description of the LES solver, filtered conservation equations, and subgrid-scale (SGS) models employed are presented. In Section 4, we explain how

2. Description of geometry, operating, and boundary conditions

Figure 1: Schematic of geometry and orientation of the computational domain.

The present simulations focus on the experiment conducted by Su and Mungal [5]. The experiment is
performed at atmospheric pressure and ambient temperature in an updraft wind tunnel with air as the crossflow fluid and nitrogen mixed with acetone as the jet fluid. The cross-flow is laminar upstream of the jet,
with a peak velocity of v∞ = 2.96 m/s. The diameter of the jet nozzle is d = 4.53 mm and the length of the
injection pipe is 70 d, which is long enough for a fully developed turbulent boundary layer to form. The bulk
jet velocity is uj = 16.9 m/s, giving a velocity ratio r = 5.7 and a jet Reynolds number of 5000. The jet fluid
is seeded with approximately 10% acetone by volume for diagnostic purposes. To seed the acetone vapor
into the jet flow, the jet fluid is passed through a bubbler containing liquid acetone. During the experimental
runs, the acetone level in the bubbler, and the seeding efficiency, “slowly diminishes” [5]. Even though the
post-processing procedure of raw PLIF signals accounts for this concentration drift, the penetration of the jet
might be sensitive to this drift through small changes in momentum-flux ratio. This issue will be addressed
further in Section 5.2. For additional details on the experimental setup, see Su and Mungal [5].
Figure 1 shows the geometry and the orientation of the computational domain. The grid spacing is
constant everywhere in both the chamber and nozzle. Use of a constant grid spacing facilitates a clear
understanding of the effects of grid resolution and removes ambiguities related to local stretching. The
spatial extent of the computational domain in the x-y plane is chosen to allow comparisons of the LES
results to available experimental data. The z spatial extent has been determined through trial-and-error to
prevent confinement of the jet and allow the development of the counter-rotating vortex-pair, which drives
far-field mixing and jet penetration.

3. Theoretical and numerical framework
LES is performed using a code framework called RAPTOR, which is a fully compressible solver that
has been optimized to meet the strict algorithmic requirements imposed by the LES formalism. RAPTOR
uses non-dissipative, discretely conservative, staggered, finite-volume differencing. Temporal integration is
performed using a dual-time multistage scheme that allows one to set the physical time-step based solely
on accuracy considerations. Details related to the baseline formulation and SGS models are given by
Oefelein [16]. Representative case studies are given by Oefelein et al. [17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23].

speed, Uref , and fluid state characterized by a reference density, ρref , sound speed, cref , constant pressure
specific heat, Cp ref , and dynamic viscosity, µref . Using these quantities, reference Mach and Reynolds
numbers are defined as M = Uref /cref and Re = ρref Uref δref /µref . Using these definitions, the filtered
conservation equations of mass, momentum, total-energy and chemical species can be written in conservative
form as follows:

4. Computational domain
In this section, the procedure to determine the computational domain (grid resolution and domain size)
is detailed.
4.1. Grid resolution
First, the LES filter size that ensures grid-independent statistics in the near-field is determined. A
small domain of size (Lx /d, Ly /d, Lz /d) = (23, 13, 6), focusing on the near-field, have been used and a
parametric study on the filter width size has been conducted. The mesh spacing is progressively refined
with an increasing number of cells, denoted “n”, within the reference length scale d (i.e., the jet diameter).
Four different levels of resolution have been considered: n = 5 (d05), n = 10 (d10), n = 15 (d15), and
n = 20 (d20), which corresponds to grids containing 0.6-million, 3-million, 8.5-million and 18-million cells,
respectively. Integration times steps have been adjusted to the spatial resolution so that a unity convective
CFL number is maintained. This ensures accurate temporal discretization for all simulations. The jet
nozzle is included in the simulations using the immersed boundary technique, which facilitates the use of
the same spatial resolution as in the chamber and removes meshing difficulties. All of the simulations have
been initialized using the same initial solution. The jet nozzle flow is initially at rest, and the inlet velocity
profile of the cross-flow air is extruded in the channel. Acetone mass fraction and jet velocity are slowly
ramped-up as the jet enters the cross-flow. The time-averaging period is equal to 200 d/uj for all cases,
which approximately corresponds to 40 flow time through the near field region (y/d <= 5). Before recording
statistics, a flushing period of 30 d/uj has been used, which was sufficient to establish the flow field in this
region. Note that this reduced domain produces some confinement of the jet in the span-wise direction and
is only used for the grid convergence analysis.

Figure 4 shows the mean and RMS fields of concentration at a transverse cut of y/d = 2.5, which is
in the near-field region after jet turbulence breaks down. This plane is used to monitor mesh convergence
since it contains the footprint of resolved coherent structures and developing turbulence, which are highly
sensitive to grid resolution at this location. Farther downstream, turbulent flow structures grow and the
deviations between mesh resolutions becomes diminished. At the two highest resolutions (d15 and d20),
classical kidney-shape structures are observed on the mean and RMS concentration fields. At the two lowest
resolutions, however, the turbulent mixing in the near-field is not predicted accurately. Other statistics (mean
and RMS of velocity and turbulent fluxes) show the same trends. Statistical results are not significantly
different between the d15 and d20 cases, which indicates grid convergence associated with these quantities is
achieved using a filter width of dx ≤ d/15. Note that for the d10 case, the non-symmetrical shape observed
in Figure 4 is not cause by too short of an averaging time. It is caused by a bifurcation phenomenon also
observed experimentally at low Reynolds number [35].
4.2. Domain size
In addition to ensuring the statistics of interest are grid independent, it is necessary to ensure that the
computational domain is free from the influence of solid boundaries. This was done by performing a coarse
simulation with a mesh spacing dx = d/5 in a large domain, denoted Dl . The dimensions of this domain are
(Lx /d, Ly /d, Lz /d) = (60, 46, 40). Statistics have been converged up to y/d = 25, which is approximately
10 diameters downstream of the end of the interrogation window in Su and Mungal [5]. The extent of the
domain was then decreased using a visual margin of 3d around the large counter-rotating vortex-pair at
y/d = 25, as shown in Fig. 5a. This new domain, D, has the dimensions (Lx /d, Ly /d, Lz /d) = (33, 33, 40).
The solution from the large domain, Dl , was then interpolated on the smaller domain, D, and the statistics
were compared. Figure 5b shows the mean concentration along one-dimensional cuts through the jet. The
time-averaging period is equal to 300 d/uj for both cases, which approximately correspond to 10 flow time
through the domain (y/d <= 27). Before recording statistics in the domain D, a flushing period of 100 d/uj
has been used.
The mean concentration field is qualitatively similar in both cases and the minor deviations are only due
to the finite averaging time used. This confirms that, even in the far-field, the jet is not being influenced by
the side boundaries of the computational domain.

5. Validation with experimental data
In this section, a detailed comparison of the LES results with available experimental data is conducted.
The goal is to demonstrate that turbulence is accurately resolved by the present simulation before analyzing
the detailed flow characteristics in the sections that follow.
5.1. Two-dimensional cross-sections
Figure 6 provides a comparison between instantaneous concentration fields obtained from LES and the
experiment. Fig. 6a shows the flow in the jet center plane (z/d = 0). Fig. 6b-d show the flow in multiple
off-center planes at (b) z = 1.25d, (c) z = 2.56d and (d) z = 3.82d. In Figure 6a, one can observe (in both
the experiment and LES) disturbances in the jet shear-layer in the first few diameters of the jet nozzle exit.
These disturbances are induced by span-wise rollers [6] that develop due to the Kelvin-Helmholtz instability.
The three-dimensional topology of these structures is shown in Section 6. In the immediate wake of the jet,
pockets of jet fluid are separated from the jet core. This is closely related to the developing counter-rotating
vortex-pair in the wake of the jet, which transports fluid from the edges of the jet back into the center plane.
The three-dimensional topology of the counter-rotating vortex pair is also shown in Section 6. Further
downstream, the jet bends due to pressure forces created by the impact of the cross-flow. Disturbances at
larger scales are visible on the outer edge of the jet in both the LES and the experiment. This is related to
the engulfment of air into the jet, and is one of the mechanisms which enhances entrainment relative to an
isolated turbulent round jet [6, 2].
In the off-center planes (see Figs. 6b-d), the formation of isolated pockets is more frequent. These
pockets contain well-mixed fluid, which is qualitatively similar to what is described as a “cliff” and “plateau”

Figure 8 shows the time-averaged scalar concentration profiles in the jet center plane. Figure 8a shows
that at x = 0.57d, the jet potential core is still present and a flat profile of concentration is observed. At
x = 2.85d, which corresponds to the end of the jet core (see Fig. 7), the concentration profile starts to
spread and the maximum concentration decreases. The maximum value of concentration is C = 0.84 for
the LES and C = 0.92 for the experiment, which represents a 9% relative deviation. The location of the
peak concentration is similar in both cases (y = 0.11d). The profile is almost symmetric around the position
of maximum concentration, except that a tail is present for y > 0.5d, although it is not as pronounced in
the LES as in the experiment. It represents jet fluid that has been advected in the wake region by the
counter-rotating vortex pair, as already observed in Fig. 6a. At x = 5.7d, which is located near the TBP,
the LES accurately predicts the asymmetric concentration profile and its maximum value C ≈ 0.4 located
at y = 0.7d. The relative error between LES and experiment at this maximum value is very small (= 4%).
At x = 8.55d, which is downstream of the TBP, the maximum concentration in the LES is comparable
to the experiment. However, it appears that the jet predicted by LES penetrates less into the chamber
than the experiment. The same observation can be made with the far-field cuts shown in Fig. 8b, even
though the asymmetric shape of the concentration profiles is comparable between LES and experiment, as
well as the evolution of the maximum concentration value. The reason for this discrepancy is not totally
clear. It is unlikely that the SGS models play a significant role in this deviation because the maximum
value of turbulent viscosity in the computational domain is comparable to the molecular viscosity. In the
far-field (s ≥ 17d along the jet center streamline), the turbulent viscosity is negligible (µt ≤ µ/10). This
suggests that the high-resolution simulation presented here does not heavily rely on the SGS model, which
is consistent with the comparison of turbulence scales and grid resolution in Appendix. B. It is also possible
that experimental uncertainties associated with the acetone concentration of the jet are a factor. Although
it is estimated in Su and Mungal [5] that the jet contains approximately 10% acetone by volume, variations
in acetone content changes significantly the actual penetration of the jet, as detailed with the parametric
study performed in Appendix. C.

6. Qualitative analysis of coherent structures
Having validated the LES results against experimental data in Section 5, we now extract complementary
information not available from the experiment by analyzing the time-resolved three-dimensional concentration and velocity fields.
Figure 11 shows the volume rendering of an instantaneous enstrophy field (= Ω2 , where Ω is the vorticity)
for increasing distances from injection (y/d = 0.5, y/d = 10, y/d = 17, extending +/−1d in the y-direction).
Close to injection, at y/d = 0.5, the vorticity layer originating from the pipe forms a wavy sheet. The presence
of a sheet is due to the mean strain rate imposed by the jet velocity. The waviness is due to the fact that
the jet is ‘free’ from solid boundaries and developing turbulence deforms the sheet. Far from injection,
at y/d = 17, the flow topology is dominated by tube-like structures. In the far-field, even though the
flow is globally inhomogeneous and anisotropic, the mean strain rate is small and turbulence is developed,
which creates a qualitatively similar topology as observed in Homogeneous Isotropic Turbulence (HIT). For
a review of the latter topology, see Antonia and Orlandi [37]. In between, at y/d = 10, a hybrid flow
topology is observed with both sheet-like structures and tube-like structures. The intrinsic characteristics
of turbulence thus change drastically between the near-field and the far-field in a JICF. This figure helps
in understanding the need and challenge of quantifying turbulence scales in such a complex anisotropic and
inhomogeneous three-dimensional flow.
Experimental and numerical studies have enabled the observation of vortical structures in a JICF, e.g.,
see [38, 39, 40]. Depending on the characteristic dimensionless numbers of a particular case (Reynolds
number, velocity ratio, density ratio), certain classes of turbulent flow structures dominate others. Thus
the main turbulent flow structures in the present configuration need to be identified. To do this, the “Qcriterion” [41] is applied to the LES instantaneous flow fields. The Q field is the second invariant of the

rotating in opposite direction.
Another important class of coherent structures is generated by the growth of an azimuthal instability of
the ring-vortices. These secondary instabilities are called v-shape vortices here, and produce couples of small
counter-rotating vortex pairs with a rotational axis that is parallel to the jet axis, as shown in Fig. 15. Similar
vortices have been observed in turbulent jets experimentally by Broze and Hussain [45], and numerically
by Silva and Metais [46]. These jet-wise structures represent the first step of the dislocation of organized
Kelvin-Helmoltz instabilities. In Yuan et al. [47], evidence of the presence of these vortices in a JICF is
given as their fingerprint is observed on some components of vorticity. The high resolution of the current
computation enables us to better understand the spatial structure of these vortices. Our analysis indicates
that these vortices are responsible for ejection of jet fluid in the upstream cross-flow. This phenomenon is
illustrated in Fig. 15c and explains the convoluted interface observed in Fig. 12b at the bottom location in
the x/d = 7 plane.
The horse-shoe vortex is a known coherent structure in this type of flow. This vortex is very stable and
settles upstream of the base of the jet close to the wall. It is induced as a consequence of the adverse-pressure
gradient generated by the varying stagnation pressure of the cross-flow on the jet surface [40]. Compared
to other vortices, its circulation is weak. Its role in turbulent mixing is marginal at the present conditions.
At the wall, quasi-streamwise (with respect to the cross-flow) vortices generated in the boundary layer [48]
are disrupted by the presence of the jet and are lifted away from the wall surface, which creates hair-pin
structures. They can be observed in Fig. 12a close to the wall. At the present conditions, where there is
a large momentum-flux ratio, the jet penetrates deeply into the chamber, while these vortices remain in
the vicinity of the wall without interacting with the jet. Similarly, wake vortices are sometimes observed
but they are not dominant in the current case. This is due to the low Reynolds number associated with
the cross-flow of air, which limits vortex shedding. Finally, broadband turbulence forms downstream of the
TBP. These far-field vortices appear when the previous types of eddies have lost their regular arrangement

7. Quantitative analysis of turbulence scales
In this section, a quantitative evaluation of key turbulence scales is provided. We calculate the integral
scale, Taylor microscale, turbulence spectra, and scalar gradient thicknesses, with emphasis on understanding
the relationship between these physical scales and the relevant numerical scales. In addition to physical
insights, the ensemble of results is useful for designing optimized grids that strike a balance between cost
and precision.

7.3. Turbulence spectra
In this section, 200 time-resolved probes are analyzed. They are placed along the windward and leeward
side curves as well as along the jet center streamline. Signal processing techniques are used to study
turbulence spectra as a function of curvilinear distance from injection. The spectrum averaging procedure
of Welch [51] is used to enable a deterministic Power Spectrum Density (PSD) estimate from a chaotic
turbulent signal with finite length. Twenty Hamming windows are used. Local values of TKE are extracted
from 800 three-dimensional snapshots acquired at a Strouhal number of St = f d/Uj = 2.3. Snapshots
are stored every 8 time-steps, with dt Uj /d = 0.053. This enables a spectrum analysis up to the Nyquist
frequency of St = 1.15. Extracting local values from snapshots allows one to precisely position the probes
along the predicted jet trajectory.
Figure 22 shows the TKE spectrum as a function of dimensionless frequency at a) s/d = 2, and b) s/d = 4
in the near-field; and c) s/d = 17 in the far field. Figure 22a shows that at the jet center, the TKE spectrum
corresponds to fully developed turbulence exiting the pipe, as a −5/3 slope is observed. In the absence
of mean velocity gradient at the jet center, coherent structures do not develop at this location since no
peak is present in the PSD. The integral of the PSD (the power of TKE) is smaller at the jet center than
on the leeward side and the windward side of the jet. On the windward side of the jet, a strong increase
of TKE is observed in comparison with the jet center streamline. This is due to strong KH instabilities
developing at the interface between the jet and the cross-flow. The dominant dimensionless frequency on
the windward side of the jet at s/d = 2 is St ≈ 0.2. Subharmonics at St ≈ 0.4, 0.6 and 0.8 are also
observed. Analysis of the time-resolved evolution of coherent structures in the near-injector region shows
that regular ring-vortices correspond to the subharmonics at St = 0.4, 0.6 and 0.8 and the large PSD peak
at St = 0.2 correspond to less frequent amalgamation events, where several coherent structures gather and
induce strong velocity fluctuations when crossing the probe on the windward side. An example of such an
28

event is shown in Fig. 22d. This figure shows the square root of the TKE signal recorded by the probe on the
windward side at s/d = 2. At the instant noted, t0 , the velocity fluctuation is very large and equal to 0.6 uj .
This corresponds to the presence of a very energetic vortex resulting from an amalgamation event. On the
leeward side of the jet, the dominant Strouhal number is smaller than on the windward side and equal to
St ≈ 0.15. Similarly to the windward side of the jet, subharmonics are observed at St ≈ 0.3, and 0.6. On
both sides of the jet, TKE accumulates in the low-frequency range (large spatial scales) and the transfer to
high-frequency range (small spatial scales) is not as effective as in fully-developed turbulence as the absolute
value of the slope is larger than 5/3. At s/d = 2, the spectra thus correspond to a superimposition of the
developed turbulence exiting from the pipe and the nascent coherent structures in the shear-layers.
At s/d = 4, Fig. 22b shows a qualitatively similar spectrum on the windward side of the jet, with strong
modes at St = 0.2, 0.3, 0.5, and a small transfer rate to high-frequencies. On the jet center streamline,
strong modes are observed at St = 0.2, 0.35, 0.5, as well as a transfer rate to high-frequencies close to −5/3,
even though peaks are observed instead of fully broadband mixing. The peak at St = 0.5 is particularly
strong on the jet center streamline. Visual observation of the jet indicates that this is due to the staggered
arrangement of the coherent structures in the range of St = 0.2 − 0.35 on the windward and leeward sides of
the jet, which induces this higher frequency mode on the jet center streamline. On the leeward side of the
jet, although modes are observed at St = 0.15, 0.3, 0.5, these modes are weak and TKE is overall weaker (the
integral is smaller) and shows a transfer of energy to high-frequencies representative of developed turbulence.
This is due to the recirculation zone existing in the wake of the jet (induced by the nascent counter-rotating
vortex-pair), which recycles downstream turbulence back into the leeward side of the jet. This is also due to
the coherent structures that form on the windward side of the jet, which are transported downstream and
break down due to large-residence time. Thus, at s/d = 4, which corresponds to the TBP, both coherent
structures and broadband turbulence are present. The modes observed in Fourier space correspond to the
ring-vortices, counter-rotating vortex-pair and v-shape vortices in physical space. Figure 22c) shows that
far downstream, at s/d = 17, both an inertial range (−5/3 slope) and a dissipation range (−7 slope) is
resolved by the present simulation, consistently with the comparison of turbulence scales and grid resolution
provided in Appendix. B. On the center streamline and on the windward side of the jet, weak modes can still
be observed in the inertial range, which indicates that small-scale structures forms at the outer shear-layer
between the jet and the cross-flow, even in the far-field region. This is consistent with the maps of turbulence
scales provided in Figs. 16 and 18.
Figure 23 shows a map of TKE spectrum as a function of dimensionless frequency and curvilinear distance
defined along the a) windward side curve, b) center streamline, and c) leeward side curve. These plots were
inspired by the experimental study of Megerian et al. [52], where similar maps are shown for the windward
side of near-field of the jet, which enables a good understanding of the spatial evolution of turbulent time
scales. For s/d ≤ 2, TKE develops on the windward and leeward sides of the jet whereas it is very small on the
jet centerline. This corresponds to the development of coherent structures in the shear-layers, as explained
previously with Fig. 22a. The maximum of TKE is located in the vicinity of s/d = 4 for the windward
side of the jet and for the jet center streamline. This corresponds to the breakdown of coherent turbulent
flow structures (in the vicinity of the TBP). On the leeward side of the jet, the breakdown of turbulence is
slightly delayed and is located at s/d ≈ 5. After this breakdown of turbulence, a classical energy cascade is
present for both sides of the jet, as well as for the jet center streamline. Further downstream at s/d ≥ 7, one
can observe how the developed TKE spectra evolves with the jet trajectory. TKE progressively decreases
with the distance from injection. Characteristic times of turbulence increase, and spatial scales grow as the
spectra shifts toward smaller frequencies. This is due to amalgamation at large spatial scales and dissipation
at small spatial scales, as well as a decrease in mean velocity. On the windward side of the jet, spatial scales
do not grow as much and deterministic modes in the TKE spectrum are observed in the high-frequency
range. This is consistent with the evolution of the integral scale in Fig. 16 and Taylor-microscale in Fig. 18,
and is related to the generation of small-scale turbulent flow structures in the outer shear-layer between the
jet and the cross-flow. On the leeward side of the jet, more TKE is present in the large spatial scales than
on the jet center streamline and windward side of the jet, which is consistent with the maps of turbulence
scales provided in Figs 16 and 18.

7.4. Scalar gradient thickness
In this section, we characterize how scalar gradients evolve with downstream distance from injection.
Although it has been shown in Secs. 7.1-7.3 how turbulence scales evolve in the three-dimensional field, it is
not straightforward to determine how resolved scalar gradient thicknesses are linked with turbulence scales.

shows that this distribution gets wider with increasing distance from injection. This is representative of the
broadband turbulent mixing that occurs in the wake of the jet. Thus, in spite of the growth of turbulence
scales, the smallest scalar gradient thickness stays approximately constant and equal to 0.25 d in the wake of
the jet. This has important consequences in an engineering LES calculation, which typically employs coarser
resolutions. If one stretches the mesh with a s/d1/3 rate to approximately follow growth of turbulence scales
in the wake of the jet (see Sects. 7.1-7.2), this would probably only be sufficient to accurately resolve
turbulence without concentration gradients. If a scalar is transported by the flow, scalar gradients would be
more and more filtered and high-order statistics such as scalar fluctuations or scalar dissipation rate would
be less accurately predicted. Since in a reacting flow case combustion occur far downstream of the injection
point, where scalar-dissipation rates are on average smaller, the present gradient thickness analysis tends to
indicate that mesh spacing should stay approximately constant in the wake of the jet to accurately predict
scalar gradients and thus flame stabilization. This would require more computational resources than what is
ordinary used in such flow computations. This might be an imperative constraint for calculation accuracy,

8. Conclusions
In the present work, we conducted a highly resolved Large Eddy Simulation (LES) of turbulent mixing
in the Jet-In-Cross-Flow configuration studied experimentally by Su and Mungal [5]. LES and experimental
measurements of mean velocity, mean concentration, Reynolds stress tensor, and the turbulent scalar flux
have been compared and errors have been quantified. This extensive validation of LES results and the
corresponding analysis confirmed that LES is a good instrument for predicting complex three-dimensional
turbulent flows. The novelty of this work consists in the extended characterization of turbulence scales, their
origin, and dynamic evolution in the flow. Measuring these types of quantities is still not attainable using
experimental diagnostics due to constraints in resolution.
Using the validated results from LES, we presented in detail the turbulent mixing phenomenology for
the present configuration and how turbulence scales spatially evolve in a JICF. LES is used to perform
the analysis since experimental measurements of time-resolved 3D fields is still in its infancy, and because
sampling periods are generally limited with Direct Numerical Simulation. Four quantities were mapped: integral scales, Taylor microscales, turbulent energy spectra, and scalar gradient thicknesses. For the dynamic
quantities (i.e., integral scales, Taylor microscales, turbulent energy spectra) a similar spatial evolution
was observed. Scales first grow at various rates before breakdown of the jet turbulence, then a one-third
power law is observed along the jet trajectory and consistently within the wake. For the scalar fluctuations
and gradients, the trend is different. A sharp linear evolution of the gradient thickness occurs before the
complete destabilization of the jet followed by a secondary, almost constant, evolution further downstream.
This shows that a linear stretching of the mesh from the injection point to the far-field is excessive and will
impact the far-field predictions of turbulent mixing by filtering out key turbulent flow structures present in
the wake of the jet.
Observations such as those made above can be used to design a computational grid that would optimally
resolve turbulence. Based on the momentum flux ratio, the trajectory of the jet can be estimated first. An
optimal initial grid size at the injector exit should be fine enough to resolve the vorticity thickness. Then,
if scalar transport is not the dominant focal point, one may use a 1/3 power law to stretch the grid to
make it consistent with the evolution of turbulent flow structures in the wake of the jet. If scalar transport
is important and scalar gradients must be captured, we would recommend a stretching rate smaller than
(y/d)1/3 to maintain good resolution of scalar fields. Smaller cells, however, should be placed at the outer
edge of the jet (with dx = O(d/3)), to capture the interaction between the jet and the cross-flow, which
gives rise to small vortical structures that inject energy in the TKE spectrum.
Future work will focus on two aspects: 1) using coarser grid sizes, we will study the impact of filtering
a posteriori on resolved scales of turbulence, their interaction, and the subsequent errors on turbulent
mixing predictions; 2) we will devise an optimal mesh with local cell sizes following the spatial evolution of
turbulence scales using the maps presented in the current work. This will enable one to determine whether
the same level of accuracy can be obtained at a significantly lower cost. Similar analyses under reacting flow
conditions would also bring crucial insights into the effects of LES filtering on prediction accuracy.

C. Acetone concentration and jet penetration
As explained in Section 2, Su and Mungal [5] state that the acetone concentration “slowly diminishes”
