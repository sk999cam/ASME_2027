ABSTRACT
Results of a recent joint experimental and computational investigation of the flow through a plenum-fed 7-7-7 shaped film
cooling hole are presented. In particular, we compare the measured adiabatic effectiveness and mean temperature against implicit large eddy simulation (iLES) for blowing ratio approximately 2, density ratio 1.6, and Reynolds number 6000. The
results overall show reasonable agreement between the iLES and
the experimental results for the adiabatic effectiveness and gross
features of the mean temperature field. Notable discrepancies include the centerline adiabatic effectiveness near the hole, where
the iLES under-predicts the measurements by ∆η ≈ 0.05, and
the near-wall temperature, where the simulation results show features not present in the measurements. After showing this comparison, the iLES results are used to examine features that were
not measured in the experiments, including the in-hole flow and
the dominant fluxes in the mean internal energy equation downstream of the hole. Key findings include that the flow near the
entrance to the hole is highly turbulent and that there is a large
region of backflow near the exit of the hole. Further, the wellknown counter-rotating vortex pair downstream of the hole is
observed. Finally, the typical gradient diffusion hypothesis for
the Reynolds heat flux is evaluated and found to be incorrect.

INTRODUCTION
To achieve high thermal efficiency in modern gas turbine
engines, it is desirable to have high combustor exit temperatures.

Thus, the temperatures encountered at the combustor exit in such
engines are often in excess of 1500K [1, 2, 3]. Because of this,
effective cooling strategies to protect engine parts are required.
In practice, multiple strategies are used, including film cooling,
where relatively cool air from the compressor is diverted around
the combustor and injected into the hot flow through the turbine through discrete holes in the parts to be cooled. While film
cooling technology has existed for over 40 years, improvements
in understanding of the fundamental governing features of film
cooling flows could lead to both better modeling capabilities and
better cooling performance, which would enable higher combustor exit temperatures and improved thermal efficiency. The goal
of this work is to use high fidelity simulation in combination with
experiments to gain insight into the critical features of industryrelevant film cooling flows.
Film cooling technology was originally developed in the
1970s and has been an active area of research since that time.
As such, there is a large database of experimental results on film
cooling flows in the open literature. See [2] for a review of this
literature. In addition, there have been a number of high fidelity
simulations of film cooling flows reported in the open literature.
See, for example, [4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. Despite this body of research, there are still gaps in the community’s knowledge regarding film cooling flows. The effect of these gaps is that it is difficult to make accurate predictions of film cooling performance for
industry-relevant designs using models that are computationally
tractable for use during design. For example, common engineering RANS turbulence models, like the k-ε model, often dramatically overpredict the film cooling adiabatic effectiveness on the
hole centerline [11].
In the context of experiments, these knowledge gaps result
from two facts. First, much of the early experimental work was
performed on cylindrical holes, while shaped holes are now being used in some applications to improve cooling performance.
Second, and more fundamentally, it is difficult to make measurements of all potentially important quantities at all potentially important points in space. For instance, it is common to assess film
cooling performance by measuring the adiabatic effectiveness,
which can be accessed by measuring the wall temperature in an
approximately adiabatic wall experiment. Further, by performing a number of experiments, one can learn how the adiabatic
effectiveness depends on basic input parameters such as the density ratio, blowing ratio, etc. However, since, for instance, it
is difficult to make flow measurements inside the film cooling
hole itself or difficult to measure all the terms in the governing
transport equations, these measurements leave one with an incomplete understanding of the physics governing the observed
performance.
Thus, while the experimental literature has provided significant insight into the features of film cooling, there is an important role for high-fidelity simulations. Specifically, the difficulty
of measuring some quantities or in some regions in space can be

overcome using high fidelity simulation, and indeed, a number
of large eddy simulation (LES) and direct numerical simulation
(DNS) studies of film cooling flows have been reported in the
open literature, [4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10]. While these results are clearly
valuable, they are primarily focused on cylindrical hole configurations. We are aware of only a few LES results for shaped-hole
configurations in the open literature [6, 8, 9, 10] and no DNS results despite the growing importance of shaped hole geometries
in turbine cooling. Each of these studies noted issues with RANS
predictions evident in the LES studies. Leedom and Acharya [6]
found that the turbulence field was highly anisotropic for cylindrical and fan shaped holes. Koponka et al. [9] evaluated the
turbulent Schmidt number downstream of injection, and found
values varying from 0.1 to 2, whereas a RANS simulation would
generally assume a single value. Zhong et al. [10] concluded that
the diffusion of coolant jets is primarily due to various discrete
vortices developed within the coolant holes, and not predictable
by a RANS simulation.
This paper reports progress on high fidelity simulations
of shaped-hole geometries at industry-relevant operating conditions. The long-term objective of this work is to develop DNS
databases that can serve to both improve understanding of the
physics of film cooling and to guide the development of improved RANS and LES models. As a first step on this path, this
paper reports results of an implicit LES—i.e., a simulation where
no explicit subgrid model is used but on a grid too coarse to be a
true DNS—for the 7-7-7 shaped hole at DR = 1.6, BR = 2.0,
ReD = 6000, and Ma∞ = 0.15. Results of this implicit LES
(iLES) are compared to new experimental measurements conducted in concert with the computational simulation. These results show reasonable agreement between the iLES results and
measured adiabatic effectiveness and mean thermal fields. For
example, the maximum error in the centerline adiabatic effectiveness is ∆η ≈ 0.05, and the error over much of the comparison
is less than 0.02. Further, we examine the features of both the inhole and downstream velocity fields, which were not measured
in the experiment. Finally, we use the simulation results to examine the mean internal energy equation fluxes downstream of the
hole. It is found that mean convection is the dominant flux but
that the Reynolds heat flux is significant also. Further, the gradient diffusion hypothesis for the Reynolds heat flux is examined
and found to be incorrect.

Numerical Details
The simulation reported here was conducted using the software package PlasComCM1 , which was originally developed
by Bodony and co-workers at The University of Illinois at
Urbana-Champagne. PlasComCM uses high-order finite difference methods on overset meshes [20, 21] to discretize the compressible Navier–Stokes equations. In this work, the 4th-order
explicit finite difference method of [22] with 4th order interpolation between overset meshes and classical 4th order Runge-Kutta
time integration was used. Further, since the grid is too coarse to
fully resolve all features of the turbulence, as this is an implicit
LES, this choice of numerics is unstable. To stabilize the simulation, a 10th order Padé filter is applied to the conserved state
variables every 10 time steps.

to get the strength of this CRVP correct.
