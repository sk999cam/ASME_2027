ABSTRACT

Modeling

This study focuses on the physics and the modeling of the
heat transfer taking place within the Penn State 777 shaped hole
flow configuration. To do so, a dedicated Large-Eddy Simulation conducted with the AVBP solver is carried out and shows
good reproduction of the main flow characteristics observed in
terms of features and adiabatic effectiveness. This simulation is
then more deeply analyzed to conduct an a priori evaluation of
the most current heat flux assumptions used in RANS modeling,
being the Prandtl analogy and the Daly Harlow hypothesis. Results obtained for this shaped hole configuration confirm previous
ones obtained for cylindrical holes, showing the deficiencies of
classical RANS models. In particular, it is shown that the equivalent Turbulent Prandtl number usually fixed at 0.9 is here largely
overestimated. The most representative value is indeed around
0.5 in most regions of interest. It is also showed that the diffusion process is not the same in all directions, namely the wall
normal and transverse directions turbulent diffusion processes
differ. A Reynolds–Stress model simulation using the Elliptic
Blending Reynolds–Stress Model developed by Manceau is then
conducted, combined with different advanced heat flux models.
With this new and more advanced model, obtained results show
major improvements compared to state of the art RANS simulation using the k-𝜔 SST along with the Prandtl analogy. In
particular, the centerline and the lateral effectiveness profiles are
considerably enhanced, exhibiting a more representative lateral
spreading. The respective contributions issued by the improved
Reynolds–stress description and the heat flux models are eventually analyzed. A modified model for the heat flux formulation
is hence suggested in an attempt to provide extra improvement
compared to Daly-Harlow formulation. In conclusion, this work
strongly advocates for the use of anisotropic models either for
turbulent stress modeling or turbulent heat flux modeling for the
description of film cooling phenomena at stake.

1. INTRODUCTION

In the last decades, modern gas turbine efficiency has been
achieved by the increase of gas turbine temperature at which such
engines operate. As a consequence, the hot gases exiting the combustion chamber now exhibit temperature levels largely exceeding existing material melting points. The turbine has therefore to
be cooled to guarantee its structural integrity and an acceptable

life duration. An overview of the techniques used to optimize
the cooling strategy can be found for instance in Bunker [1] or
Dutta [2]. A major option relies on film cooling where cold gases
extracted at the compressor level circulate inside the blade before
being ejected through small holes at its surface to generate an
insulating layer. The geometry of the holes as well as their shape
needs thus to be optimized to maximize the coverage provided by
such a film. Numerous experimental or numerical studies have
considered the characterization and modeling of film cooling behavior and it has been shown to strongly depend on the geometry
but also on a number of flow parameters such as the Velocity Ratio
(VR), the Blowing Ratio (BR), or the Density Ratio (DR). Dedicated syntheses are provided by Bogard and Thole [3] or Dutta [2].
In this context, Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) has been
used to analyze and predict film cooling for at least thirty years.
Pioneering works by Demuren et al. [4], Leylek and Zerkle [5]
and Walters and Leylek [6] analyzed the main flow characteristics
and the coupling between the coolant issued from the hole and
the mainstream. Note that overall, the underlying physics is based
on the one encountered for a jet in crossflow configuration. After
hole ejection, the flow and the thermal mixing is dominated by
the presence of a pair of counter-rotating vortices, which drive
the turbulent mixing. This topology is associated with high levels
of turbulence, characterized by a strongly anisotropic structure.
Those early predictions revealed the poor estimations provided
by RANS techniques to evaluate parameters such as the adiabatic
or cooling effectiveness. In particular, the lateral spreading of the
film was shown to be largely underestimated by RANS models,
whereas the centerline effectiveness was usually overestimated.
The explanation for such an incorrect behavior relies in the eddy
viscosity assumption mostly used for RANS models. To increase
the lateral spreading, Bergeles et al. [7] suggested to take into
account the anisotropy of the flow by applying a correction to the
eddy diffusivity in the lateral directions. The anisotropy correction was based on the one encountered in a pipe flow. Lakehal et
al. generalized this work by using an explicit algebraic Reynolds–
Stress model, combined with a modified turbulent Prandtl number
near the wall and showed a significant improvement of temperature predictions [8, 9] on a flat plate configuration. However,
the lateral spreading was still underestimated on a blade test case.
Differential Reynolds–Stress Transport Models (DRSM) are a
natural extension of the preceding strategies. Those models do
not rely on an equilibrium assumption, but can suffer more from
numerical convergence issues, an increased CPU cost, and different grid requirements. However, Most [10] showed the potential
interest of such models for a multi-hole cooling configuration. In
particular, the velocity field appeared in better agreement. His
conclusions were confirmed by Rouina [11], on a fan shaped hole
configuration, where a DRSM showed superior performance for
the prediction of the adiabatic effectiveness maps, for BR of 1
and 2. More recently, Paysant [12] successfully applied two versions of DRSM models to describe the trajectory and mixing of
a 400°C hot jet in an ambient crossflow. Compared to a k-𝜔
SST model, the mixing of the counter-rotating vortices is largely
increased, leading to a significantly enhanced temperature prediction. In parallel to these RANS estimations, LES were conducted
and showed a satisfactory agreement with experimental data as

shown by Stratton [13]. The generated databases were then analyzed, to further investigate the turbulent characteristics of the
flow. Stratton and Shih [14] showed in particular that the turbulent stress and strain fields are aligned far from the wall, but their
misalignment cannot be neglected near the wall, leading to large
discrepancies.

model is the Gradient Diffusion Hypothesis (GDH) where the
transport of heat by turbulent fluctuations 𝑢 𝑖′𝑇 ′ is taken to be
proportional to the temperature gradient:

2. FLOW CONFIGURATION

The 777 pattern was defined by Shroeder and Thole in [20]
and its characteristics can be visualized in Figure 1. The name
originates from the 7◦ expansion in all directions for the diffuser
part. The film cooling array is made of five shaped holes, with a
metering diameter of 𝐷 = 7.75 mm. The retained configuration
corresponds to a low freestream intensity 𝑇𝑢∞ = 0.5%. In terms
of main characteristics ratios, the studied case is defined by a
Blowing Ratio (BR) and a Density Ratio (DR) of 1.5. The
freestream velocity and temperature are 𝑈∞ = 10.17 m.s−1 , and
𝑇∞ = 295 K. The coolant temperature is 198 K. The approach
boundary layer characteristics were measured upstream the hole
by Laser Doppler Velocimetry for 𝑥/𝐷 = −4.7 and are provided
in Table 1. The origin is located at the intersection of the metering
section axis and the surface of the plate. The mass flow rate in
the plenum is controlled to guarantee the appropriate blowing
ratio and conditioning screens guarantee a uniform flow and a
low turbulence level. Additional details are provided in [20].

From those maps, it clearly appears that a GDH hypothesis fails to represent the complex flow features involved. Even
the more elaborate GGDH and HOGGDH only partly reproduce
the gradient/heat flux misalignment, in particular near the walls.
Similar conclusions had been formulated by Stratton [13] for the
GDH, and by Ling et al. [17] for higher order models. However,
those conclusions were appropriate only for cylindrical holes and
the present study allows a generalization to shaped hole geometries. The conclusions on the Reynolds stress vs strain and heat
flux vs temperature gradients distributions being on this case very
similar to the above mentioned studies [13] [17], no extra analysis
is presented here.

A more accurate prediction of film cooling heat transfer is

6. CONCLUSION

The Penn State 777 shaped hole configuration was studied
combining a Large-Eddy Simulation with RANS calculations
conducted in parallel. The Large-Eddy Simulation exhibited
a good agreement with experimental results and discrepancies
could be largely explained by conduction effects present on the
mock-up and neglected in the calculation. The LES reference
results were then used to investigate the validity of advanced turbulent heat flux models on such a configuration. This analysis
confirmed the inability of eddy diffusivity models to represent
heat transfer adequately. More specifically, an equivalent turbulent Prandtl number was derived and its value in the central region
was shown to be around 0.5. The GGDH and HOGGDH models
were more appropriate, and the embedded constants showed less
variations, compared to the turbulent Prandtl number. In parallel,
calculations carried out with a state of the art Reynolds–Stress
Model confirmed the major improvement brought by the use of
a full anisotropic model. Compared to the 𝑘 − 𝜔 SST reference,
the wall temperature description is largely enhanced, not only on
the centerline, but in the spanwise directions as well. This improvement is due to a better representation of the kidney-shaped
vortices typical of a jet in crossflow interaction, and whose magnitude is usually overestimated by eddy viscosity models.The
