Boeing Commercial Airplanes
In this paper we have confirmed that eddy viscosity turbulence models are
inadequate to predict secondary vortical flows developed from corners in
internal flows. To remedy this shortcoming, we have added the quadratic
constitutive relation (QCR) of Spalart to the one- and two-equation SA and SST
turbulence models, respectively. The results of QCR with the SA and SST
turbulence models have been validated against experimental data of Davis and
Gessner for supersonic flow through a square duct. The approach is shown to
be simple to implement and overall agreement is seen to improve with the use
of QCR.
Introduction: Supersonic flow through a square duct is altered by secondary vortical flow
developing from the corners. These secondary flows are generated by Reynolds stress
gradients acting in the corner region and appear to have similar structure to those found in
subsonic flow through square ducts [1-3]. Such flow is representative of various airplane inlets
and therefore it is important to understand and predict the impact of this secondary flow on
inlet pressure recovery and distortion. An experimental study was performed at the University
of Washington to gain a better understanding of how the secondary flow associated with
corners affects local flow conditions in a square duct over the development length [3]. This
configuration was utilized in this study to validate and improve the RANS turbulence models
utilized for corner flows at Boeing.
Accurate prediction of the flow around and through the aircraft is essential for design
improvement, risk mitigation, and wind-tunnel and flight test reduction. At Boeing, the RANS
approach is used routinely for design and analysis of our configurations. While RANS has
proven to be a powerful approach for flow-field prediction, it still has some shortcomings even
for steady state flow predictions. The one- and two-equation SA [4] and SST [5] eddy viscosity
turbulence models, respectively, are still the workhorses for routine applications. The Reynolds

Stress turbulence models (RSTM), which do not use turbulent viscosity and therefore have the
natural potential to capture secondary flows of the second kind, are not yet robust and efficient
for routine analysis.
The lack of accurate prediction capability of corner flow associated with linear eddy viscosity
turbulence models such as SA and SST has created a serious shortcoming in using RANS for
design, analysis, and wind-tunnel predictions in configurations with corners. Accurate
prediction of inlet recovery and distortion requires the ability to predict the secondary flow
associated with sharp corners. In this paper, we will investigate supersonic flow at Mach 3.9
through a square duct using SA and SST turbulence models, comparing results with
experimental data of Davis and Gessner [3], and confirm the shortcomings of these models.
We will then demonstrate an approach proposed by Spalart [6] to remedy this shortcoming by
utilizing a Quadratic Constitutive Relation (QCR) which can be combined with SA or other eddy
viscosity models such as SST.
Supersonic flow through the duct employed in this study was tested by Davis and Gessner [3] at
the University of Washington. The square duct tested has a length-to-width ratio of 50, with
dimensions of 1x1x50 inches (Figure 1a). The computational domain consists of two zones: an
upstream zone (Figure 1b) to ensure clean flow entering duct, and the test section highlighted
in red in Figure 1a. A grid resolution study was performed to ensure the results presented in
this manuscript are mesh-independent. Based on the experiment performed at the University
of Washington the flow is steady and symmetric and therefore only ¼ of the configuration was
used to perform the body of the work as well as the mesh resolution study. A periodic
boundary condition was used to connect the quadrants. A CFD run was obtained on the full
configuration to ensure the flow is steady and symmetric, as it was predicted in the experiment.
The results of the full duct vs. ¼ duct and the grid resolution study are discussed in the last
section of this paper.

Analysis: The standard SST, SA, and SA-RC turbulence models were selected for the purpose of
this study as they are the primary turbulence models used at Boeing for design and analysis.
The CFD results obtained in this study are compared with the experimental data which were
collected for total pressure and mean velocity at four axial locations, X/D of 5.37, 20, 40, and
50. Figures 2a, 2b, and 2c show the velocity profiles at X/D of 20, 40, and 50 for the wall
bisector and corner bisector for the SA, SA-RC, and SST turbulence models. The SA-RC model
includes a correction for Rotation/Curvature, which is not powerful in the present flow. All
models predicted the wall bisector velocity profile with reasonable accuracy. At X/D=40, the SA
and SA-RC results are slightly closer to data than SST for the wall bisector. At X/D=50 where
flow is fully developed, SA and SA-RC matches the data for the wall bisector. No model
compared well with data for the velocity corner bisectors.

These results clearly indicate the shortcomings of eddy viscosity models due to the lack of
ability to predict the aspects of anisotropy, namely the differences between streamwise, wallnormal, and lateral Reynolds stresses, which create the secondary flow. This result is not new
and was expected, as it has been known for many decades that eddy viscosity models will not
predict square duct flow. The QCR model developed by Spalart has been implemented into
BCFD [7] for the SA and SST turbulence models. The same case was rerun to investigate and
document the improvements associated with the QCR for supersonic turbulent flow in a square
duct. Other methods, such as stress flux models, would also predict the turbulence driven
secondary flows but were not examined in this study.

The 2000 model lacked the Ccr2 term, but corner flows are dominated by the Ccr1 term. This
term is related to a proposal of Wilcox and Rubesin [8], and is found in many Algebraic Reynolds
Stress models, along with (often numerous) cubic and other terms. Note also that the acronym
QCR was not used in the original paper. Finally, note that correction such as that of Wilcox and
Rubesin [8] would not be ready for application to one-equation models.
Grid Topology Study: While the square duct of this study lends itself nicely to a hexahedral
mesh, the turbulence modeling lessons learned will be applied to more complicated
configurations. This necessitates investigating any topological dependencies of the QCR
modification. The two topologies are compared on the medium hexahedral and mixed element
meshes after conducting a grid resolution study for each topology. The hex mesh consisted of
5.1 million cells, while the mixed mesh had 20.1 million cells. The hex mesh required far fewer
grid points since the grid could be stretched in the axial direction. The initial wall spacing,
0.00008 inches, is the same between the two grids, as is the size at the centerline of the duct,
0.02 inches. The cross-plane resolution of each mesh can be seen in Figure 6.

During the grid resolutions studies, it was determined that for the mixed element meshes, the
default value of Ccr2 was hindering convergence and solution stability. Instead, a Ccr2 value of
zero is needed for robust solutions on the mixed element mesh. Attempts with Ccr2 set to zero
for the hex mesh experienced similar convergence and stability issues as the mixed mesh with
the default value of Ccr2. Therefore, all mixed element solutions presented in this paper have
Ccr2 set to zero, while the hexahedral mesh has the default value. Figure 7 shows the wall
bisector velocity at X/D=20 and 50 for mixed element and hexahedral meshes. At X/D of 20,
the velocity profiles are almost identical, and by X/D of 50, only slight differences exist near the
wall. This is likely due to resolution rather than topology. The span-wise skin friction shown in
Figure 8 is also quite similar between the two mesh topologies at both locations. Given that
both meshes compare well with the test data and the need to apply the QCR modification to
arbitrary configurations, the remainder of the study focuses on the mixed element topology.

Results: The velocity profiles for the wall and corner bisector are shown in Figure 9 for three
axial locations (X/D=20, 40, and 50). The QCR improves the corner bisectors and most wall
bisector velocity profiles at all three locations; and matches the data more closely in the
upstream section than in the downstream section. The velocity vectors for SA, SA-QCR, SSTQCR, and wind-tunnel data at X/D=50 are shown in Figure 10; the secondary vortical flows are
indeed predicted by the QCR modified models. Again, the scale between CFD solutions is
consistent, but is not consistent with the WT data. Figure 11 shows the transverse velocity
profiles with QCR correction at X/D=20 and 50 for wall and corner bisectors. The wall bisector
(Figure 11a) shows initially (X/D=20) the velocity profile to be negative, indicating the flow is
toward the wall, until the secondary flow from the corners have grown to reverse the flow
direction, as shown at X/D=50. At X/D=20 the SST-QCR velocity profile matches the data
closely, while SA-QCR is slightly under-predict the data, indicating small vortical flow has been
initiated. At X/D=50 both models predict nearly the same velocity profile, matching the peak
velocity. However, both models over predict the size of corner vortical flow. The wind-tunnel
results indicates reversal of flow (toward the wall) at y/a=0.5 while CFD shows a mild reversal at
y/a=0.8 (Figures 10 and 11). Figure 11b shows that the transverse velocity is always negative,
indicating flow being toward the corner. The peak velocity is predicted to be about y/a=0.3,
which is close to the corner. The magnitude of the velocity is under-predicted by the CFD along
the duct.
Figure 12 shows the core velocity and color contour of velocity on the wall bisector. The QCR
shrinks the extent of the core velocity for both turbulence models, bringing it closer to the wind
tunnel data. SA-QCR and SST-QCR core velocities closely compare with experimental data
(Figure 12) and the flow is fully developed about X/D=32, while Davis and Gessner indicated
that the flow is fully developed about X/D=35. Both models match the wind-tunnel data in the
core region, however, SA-QCR matches the wind-tunnel data in the fully developed section

The normalized total pressure profiles are shown in Figure 14, and the QCR modification to the
turbulence models clearly improves the pressure profiles for both wall and corner bisectors. In
this case, the SA-QCR matches the data more closely than SST-QCR toward the duct centerline
especially at X/D=40 and 50 where the flow is fully developed. This indicates that SST ever
slightly over predicts the extent of the corner flow. Also, there is no reason a priori to use
different values of the QCR constants for the two models. At X/D=20 the experimental pressure
for the corner bisector indicates no core flow and deviates from the predicted inlet total
pressure. Davis and Gessner [3] indicated that this is due to the weak pressure reflection at this
location in the duct in the inviscid core. The pressure reflection predicted by the CFD analysis
at this location is small and does not alter the core flow total pressure profile. In the near wall
for the wall bisector the total pressure has been expanded for X/D=40 and 50. The total
pressure at both locations approaches the static pressure on the wall (Figure 13), which

Conclusions: In this study we have verified that linear eddy viscosity models are inadequate to
predict the flow behavior in presence of corners of inlets – a result which was expected a priori.
We have also demonstrated that a Quadratic Constitutive Relation does improve the velocity,
total pressure, and skin friction predictions due to its ability to predict turbulence driven
secondary flows. From the CFD results one can conclude that vortex strength more accurately
predicted in the upstream region (X/D=20) than downstream section (X/D=50), See Figures 11
and 15. It appears from this study that QCR is a viable approach for improved predictions of
pressure recovery and distortion at the Aerodynamic Interface Plane (AIP) in presence of
corners. Additional investigation is required to quantify the improvement of pressure recovery
and distortion.
