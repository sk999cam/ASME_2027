Numerical Investigation
of a Film-Cooling Flow Structure:
Effect of Crossflow Turbulence
Numerical simulation results using large-eddy simulation of a flow configuration relevant
to the film cooling of turbine blades are presented. The flow configuration and the simulation parameters are chosen according to an experiment from literature, in which a hot turbulent crossflow over a flat plate is cooled by fluid issuing from a large isobaric plenum
through a short inclined circular nozzle. Special attention is paid to the flow structure
within the jet nozzle and the mixing region, as well as to the effect of the crossflow fluctuations thereon. To this end, the numerical results with the turbulent crossflow are compared
to our previous data obtained with a steady mean-turbulent inflow profile. While the flow
inside the nozzle is very similar for the two cases, large differences occur in the mixing
region, where a much enhanced spreading of the coolant is observed with the turbulent
crossflow. Consequently, the good agreement of the film-cooling efficiencies with the experimental data for the turbulent-crossflow case is contrasted by large deviations with the stationary inflow due to the lack of crossflow fluctuations. [DOI: 10.1115/1.4023361]
Keywords: film cooling, jet in crossflow, large-eddy simulation, approximate-deconvolution model, turbulent crossflow, synthetic-eddy method

Introduction

In modern high-performance gas turbines, the hot gas from the
combustor enters the turbine stages at temperatures that often
exceed the material limits of the turbine blades. Therefore, they
have to be thermally protected, which is usually done by a technique called film cooling [1–3]. Here cool air (usually bleed air
from the turbine compressor) is ejected through rows of small
holes near the leading edge of the blades and across their surface.
The cool air mixes with the oncoming hot crossflow and creates a
layer of relatively cold gas which shields the blade surfaces.
Film-cooling flows belong to the class of jets-in-crossflow, which
is a heavily researched flow configuration with a wide range of
practical applications [4]. Film cooling itself has also been subject
to intensive research for several decades. A good recent review is
given in [5], and older investigations are summarized in [6]. However, despite the large body of knowledge and experience, film
cooling is still a subject not fully mastered yet. The main concern
of current academic research and industrial development efforts on
film cooling is the optimization of the cooling process, which
requires a thorough understanding of the instantaneous flow behavior due to the complex flow topology in the mixing region near and
downstream of the film-cooling hole.
A topic that deserves particular attention is the avoidance of socalled “hot spots” [7]. These instantaneous patches of hot gas at
the blade surface are caused by deficiencies in the film-cooling
process. They thermally strain the blade surface and lead to the
premature degradation of the blade material or even its structural
failure, necessitating a costly repair or exchange. Due to the high
frequencies and the closed and confined measurement environment, experimental investigations of hot spots have remained
notoriously costly and challenging. In the numerical domain, the
Reynolds-averaged Navier–Stokes simulations, which are current
industry practice, are not able to capture the instantaneous

Since the cooling process is strongly influenced by the vortices
that are formed around the film-cooling hole and farther downstream, the physical understanding of the vortex dynamics and
vortex interaction is crucial to film cooling. Matters are further
complicated by the fact that secondary flows and recirculation
regions within the nozzle connecting plenum and crossflow can
greatly affect the downstream mixing properties [8–11]. This was
shown to be particularly important in the case of low length-todiameter ratios as occurring in real applications [12–14]. Therefore, a numerical investigation of a realistic film-cooling scenario
is bound to fully include the plenum and nozzle geometry as well
as proper flow conditions for them. Furthermore, one cannot
neglect to analyze the influence and interaction of the secondary
flow structures (which are themselves heavily dependent on the
specific properties of plenum and nozzle) with the main flow.
One of the most frequently investigated film-cooling configurations consists of a nozzle with a length-to-diameter ratio of 1.75 or
3.5 with a streamwise inclination of 35 deg at a spanwise hole distance of three nozzle diameters. One such series of experiments was
carried out by Sinha and others [15–29]. Numerical simulations of
this configuration based on the Reynolds-averaged Navier–Stokes
(RANS) approach were performed by several groups [8–10,30–32],
as well as isolated detached-eddy simulations (DES) [33].
Although these RANS studies were generally able to reproduce
the gross flow behavior and predict the mean-flow quantities such
as the film-cooling efficiency with reasonable accuracy, a timedependent simulation approach such as large-eddy simulation
(LES) offers great advantages. Since the mixing process is an inherently nonstationary phenomenon, which is (in the near field) dominated by secondary flows and vortex interactions, only unsteady
simulation methods can properly capture these aspects. One prime
example are the hot spots, which can only be seen in an unsteady
flow field but not in the time-averaged solution obtained by RANS.

Tyagi and Acharya [36,37] published one of the first LES treatments of film cooling. Their approach was based on an incompressible code using the dynamic mixed subgrid-scale model (DMM)
[38] and a passive scalar for the evolution of the temperature field.
Two geometric variants for the jet inlet were considered, the first
one consisting of a plenum feeding a short nozzle as in [25]. In
the second one, the long delivery tube of the experiment in [26]
was modeled with a short nozzle, at whose inlet unsteady velocity
profiles are prescribed (by a combination of mean profiles from a
RANS computation with Gaussian noise). In both cases the
oncoming turbulent boundary-layer crossflow was generated with
Gaussian white-noise perturbations in the inflow plane.
Liu and Pletcher [39] performed a quite highly resolved LES
with a compressible finite-volume code based on the LU-SGS
scheme and a dynamic SGS model. Instead of a plenum, a long
delivery tube was employed, whose inflow data was provided by a
simultaneously running LES of turbulent pipe flow. The turbulent
boundary-layer fluctuations were generated with a rescalingrecycling method. Their analysis mostly focuses on the filmcooling aspects of the flow and demonstrate a good agreement
with experimental film-cooling efficiency data from literature.
Lele and Iourokina/Peet also developed an LES approach of
Sinha’s film-cooling configuration [40–44]. Their simulation
method used two different coupled codes, a low-Mach number
code for the plenum and the film-cooling holes, and a compressible code for the crossflow and mixing regions. The dynamic
Smagorinsky model was used for the treatment of the subgrid
scales. In the first publication [40] a simplified flow configuration
with a laminar crossflow and perpendicular ejection was considered, and the investigation focuses on qualitative aspects of the
velocity field. In the subsequent paper [42] the setup was refined by
using a 35-deg ejection angle, as well as a turbulent boundary layer
with fluctuations generated by the rescaling-recycling method. Contours of the velocity field compare favorably to the measurements
of Pietrzyk et al. [23]. Reference [44] contains a more detailed
comparison of velocity profiles, which are found to be in good
agreement, as well as the film-cooling efficiency, where some
deviations are observed to the data of Sinha et al. [25].
A series of LES results was published by Schröder et al.
[45–48]. While the individual contributions vary in scope, they
share a common simulation approach using a compressible finitevolume code based on the implicit subgrid-scale model AUSM
(advective upstream splitting method) [49], low-Mach number
improvements (dual time stepping, multigrid, and preconditioning; except for the first publication), a rescaling-recycling method
for the turbulent boundary layer, and a large plenum feeding the
jet. The first contribution [45] studies the influence of two different ejection angles (30 and 90 deg) for the jet on the flow field.
The following publication [46] keeps the ejection angle at 30 deg
and demonstrates a good agreement of the velocity field with
in-house PIV measurements, as well as of the film-cooling efficiency with the experiments of Sinha et al. [25]. The two subsequent papers focus on the effect of the jet-to-crossflow density
ratio [47] and the shape of the film-cooling hole [48] on the filmcooling behavior.
Another LES investigation of film cooling targeting the leading
edge of the blade was undertaken recently by Rozati and Tafti
[50–53].
In our present numerical study of film cooling we investigate
the experimental configuration of Sinha et al. [25] by performing
large-eddy simulations (LES) with the approximate deconvolution
model (ADM) [2,54]. In a series of LES of separated flow configurations with increasing complexity [55–60], the present ADM
implementation has been successfully validated, and the subgridscale model has been proven to work well for compressible flows
featuring strong unsteady vortical structures.

To reduce the computational effort, the simulation approach
was simplified in a first step. The oncoming turbulent boundary
layer of the experiment was represented with a steady meanturbulent profile, which also models to some extent the profile of
the crossflow shortly downstream of the leading edge of a turbine
blade (before its transition to turbulence). The evaluation of the
adequacy of this approach for the prediction of the data of Sinha
et al. [25], as well as the analysis of the complex flow structure
within the nozzle and the mixing region, were the primary goals
of our first contribution [61]. However, large deviations in the
film-cooling efficiencies from the experimental data indicated that
the steady crossflow did not suitably model the behavior of fully
turbulent inflow for the purpose of film cooling, mainly due to the
lack of crossflow fluctuations.
In the present paper we consequently employ unsteady turbulent inflow data for the crossflow, which is generated with the
synthetic-eddy method (SEM) [62,63]. The present focus is twofold. First, the differences in flow topology and cooling behavior
between the two crossflow types (stationary and unsteady turbulent) are investigated. Second, the more realistic inflow conditions
for the crossflow should yield a much improved prediction of the
experimental results of Sinha et al. [25] and therefore demonstrate
the suitability of our refined simulation approach for film cooling.
The generation of realistic inflow turbulence is still a quite new
area of computational fluid dynamics, which has experienced a
considerable boost with the recent rise of the DES simulation
methodology. The main demands for an inflow-generation method
is a low computational overhead, a fast transition to a fully developed turbulence state (i.e., a short streamwise transition length),
and flexibility with respect for the prescription of arbitrary
Reynolds-stress profiles or autocorrelations of the velocity fluctuations. Moreover, for the present simulation approach, a straightforward application to domain decomposition and parallelization
are further desirable properties.
The tripping method [64] imposes a temporally varying near-wall
volume force to a spanwise stretch of the flow that simulates the
influence of a trip wire. While its implementation is relatively simple, the transition length of the tripping method (and thus the computational overhead) is quite long since the flow must undergo more
or less full physical transition-to-turbulence as in a flow experiment
in a wind tunnel. Similar statements can be made for inflowgeneration methods based on suction and blowing at the wall.
The rescaling-recycling method [65], which was also used in previous large-eddy simulations of compressible boundary-layer flow
using ADM [66], is another prominent example of an inflowgeneration method. Here an independent simulation of spatially
developing boundary-layer flow is conducted parallel to the main
flow simulation. The flow at a fixed downstream location is
sampled, suitably rescaled (using standard scaling laws from
boundary-layer theory) and imposed (“recycled”) in its inflow plane.
The unsteady flow profiles at a chosen downstream location (e.g.,
depending on the desired Reynolds number) can then be prescribed
in the inlet plane of the boundary layer of the main simulation.
While the transition length of the rescaling-recycling method
should be somewhat shorter than that of the tripping method, due
to the prescription of relatively realistic turbulence, it has some
serious downsides. The implementation is quite challenging, especially that of the spanwise sampling when considering domain
decomposition, curvilinear grids, and parallelization. Some
“kludges” are required to make it work in a robust way (especially
in the initial startup phase). The quasi-periodic boundary conditions of the precursor simulation create artificial (i.e., unphysical)
streamwise autocorrelations of the flow fluctuations. Finally, the
computational overhead is considerable, due to the inclusion of a
full separate boundary-layer simulation and the moderately long
transition length.
The synthetic-eddy method was developed recently by Jarrin
et al. [62,63] and has already become a popular inflow-generation
method for DES simulations. It owes this mainly to the fact that it
exhibits a number of intriguing properties and none of the

This paper is structured as follows. First the investigated flow
configuration and the simulation approach are introduced in the
next section. In Sec. 3 the large-eddy simulation results with both
the stationary and the unsteady inflow data for the turbulent crossflow are presented and compared to each other. After an analysis
of the instantaneous flow field in Sec. 3.1, the time-averaged flow
data is studied in various cross sections of the configuration. Special focus is placed on the flow structure in the film-cooling hole
and the complex system of vortices evolving within the nozzle
and in the mixing region. Finally the mean film-cooling efficiency
results are compared to the experimental data of Sinha et al. [25].

Flow-Case Specification and Simulation Approach

2.1 Flow Configuration, Boundary Conditions, and Spatial
Discretization. Figure 1 depicts a schematic diagram of the filmcooling configuration of Sinha et al. [25]. The hot fluid in the
turbulent boundary layer above the flat wall is cooled by a
spanwise-periodic row of jets emerging from a large isobaric plenum below the wall. The plenum and the boundary layer are connected through a short hole of circular cross section with a
streamwise inclination angle of 35 deg with respect to the wall of
the boundary layer. Once the coolant jet (shaded gray in the
sketch) exits the film-cooling hole, it mixes with the oncoming

crossflow, thereby forming a layer of relatively cool fluid in the
vicinity of the wall. With increasing streamwise distance, the jet
dilutes and spreads in the vertical and spanwise directions, indicated by its widening boundaries in the sketch.
All lengths are nondimensionalized with the diameter of the filmcooling nozzle D (hereafter omitted for brevity). The overall dimensions of the computational domain above the flat-plate wall are
25  3  5 (Fig. 1). The center of the orifice of the film-cooling hole
is located five nozzle diameters downstream of the boundary-layer
inlet. The thickness of the wall between the plenum and the boundary layer is one nozzle diameter, leading to an axial length of the
film-cooling nozzle of approximately 1.75 (measured between the
intersections of the nozzle axis with the boundary layer and upper
plenum walls). The plenum is modeled with a rectangular box of
dimensions 8  3  7. In the vertical (z) direction, a stretch of two
nozzle diameters is used for a dampening region (“sponge”), in
which the desired inflow quantities are imposed [67,68]. The boundary of the sponge region is marked with a dashed line in Fig. 1(a).
The boundary conditions are the same as in our previous filmcooling simulation [61]. At the adiabatic walls no-slip boundary
conditions are enforced, and no explicit wall modeling (such as the
use of wall functions) takes place. The spanwise boundaries are periodic, and nonreflecting boundary conditions are applied at all outflow planes on the top and downstream faces of the computational
domain. Special care was found to be necessary in the formulation
of the inflow conditions in the plenum, where for subsonic flow
computations using the compressible Navier–Stokes solutions a
special problem arises. While the desired inlet values for the density and velocities are known from the experiment, the physically
appropriate pressure is unknown and needs to be computed from
the instantaneous flow solution. This is achieved by using a dampening region (sponge) in combination with a nonreflecting boundary condition at the plenum inlet [67,68].

2.2 Simulation Code, Numerical Method and SubgridScale Modeling. The NSMB (Navier–Stokes multiblock) code
[69,70] employed for this investigation is a block-parallel cellcentered compressible finite-volume solver for structured grids.
The present simulation is carried out with a skew-symmetric central spatial discretization of fourth-order accuracy for the inviscid
fluxes, whose intrinsic properties contribute to accuracy and numerical stability by reducing aliasing errors [71,72], and a secondorder scheme for the viscous fluxes. The flow solution is advanced
in time with a four-stage Runge–Kutta scheme. To facilitate statistical sampling, the time step was held constant at Dt ¼ 103 time
units tref ¼ D=u1 . This corresponds to a CFL number of 2, which
is approximately 70% of the linear stability limit of the time
discretization.

form from an approximation U* of the unfiltered state vector U,
which is obtained by a defiltering procedure [54]. The approximatedeconvolution filter QN (of order N) is based on a truncated series
expansion of the inverse LES filter G1, leading to an approximate
deconvolution U* defined by a linear combination of the repeatedly
filtered state vector (the overbar  denotes spatial filtering, I is the

The synthetic-eddy method (SEM) [62,63] was developed
recently to facilitate the generation of realistic turbulent inflow
data by reducing the computational demands and improving the
flexibility. In SEM, the turbulent fluctuations in the inflow plane
are constructed from the contribution of many coherent structures
(or eddies). These eddies exhibit a location, a length scale, and an
orientation and are convected (independently of the main simulation) with a characteristic mean velocity in a rectangular box with
periodic boundary conditions. The fluctuating velocity at a given
location in the inflow plane can be computed at each time from
the contributions of all eddies. Arbitrary Reynolds-stress tensors
can be imposed by transforming the generated signal with the
Cholesky-decomposed Reynolds-stress tensor [65].

summarized in Table 1. In accordance with the experiments, which
were conducted in a wind tunnel using air, the isentropic coefficient
c ¼ 1.4 is that of a diatomic gas and the Prandtl number is Pr ¼ 0.7.

ðquÞ1 is used for the nondimensionalization.
An instantaneous flow field of the predecessor simulation with
steady crossflow [61] was used as initial condition for the present
simulation. After switching on the SEM method and prescribing
its unsteady inflow data, a transitional period of about 300 time
units (or about 12 flow-through times Lx =u1 , with the streamwise
domain size Lx ¼ 25) was awaited until the flow solution was
found to be statistically stationary.
The mean flow was computed by temporal averaging of the full
three-dimensional flow field during the numerical simulation. Statistical sampling was performed every 40 time steps during 554
time units D=u1 , thereby accumulating almost 14,000 samples.
The convergence of the statistics was visually confirmed by comparing the averaged solution after different sampling time intervals. The computation was carried out in parallel on four
processors of a NEC SX-8 vector machine, using about 1000 CPU
hours (including initial transients).

(Favre) filtering from our Nomenclature in comparison to our previous publication [61] (except for the discussion of the ADM
subgrid-scale model above). Therefore, all flow quantities should
be understood as filtered ones.

4

Summary and Conclusions

The flow structure and mixing behavior of the film-cooling flow
configuration of Sinha et al. [25] was numerically investigated and
compared for two different crossflow types. In the first, a steady
mean-turbulent profile models the crossflow behavior shortly downstream of the leading edge of a turbine blade, whereas in the second
unsteady turbulent inflow data (generated with the synthetic-eddy
method [62,63]) provides more realistic inlet boundary conditions
for a comparison with the experimental measurements. The simulations were performed using the NSMB code [69,70] in conjunction
with the large-eddy simulation approach based on the approximatedeconvolution subgrid-scale model (ADM) [2,54].
The presented results concern both instantaneous and timeaveraged data. In visualizations of the three-dimensional instantaneous flow field, the complicated flow structure in the mixing
region was investigated, in particular the most prominent vortices
and their interaction. While the shedding of hairpin vortices is
present in both cases, the location of their formation is situated
considerably farther upstream with the turbulent crossflow.
The topology of the mean flow field was studied in various
cross sections. The most prominent difference between the results

of the two crossflow types is the faster spreading of the coolant jet
in the turbulent-crossflow case. Furthermore; in contrast to the
steady inflow, it also does not detach from the crossflow wall
upon leaving the orifice. The flow structure within the jet nozzle
and near the crossflow wall; on the other hand, was found to be
very similar in both cases.
The predicted film-cooling efficiencies from both simulations
were compared with the measurements of Sinha et al. [25].
Whereas considerable deviations to the experimental data were
found in case of the stationary crossflow, the results obtained with
the unsteady crossflow agree well with the reference data. They
thus demonstrate the applicability of the current simulation
approach to film-cooling flows and confirm our presumption [61]
that the deviations of the steady-crossflow case are due to the lack
of crossflow fluctuations.
This leads to the conclusion that while the usage of a stationary
crossflow might model certain aspects of the flow behavior near the
leading edge of the turbine blade, it is not a suitable simplification
for film cooling near the center of the blade (which is commonly
investigated). Here only a simulation approach that includes unsteady
inflow fluctuations for the boundary layer can be expected to yield a
realistic reproduction of the flow structure and the mixing behavior.
