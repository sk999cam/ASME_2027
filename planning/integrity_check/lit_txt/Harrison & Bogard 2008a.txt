ABSTRACT
The realizable k-ε, standard k-ω, and RSM turbulence models
were used to simulate flat plate film cooling experiments that are
commonly described in literature. Adiabatic effectiveness simulations
revealed that using the standard k-ω model resulted in the closest
agreement with experimentally determined laterally averaged adiabatic
effectiveness, but the worst agreement with centerline adiabatic
effectiveness. Conversely, the realizable k-ε model agreed worst with
experimental laterally averaged adiabatic effectiveness values and best
with centerline values. Use of the anisotropic RSM model was not
found to predict more realistic coolant spreading than the other
models. Simulations to find heat transfer coefficients without film
cooling showed good agreement with correlations for all three models,
and the closest agreement resulted from using the realizable k-ε model.
Heat transfer coefficient augmentation was also examined for two
configurations: unit density ratio with and without upstream heating.
Laterally averaged heat transfer coefficient augmentation simulations
using all three turbulence models agreed well with experiments.
However, the spanwise variation in heat transfer coefficient
augmentation in all cases was greater than is typically seen
experimentally.
INTRODUCTION
Many computational studies in literature have compared various
turbulence models with experimental film cooling data. Despite this,
selecting a turbulence model for CFD predictions of film cooling
based on results from literature remains a difficult task due to the
many different studies and recommendations based on limited and
varying criteria. This study aims to find the best turbulence model for
predictions of film cooling on a flat plate.
Many studies have made significant contributions to film cooling
CFD. A systematic computational methodology for CFD simulation of
film-cooling was developed by Walters and Leylek [1]. They proposed
four critical steps necessary for successfully simulating film cooling
including effective turbulence modeling. They simulated adiabatic
effectiveness, η, experiments and compared to data from Sinha et al.
[2] using the standard k-ε turbulence model (SKE) with standard wall
functions (SWF). Less lateral coolant spreading was apparent in the
simulations than experiments and centerline η was not very well
predicted. The authors suggested that some of the disagreement
between computational and experimental results could be explained by
jet skewness in the experiments of Sinha et al. [2]; after correcting for
this skewness, the centerline results showed much better agreement
with experiments.

When using the SKE turbulence model with SWF, Walters and
Leylek [1] were not able to capture the jet separation and reattachment
that was suggested by low η levels close to the hole. They postulated
that separation was not predicted due to the use of wall functions with
limited grid resolution at the wall. The grid was refined such that it
extended into the viscous sublayer and they predicted separation more
accurately. However, as the authors cautioned, SWF are invalid in this
region so the results were just a suggestion that that near-wall
modeling may have led to better predictions. The authors also
predicted that CFD would continue to under-predict lateral spreading
until anisotropic turbulence models were used.
The Walters and Leylek group later studied seven turbulence
modeling approaches in Ferguson et al. [3]: SKE with SWF, SKE with
non-equilibrium wall functions (NEWF), SKE with two layer wall
treatment (2LWT), renormalization group k-ε (RNG) with SWF, RNG
with NEWF, Reynold’s stress model (RSM) with SWF, and RSM with
NEWF. The SKE with 2LWT predicted experimental measurements of
centerline and spanwise η by Sinha at el. [2] more accurately than the
RNG or RSM models at M = 1. The SKE with 2LWT also predicted
separation along the centerline much more accurately than any other
wall treatment. The superiority of the SKE model with 2LWT was
reiterated in Walters and Leylek [4].
Thermal profiles above a film cooled flat plate were examined
computationally by Lakehal et al. [5] using the SKE with SWF and
SKE with 2LWT turbulence models. Simulations using SKE model
with 2LWT resulted in more lateral jet spreading and less jet spreading
normal to the flat plate in comparison to the SKE model with SWF for
M = 0.5. Simulations using 2LWT also showed better agreement with
experimental results by Honami et al. [6] near the wall.
Keimasi and Taribi-Rahni [7] studied the velocity and turbulence
profiles of square jets in crossflow using the SKE with SWF and the kω shear stress transport (SST) turbulence models. Agreement with
experimental data by Ajerch et al. [8] was highly dependent on
position and the flow variable being considered. The study did not
mesh the plenum and also ran simulations with the experimental jet
exit profile set as the boundary condition at the hole exit.
Simulations of a turbine airfoil using the SKE with 2LWT,
realizable k-ε (RKE) with 2LWT, RNG with 2LWT, and RSM with
2LWT turbulence models were run by Walters and Leylek [9] and
compared to experimental turbulence intensity and primary loss
coefficients found by Ito [10] and Ito et al. [11]. The SKE, RKE, and
RNG models were also compared to experimental results for η at M
= 1. They found that overall, the RKE and RSM turbulence models
best predicted experimental results.

Flat plate film cooling using the RKE with 2LWT, SST, and SA
turbulence models was simulated by Na et al. [20] and compared to
measurements by Kohli and Bogard [22]. The study found that the
SST model best predicted laterally averaged effectiveness, η , and the
RKE provided the worst prediction. Conversely, the RKE model best
predicted centerline η and the SST model predicted η worst along the
centerline. The study also used several meshing strategies which
varied in the level of refinement and how the grids were structured
around the hole exit. They found the best results when the grid was
very refined and when the grid lines wrapped around the corners of the
hole exit. However, the small differences between the meshes were
confined to the first few hole diameters.
Gustafsson and Johansson [23] used the RKE with 2LWT, SST,
and the RSM with 2LWT turbulence models and compared the
velocity distributions to experimental data also presented in the paper.
They found that the RSM model best predicted experimental results
and the RKE model resulted in the worst agreement.
Hoda and Acharya [24] simulated velocity and turbulence fields
above the wall with several forms of the k-ε and k-ω turbulence
models and compared the simulations to experiments. The study found
that none of the models captured the flow adequately.
Several computational studies have also compared various
modified forms of turbulence models. Some research groups have
modified certain parameters, some have used a correction for
anisotropic turbulence suggested by Bergeles et al. [25], and some
have used DNS data near the wall to better model the near wall region.
These authors include: Amer et al. [26], Chen et al. [27], Lakehal et al.
[28], Azzi and Lakehal [29], Lakehal [30], Azzi and Jubran [31], and
Bacci and Facchini [32]. Most of these attempts to improve twoequation models have resulted in improved predictions of experimental
data. These results will not be discussed in detail because this study
aims to determine the best turbulence model for modeling both
adiabatic effectiveness and heat transfer without modifying existing
turbulence models. Also, often these modifications result in better
agreement only under certain conditions.
Many computational flat plate film cooling studies have based
simulations on the experiments of Sinha et al. [2] with varying degrees
of success and many compare only to centerline η. Although centerline
η is an important result to match, it is also experimentally difficult to
ensure the centerline values are determined precisely with the array of
thermocouples used in Sinha et al. [2]. The study is undoubtedly a
quality benchmark case and there are advantages to many
computational studies using the same geometry and conditions for
comparisons. However, using more current experimental studies has
the advantage of being able to provide better spatial resolution with
more advanced thermal measurement techniques. Moreover, Walters
and Leylek [1] showed that just the small amount of jet skewness in
Sinha et al. [2] complicates comparisons between computationally
determined centerline η and experimental data. This is a clear
demonstration of the difficulty in measuring η values exactly at the
centerline in studies that use arrays of thermocouples. Yet many
computational studies have continued to compare primarily centerline
η to Sinha et al. [2] and other experimental data sets.
Furthermore, it has been shown repeatedly that simulations with
standard two equation turbulence models generally result in less lateral
spreading than is seen in experiments. This makes two-equation
models difficult to use for accurate predictions of centerline
effectiveness. It is useful to compare laterally averaged adiabatic
effectiveness because over-predicted centerline values are generally

The SST, SKE with 2LWT, and RSM with 2LWT turbulence
models were compared for simulated normal jet film cooling injection
by Javadi et al. [12]. The RSM model best predicted jet velocity
profiles, shear stress, and turbulence kinetic energy found by Ajersch
et al. [8] at various M.
York and Leylek [13] conducted a conjugate heat transfer study
and found the overall effectiveness on the surface of a simulated
turbine vane using the SKE and RKE turbulence models with 2LWT.
The RKE model better matched experimental data by Hylton et al. [14]
than the SKE model.
Flat plate film cooling was investigated computationally by Silieti
et al. [15] using the SKE with 2LWT, RNG with 2LWT, RKE with
2LWT, standard k-ω (SKW), and SST turbulence models. The study
found that close to the hole (x/d < 6), the RKE model best predicted
experimental centerline η data from Gritsch et al. [16] for M = 1.
Farther from the hole (6 < x/d < 10), the SKE model predicted the
experimental data most accurately. The RNG, SKW, and SST models
under-predicted centerline η near the hole and over-predicted η farther
from the hole.
Silieti et al. [17] ran simulations of a fan-shaped film cooling hole
on a flat plate using the RKE with 2LWT, SST, and V2F turbulence
models. Use of the RKE and SST models resulted in very similar
centerline adiabatic effectiveness near the hole, but the SST model
results agreed slightly better with experiments by Gritsch et al. [16] far
from the hole. The V2F model showed the worst agreement with
experimental data. The study also evaluated the results from
hexahedral, hybrid, and tetrahedral meshes, finding that the hexahedral
and hybrid meshes exhibited the closest agreement with experimental
data and were very similar.
Zhang and Hassan [18] modeled flat plate film cooling based on
the geometry and flow conditions in Sinha et al. [2] and compared the
RKE with SWF, RKE with NEWF, SKE with SWF, RNG with SWF,
Spalart-Allmaras (SA), SKW, and RSM with SWF turbulence models
to centerline η at M = 1. The RKE with SWF best captured centerline
jet separation and the study matched experimental data more closely
than previous studies. Whereas past studies have found that agreement
with experiments was worst at the centerline, Zhang and Hassan [18]
matched η experimental results fairly well at the centerline. They
attributed their successful agreement to several factors. First, they used
a structured mesh, which they found to require many fewer cells to
achieve the same accuracy as the unstructured mesh. Second, they
stressed the importance of using correct y+ values and suggested that
SWF could be better than 2LWT (but they did not compare SWF and
2LWT). Third, they found that the height of the domain affected
solution accuracy. A domain height of at least 20d provided the closest
match to experimental data. However, the grid independence study
showed that changing the grid from 450,000 cells to the maximum grid
size of 900,000 cells resulted in changes in centerline η of up to 8%.
They also commented that many studies prior to Sinha et al. [2] may
not have correctly accounted for conduction effects, and this could
explain some scatter in early experimental data. However, most
computational studies compare to Sinha et al. [2] or later experimental
studies.
Another turbulence model comparison analyzing flat plate film
cooling was performed by Hassan and Yavuzkurt [19]. The SKE with
2LWT, RNG with 2LWT, RKE with 2LWT, and SKW turbulence
models were compared to experimental centerline η data. They found
that the RNG and RKE models showed slightly better lateral spreading
than the SKE model, but predicted centerline η measured by Mayhew
[20] less accurately. At M = 0.5, the RKE and RNG models overpredicted the centerline η, but at M = 1.5, they both under-predicted
the centerline η. Conversely, the SKW model vastly under-predicted

COMPUTATIONAL SETUP
Flat plate film cooling was simulated using geometry and flow
conditions based on Baldauf et al. [33]. The geometry and hexahedral
mesh were generated using GAMBIT (part of the FLUENT package)
and cases were solved using FLUENT 6.3. The geometry used in the
simulations is shown in Fig. 1 and the initial mesh of the injection
region is shown in Fig. 2. The mesh for all simulations was developed
with y+ values approximately equal to unity so that near wall treatment
could be used. The maximum stretching ratio normal to the flat plate
wall was 1.25. Three turbulence models were examined: RKE, SKE,
and RSM. For the RKE and RSM models, enhanced wall treatment (a
two layer treatment) was used.
Symmetry boundary conditions at the hole center plane and half
pitch plane between the holes were used to simulate a row of film
cooling holes. To prevent formation of a boundary layer at the top of
the domain, a symmetry boundary condition was also used at the top
of the tunnel. Adiabatic effectiveness simulations were run with the
domain height increased to 20d in addition to the 6d height shown in
Figure 1. The increase in height had no effect on the solution, so 6d
was deemed high enough. All walls were set to adiabatic for the η
simulations and all walls except the top of the flat plate were set to
adiabatic for the heat transfer simulations. For the heat transfer
simulations, a constant heat flux boundary condition (q” = 15 kW/m2)
was applied to the surface of the flat plate.
Simulations were run with and without heating upstream of the
holes. Studies by Coulthard et al. [38] on a flat plate and Harrison et
al. [39] on a turbine vane have demonstrated that there are significant
differences in heat transfer coefficient augmentation with and without
upstream heating. Because upstream heating is a more realistic

Thus, a turbulence model would be very useful if it could accurately
predict laterally averaged adiabatic effectiveness even if it was
incapable of capturing exact values for centerline effectiveness. The
current study is based on the geometry of Baldauf et al. [33] and
centerline, lateral distributions, as well as laterally averaged results
will be presented.
While there are many computational studies comparing
turbulence model predictions of adiabatic effectiveness on a flat plate,
there are fewer computational predictions of heat transfer coefficients.
Many computational studies have simulated heat transfer experiments
on airfoils, but few on flat plates. Ferguson et al. [3] ran heat transfer
simulations and examined centerline and lateral distributions of heat
transfer coefficient augmentation, hf/h0. Results found from using SKE
with SWF, RNG with SWF, and RSM with SWF turbulence models
were compared to experimental data from Sen [34]. For M = 1,
centerline hf/h0 was over-predicted near the hole and under-predicted
far from the hole using the SKE model with SWF. Simulations with
the RNG model with SWF resulted in over-predicted centerline hf/h0
values but merged with the experimental data downstream. Centerline
hf/h0 was best predicted through simulations with the RSM model with
SWF, except very close to the hole. Lateral distributions of hf/h0 using
the SKE with SWF and the RNG with SWF resulted in much greater
variation than found experimentally and hf/h0 values were generally
over-predicted by the simulations. The RSM model with SWF
predicted a laterally constant distribution in hf/h0 and best agreed with
experimental data, although the experimental data does indicate some
lateral variation.
Zhang and Hassan [35] simulated heat transfer experiments by
Eriksen [36] using the SKW, RSM with SWF, SA, SKE with SWF,
SKE with NEWF, and SKE with 2LWT. The RKE model with SWF
resulted in the closest overall agreement with experiments for M = 1,
but the SA and SKW models also resulted in reasonable predictions of
centerline hf/h0. The RSM model resulted in the worst predictions far
from the hole but the best prediction close to the hole. Comparisons of
the SKE model with SWF, NEWF, and 2LWT showed little difference
in centerline hf/h0. The study also compared simulation results to
experiments by Sen [34] and found simulations agreed well with
centerline hf/h0 for M = 1. For M = 0.5, the SKE with SWF underpredicted centerline hf/h0, especially near the hole. Lateral hf/h0
simulation results found by applying a constant heat flux to the flat
plate showed more variation in hf/h0 than was seen experimentally in
Sen [34], as was also shown by Ferguson et al. [3].
Since it is difficult to determine the best turbulence model based
on literature, this study aims to analyze both adiabatic effectiveness
and heat transfer coefficients. The results are used for turbulence
model selection in Harrison and Bogard [37]. To analyze the
turbulence model performance thoroughly, centerline, lateral
distributions, and laterally averaged adiabatic effectiveness and heat
transfer coefficients are compared. Previous turbulence model studies
have not examined the effects of various turbulence models by
comparing laterally averaged, centerline, and lateral distributions for
both adiabatic effectiveness and heat transfer coefficient augmentation.

Blowing ratios of M = 0.5 and M = 1 were examined for both the
adiabatic effectiveness and heat transfer simulations. For η
simulations, density ratios of DR = 1.5 and 1.8 were analyzed. The
heat transfer simulations were run at unit density ratio to compare to
what is commonly used experimentally. In all simulations, the
properties of air were set to vary as either linear or quadratic functions
of temperature since there was considerable density variation.
The flow parameters are summarized in Table 1. The mainstream
velocity was set to nominally 60 meters/second for consistency with
Baldauf et al. [33], and a velocity profile was added at the tunnel inlet
to match the displacement thickness of δ1/d = 0.1 reported at the hole.
The turbulence intensity at the inlet was set to Tu = 1.5% to match
Baldauf et al. [33]. Since values for the turbulence length scales in the
tunnel and plenum and for the turbulence intensity in the plenum were
not reported, the turbulence length scale and turbulence intensity at the
plenum inlet were set to Λ = 1d and Tu = 2%, respectively.
Convergence and grid independence were carefully determined.
Simulations were judged to be converged when each of several
parameters did not change more than 0.005% for at least 100
iterations. The parameters used for convergence criteria were: laterally
averaged adiabatic effectiveness on the surface of the plate, velocity at
the center of the hole exit, and Tu at the center of the hole exit. The
residuals for all parameters dropped by at least five orders of
magnitude (energy at least 7 orders) when the solution had converged
in all simulations.

Regardless of the reason for over-predictions of centerline η, it is
necessary to analyze which turbulence model best captures the correct

CONCLUSIONS
It is difficult to determine the most effective turbulence model for
simulating both film cooling adiabatic effectiveness and heat transfer
coefficients. Recommendations in literature vary and few studies
include both heat transfer and adiabatic effectiveness. Furthermore,
many studies are limited to or focused on centerline adiabatic
effectiveness and are based on studies using arrays of thermocouples
which are very sensitive to thermocouple placement and jet skewness.
This study sought to analyze both adiabatic effectiveness and heat
transfer coefficient augmentation and compare results to experimental
laterally averaged, centerline, and lateral distributions.
The SKW (standard k-ω) model best predicted laterally averaged
adiabatic effectiveness and the RKE (realizable k-ε) model resulted in
the worst predictions. Centerline adiabatic effectiveness was best
predicted by the RKE model and worst predicted by the SKW. All
three turbulence models provide poor predictions of lateral
distributions due to lack of sufficient lateral spreading. Although
anisotropic, use of the RSM (Reynold’s stress) model did not result in
significantly better lateral spreading.
Heat transfer coefficients were also examined. The RKE model
best predicted h0, but all three models predicted reasonable results. All
three models predicted reasonable laterally averaged hf/h0 distributions
but also under-predicted centerline hf/h0 and over-predicted lateral
variations in hf/h0. Use of the RSM model resulted in the least underpredicted centerline augmentation values. All models captured the
effects of upstream heating well.
