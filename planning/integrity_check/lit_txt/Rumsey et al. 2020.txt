Modifications to the quadratic constitutive relations (QCRs) of Spalart (“Strategies for Turbulence Modelling and
Simulation,” International Journal of Heat and Fluid Flow, Vol. 21, 2000, pp. 252–263) and Mani et al. (“Predictions
of a Supersonic Turbulent Flow in a Square Duct,” AIAA Paper 2013-0860, Jan. 2013) are proposed. These
modifications result from computational fluid dynamics (CFD) studies in the FUN3D and OVERFLOW solvers of
the flowfield in the body boundary layer and near-corner region of the NASA Juncture Flow model. When used in
conjunction with the Spalart–Allmaras turbulence model with rotation/curvature correction, the new relations
improve the CFD Reynolds normal stress predictions, as compared with the experiment. This improvement leads
to better predictions of separated corner flow behavior across a wide range of angles of attack that were measured,
somewhat beyond the original QCR effect. The model’s performance in a square duct, driven by turbulence-induced
vortices, is slightly improved. The QCR remains fairly simple to program and compatible with other eddy-viscosity
models.

Introduction

N THIS study, modifications to the quadratic constitutive relations
(QCRs) of Spalart [1] and Mani et al. [2] are proposed, based on

4375

the NASA Juncture Flow (JF) experiment [3–5]. The purpose of the
JF experiment was to provide computational fluid dynamics (CFD)
practitioners with high-quality mean and turbulence flowfield data in
the near-corner region of a wing–fuselage junction that experiences
separation. This area was of concern at the AIAA CFD Drag
Prediction Workshops [6–8]. The test was designed to be a “CFD
validation” experiment by modern standards, with considerable
effort devoted to establishing information, including boundary
conditions and experimental uncertainty, needed by CFD to match
the experimental setup as closely as possible. Very detailed turbulence measurements were also made possible by a creative use of
instrumentation. The key needs from CFD’s perspective are 1) the
assurance that major differences between CFD and experimental
flowfields are due primarily to inadequacies of the turbulence models
and not to inconsistencies in how CFD is physically representing the
experiment, and 2) that errors between CFD and the experiment take
into account all relevant uncertainties (i.e., CFD should be considered
incorrect only if its uncertainty bounds lie outside of realistic uncertainty bounds of the experiment). With these assurances, efforts
to improve the turbulence models can then proceed with more
confidence.
Several Reynolds-averaged Navier–Stokes (RANS) CFD efforts
have been conducted to date, comparing predictions with the JF
experiment [9–14]. These efforts have included grid-resolution studies (to ascertain the influence of numerical discretization errors on
quantities of interest) as well as free-air versus in-tunnel studies
(to ascertain the influence of wind tunnel walls). When making use
of the Spalart–Allmaras (SA) turbulence model [15] with rotation/
curvature correction (RC) [16] and a quadratic constitutive relation
(QCR) (either QCR2000 [1] or QCR2013 [2]), the onset of separation
on the NASA JF model was predicted too far upstream, with separation length in error by roughly 30%. It should be noted that even
though SA-RC-QCR predicted separation onset too far upstream, the
inclusion of QCR dramatically improved the results compared with
SA-RC alone, which predicted about 100% error in separation
length. Significant qualitative improvement from QCR has been seen
in other junction flow studies, both with SA as well as with other
linear models [17–19]. QCR can be applied with other eddy-viscosity
models, normally without adjustment. However, for two-equation
models, the internal value of the turbulent kinetic energy (i.e., the k
solved for by the model) may be preferred to the estimates used with
the one-equation model, which does not have k available.
With detailed near-corner flowfield data available from the NASA
JF experiment, a wealth of new information is now available that
allows for improved assessment of the flaws in existing models,
leading to the ideas in the current paper for how they might be
improved. In the work of Rumsey et al. [12], it was hypothesized
that a main requirement for a turbulence model to be able to predict
the NASA JF corner separation correctly was its ability to capture a
“stress-induced vortex” deep in the junction corner. It is known that
differences between the Reynolds normal stresses are a driver for this
near-wall secondary corner vortical flow [20], which, when present,
acts to inhibit corner separation [21]. Therefore, a key driver in the
case of SA-RC-QCR is QCR’s ability to modify the incorrect behavior of the Reynolds normal stresses in the linear model (e.g., SA-RC
alone) to more closely reproduce the Reynolds normal stresses in the
experiment.
It is important to note that the expression “normal stresses” usually
implies a set of axes, two of which are aligned with the flow direction
and the direction of most rapid variation, respectively. In boundary
layers, the latter direction is normal to the wall. Recall that the
distinction between “normal” and “shear” stress depends on the axes
used [22]. However, throughout this paper, Cartesian axes are used,
with x aligned with the fuselage body, y to the right, and z up. This is
the same coordinate system used in the experiment. Therefore, x is
only approximately streamwise and z approximately normal to the
wing upper surface. The comparisons are valid, but the physical
meaning of some stresses requires attention: for instance, in a twodimensional boundary layer expressed by a velocity uy (with y up
and z the third dimension), both τ13 and τ23 would be zero. Here, this
is no longer necessarily true.

In this paper, proposed modifications to QCR are described.
Improved results are then demonstrated for the NASA JF configuration. The model is also applied to a zero-pressure gradient flat plate
and a supersonic square duct configuration to demonstrate that it does
not negatively impact cases where the original QCR model was
considered adequate.

When applied to the JF configuration, the above (existing) QCR
models in conjunction with SA-RC performed essentially identically
to each other in terms of flowfield prediction. This was expected. As
described in previous CFD studies [10–12], SA-RC-QCR results
were dramatically improved compared with SA-RC alone but were
still somewhat deficient compared with experimental data. In particular, the separation location was predicted too far upstream in the
junction corner. In terms of Reynolds stresses, it was noted that the
near-wall peak of the x-direction Reynolds normal stress component
hu 0 u 0 i was severely underpredicted. The other components also
differed from experiment to some degree, with the normal stress
components tending to be worse than the shear stress components.
For the new version of QCR introduced in this paper (termed
“QCR2020”), it is desired to improve the prediction of this hu 0 u 0 i
peak near the wall, at least to some degree, as well as to balance the
differences between the other Reynolds normal stresses so as to
achieve a more accurate separation location. To accomplish this,
the main change to the QCR model is a modification to the term in
front of Oik τLjk  Ojk τLik . Instead of a constant, the multiplier is

decreases it toward the boundary-layer edge. (The second term

importance, as discussed earlier.) The particular choice of functions

comparisons and separation size. The Reynolds stresses are multifaceted and still leave more room for improvement.

FUN3D [27] was employed on a semispan free-air grid, run fully

used for the calculation of the inviscid terms for all the results in this
paper. The grids from Rumsey et al. [12] were employed.

OVERFLOW [29] was also used on its own set of overset fine grids
[12] to help verify the implementation of the new model in FUN3D.
Like FUN3D, OVERFLOW was run in steady-state mode. A thirdorder Roe upwind scheme [28] was used for the convective fluxes,
and the implicit solve was performed using the Beam–Warming
scalar pentadiagonal scheme and low-Mach preconditioning. OVERFLOW’s grids [12] were somewhat smaller than FUN3D’s, with
about 25.5 million grid points (M) and 86.6 million grid points (F)
for half the model. Sample plots demonstrating the effect of grid for
SA-RC-QCR2020 in OVERFLOW are shown in Fig. 3, along line

work of Rumsey et al. [12].
Sample plots comparing the Reynolds normal stresses for the two
codes are shown in Fig. 4, for line L1. The two codes yielded nearly
identical results for both QCR2013 and QCR2020. At other locations
on the wing, there were small differences between the codes due to the
presence of higher discretization errors in the region near separation
(see additional details from the grid refinement study in the work of
Rumsey et al. [12]). However, the trends resulting from the changes to
QCR were consistent for the two codes. Examples showing the
consistent trends are shown in Fig. 5, for line L2.

Figure 7 shows sample surface-restricted streamlines for α  5°,
comparing SA-RC-QCR2013 with SA-RC-QCR2020 (the surface is
colored by cf;x, the skin friction coefficient in the x direction).
QCR2020 produced a smaller separation region. This was true at
all other angles of attack as well. In all of the QCR models, the
constant (or function) multiplying the Oik τLjk  Ojk τLik  term has an
overwhelming influence on this aspect of the flow. Leger et al. [19]
demonstrated the influence of Ccr1 in QCR2000. Increasing it from its
nominal value of 0.30 improved results very near the corner of a
supersonic square duct compared with LES but also produced additional, nonphysical vortices further from the wall when it was
increased too much. Spalart et al. [32] also found that values of Ccr1 >
0.35 tended to cause instabilities and spurious vortices. QCR2020
appears to overcome this problem because the function, Eq. (11),
increases the multiplier only near the wall but decreasing it away from
the wall.

inside its boundary layer), upstream of separation. Here, only small
differences in mean flow behavior between QCR2013 and QCR2020
are visible, primarily seen in the u component of velocity near
the wall in Fig. 9a. The Reynolds normal stresses from QCR2020
(Fig. 9b) are in better agreement with the experiment than QCR2013.
In particular, note the larger spread between the components, which
better approach the experimental levels. Again, there are only minor
differences in the Reynolds shear stresses of Fig. 9c.
With improved predictions of the Reynolds normal stresses
upstream of separation, the QCR2020 model yielded a far-more
accurate separation prediction than QCR2013. In Fig. 10, results
are shown along line L3, very close to where separation occurred in
the experiment. Indeed, the QCR2020 results were far superior to
QCR2013, which separated too far upstream.
B. Other Canonical Test Cases
Fig. 6 Length and width of separation size with angle of attack.

The influence of the modified QCR can be seen in Figs. 8–10, for
the case of α  5°. Figure 8 shows results along line L1, far upstream
on the nose, where most state-of-the-art turbulence models are
expected to perform well in terms of the mean flowfield. Indeed,
both SA-RC-QCR2013 and SA-RC-QCR2020 yielded essentially
identical mean flow profiles, Fig. 8a, in very good agreement with the
experiment. However, QCR2020 produced significant differences in
Reynolds normal stress profiles, seen in Fig. 8b. In particular, near the
wall the QCR2020 spread the three profiles apart more than
QCR2013. Although still not in very good agreement with the experiment (and particularly low in its absolute prediction of near-wall
hu 0 u 0 i), QCR2020 tended to at least move the profile trends in the
right direction. It did a better job than QCR2013 both in terms of
spread between the components as well as in absolute levels. As
expected after examining equations such as Eq. (10), QCR2020
yielded only very minor differences in Reynolds shear stress profiles
compared with QCR2013, as seen in Fig. 8c. Note the nonzero values
of the “apparent shear stresses” hu 0 w 0 i and hv 0 w 0 i, which are zero by
symmetry in a 2D case if x is streamwise; the reason why they are not
zero here is that the flow direction is not quite aligned with the x axis.
As is well known, the Reynolds normal stresses have little influence on the mean flow in a standard wall boundary layer like that

QCR2020 was calibrated using JF data, so it is perhaps no surprise
that it improved results for that case. We now briefly examine the
model’s behavior for two other canonical test cases, to verify that it
still performs as least as well as QCR2013. Here, the FUN3D and
OVERFLOW results were essentially the same, so only FUN3D
results are shown.
1. Flat Plate

The effect of QCR2020 compared with QCR2013 is shown in
Fig. 11 for a canonical flat plate case from the NASA Langley
Turbulence Modeling Resource (TMR) website [33]. The
545 × 385 grid from the TMR website was employed. For this case,
Reynolds number is 5 million based on plate length and the Reθ at the
location shown is approximately 7600. Scaled DNS results from
Schlatter and Orlu [34] are also included in the figure for reference,
although the Reθ of the DNS was somewhat smaller (4061). The
Reynolds shear stress hu 0 v 0 i for SA-QCR2020 and SA-QCR2013
are identical and in good agreement with the DNS. Regarding the
Reynolds normal stresses, QCR2020 generally does a better job than
QCR2013 in capturing the DNS trends. Mainly, QCR2020 is seen to
produce a lower overall hv 0 v 0 i, a somewhat sharper hw 0 w 0 i near the
wall, and a much sharper hu 0 u 0 i near the wall. The former two
components are in reasonable agreement with the DNS, but hu 0 u 0 i
is still severely underpredicted by the model. Observe the strong
similarity between Figs. 8b and 11, which attests to the accuracy of
the JF measurements.

2. Supersonic Square Duct

The supersonic square duct case is widely used to demonstrate
the need for a nonlinear constitutive relation for capturing stressinduced secondary motion in corners. Experimental data are from
Davis and Gessner [35]. The reference Mach number is 3.9 and
Reynolds number based on channel height is 508,000. Results for
many existing turbulence models are provided on the TMR
website [33].
Comparisons are made in Fig. 12 between the new SA-QCR2020
model and the existing SA-QCR2000 and SA-QCR2013 models.
The 481 × 81 × 81 grid from the TMR website was employed. As
seen in Figs. 12a–12c, QCR2020 still yielded very similar results to
both QCR2000 and QCR2013 in terms of the vortical motions
induced by the Reynolds normal stress differences. However, in
QCR2020, the magnitude of the velocity driven into the corner
along the diagonal was somewhat increased. The normalized u
velocity along both a diagonal and a vertical cut, shown in Fig. 12d,
was essentially unchanged and was in very good agreement with the
experiment. The QCR2020 skin friction coefficient shown in
Fig. 12e was slightly improved compared with the older models.
In other words, the modifications made to better reproduce the JF
measurements were slightly favorable in a fully independent
test case.

IV.

Conclusions

Proposed modifications to the QCR of Spalart and Mani et al. were
shown to improve CFD results for the NASA JF configuration.
Predicted corner separation with the new model (in conjunction with
SA-RC) agreed better with the experiment. Although the Reynolds
normal stresses were still not in very good agreement with the experiment, the new model pushed them in the right direction, particularly
in regard to the spread between the components near the walls. Flat
plate results demonstrated somewhat improved Reynolds normal
stresses, with no change to the Reynolds shear stress or mean flow
results, as expected. The model also did no harm to (in fact it slightly

improved) results for a supersonic square duct, for which the original
QCR model versions were already considered to be adequate.
