Numerical Simulations of the
Near-Field Region of Film
Cooling Jets Under High Free
Stream Turbulence: Application
of RANS and Hybrid URANS/
Large Eddy Simulation Models
This paper investigates the flow field and thermal characteristics in the near-field region
of film cooling jets through numerical simulations using Reynolds-averaged Navier–
Stokes (RANS) and hybrid unsteady RANS (URANS)/large eddy simulation (LES) models.
Detailed simulations of flow and thermal fields of a single-row of film cooling cylindrical
holes with 30 deg inline injection on a flat plate are obtained for low (M ¼ 0.5) and high
(M ¼ 1.5) blowing ratios under high free stream turbulence (FST) (10%). The realizable
k-e model is used within the RANS framework and a realizable k-e-based detached eddy
simulation (DES) is used as a hybrid URANS/LES model. Both models are used together
with the two-layer zonal model for near-wall simulations. Steady and time-averaged
unsteady film cooling effectiveness obtained using these models are compared with available experimental data. It is shown that hybrid URANS/LES models (DES in the present
paper) predict more mixing both in the wall-normal and spanwise directions compared to
RANS models, while unsteady asymmetric vortical structures of the flow can also be captured. The turbulent heat flux components predicted by the DES model are higher than
those obtained by the RANS simulations, resulting in enhanced turbulent heat transfer
between the jet and mainstream, and consequently better predictions of the effectiveness.
Nevertheless, there still exist some discrepancies between numerical results and
experimental data. Furthermore, the unsteady physics of jet and crossflow interactions
and the jet lift-off under high FST is studied using the present DES results.

Introduction
High turbine inlet temperatures are required to obtain high efficiency in advanced gas turbines. Therefore, it is typical to have
turbine inlet temperatures as high as 1400  C [1]. However,
enhancing the thermal performance of gas turbines by increasing
the turbine inlet temperature may cause irreparable damage to the
blades. Therefore, a reasonable lifetime of turbine blades can only
be achieved by an efficient surface cooling mechanism such as
film cooling. In film cooling, a secondary fluid (coolant) is
injected through several holes on a surface, which forms an air
film with lower temperature between the surface and hot mainstream, and protects the surface from overheating. The fullcoverage film cooling [2] incorporates several rows of holes over
the entire surface, resulting in not only more uniform heat transfer
rates on the surface but also protection of the whole surface which
is desirable [3]. This, however, gives rise to the importance of the
study of the near-field region in the film cooling since in the fullcoverage film cooling, which is more widely used nowadays, the
whole surface is in the near-field of the cooling jets.

Making significant advances in the cooling technology requires
a fundamental understanding of the physical mechanisms
involved in film cooling flow fields. The low cost and availability
of a detailed set of data, relative to experimental measurements,
make computational fluid dynamics (CFD) an important tool for
film cooling investigations. In addition to the direct application of
CFD simulations in several stages of the film cooling design, they
can be used to develop film cooling effectiveness correlations [4]
which are essential tools for turbine manufacturers. Most previous
film cooling flow predictions have been carried out employing
the steady RANS equations with two-equation turbulence
models [5–9]. Although the RANS simulations were generally
able to predict the gross flow and heat transfer behavior, they fail
to correctly describe the anisotropic turbulence associated with
the jet–crossflow interactions. Therefore, as one of their known
deficiencies, RANS simulations underpredict the lateral spreading
and mixing of the coolant jet [10]. Furthermore, steady RANS
simulations are unable to capture unsteady phenomena associated
with mixing of the jet and the crossflow in film cooling. Experimental studies [11] show that the film cooling flow field is highly
unsteady and complex with secondary flows and various types of
vortical structures which dominate the mixing process in the nearfield region. Therefore, unsteady simulations are necessary in
order to properly capture inherently unsteady features of the film
cooling flow.

In the unsteady simulation framework, LES, which directly
resolves the more influential, energy carrying eddies and models
only the small (subgrid) scales, has been widely used. Tyagi and
Acharya [12] performed LES of film cooling flow from inclined
cylindrical jets. They were able to identify various vortical structures, i.e., counter-rotating vortex pair (CRVP), roller vortices,
and upright wake vortices, within the film cooling flow field and
present them in terms of basic hairpin coherent structures. The
time-averaged results obtained from their LES show good agreement with experimental data, although a few discrepancies were
seen at stations farther downstream. LES of film cooling flow was
carried out by Peet and Lele [13] by including the supply plenum
in the computational domain. Unsteady flow above the test surface, as well as inside the plenum and the film hole was investigated. The laterally averaged film cooling effectiveness was slightly
overpredicted, while good agreement for the centerline effectiveness
was obtained for the blowing ratio of 0.5. Johnson and Kapat [14]
used LES with dynamic Smagorinsky subgrid scale model for simulation of film cooling flow and heat transfer. Their results showed
that LES predicts the development and mixing of the jet well, except
in areas close to the injection hole and near the wall. The initial mixing of the coolant and the crossflow was underpredicted by 30–50%
right after the hole. Furthermore, the coolant coverage was underpredicted near the wall even further downstream. They argued that
under-resolution of the near-wall and near-field mixing layers or failure of the simplistic Smagorinsky subgrid model result in underestimation of the mixing. In fact, the near-wall difficulty is well known
in the context of LES, which is mainly due to the anisotropy of all
scales (even the small ones) near the wall (more details are given in
the Hybrid URANS/LES Modeling section).
An alternative solution is to apply hybrid URANS/LES models.
They are intended to combine the advantages of LES and URANS
models. However, comparing to LES, there are considerably
fewer studies attempting on investigating unsteady features of the
film cooling flow using hybrid URANS/LES models. DES is the
most commonly used hybrid URANS/LES model. The first
attempt on simulation of film cooling flow with DES was reported
by Roy et al. [15] who applied a Spalart–Allmaras based DES
[16] model to a film-cooled flat plate with blowing ratio of unity.
It was shown that DES greatly enhances the realistic description
of the dynamic anisotropic mixing processes comparing to RANS
simulations. However, DES predictions of the laterally averaged
film cooling effectiveness showed no more accuracy than that of
the RANS model, which were partly attributed to forcing a symmetry boundary condition at the hole centerline inhibiting the
growth of three-dimensional asymmetric instabilities. Kim and
Hassan [17] performed unsteady simulations of flat plate film
cooling using URANS, LES, and DES models. They showed that
results of the URANS simulations become almost the same as
those of the steady RANS simulations, while no unsteady vortical
structure can be captured. LES and DES results, on the other
hand, showed the coherent vortical structures of the film cooling
flow field. Both LES and DES models, however, underpredicted
the time-averaged film cooling effectiveness in the near-field
region, and overpredicted it far downstream.
This paper investigates the flow field and heat transfer in film
cooling with a single-row of cylindrical holes (30 deg inline injection) on a flat plate applying RANS and hybrid URANS/LES models. Some of the distinctive features of the present simulations are:
(1) considering a test case with high FST, closer to actual conditions of gas turbines
(2) applying time-dependent, perturbed inlet boundary conditions in order to take the effects of inlet turbulent fluctuations into account
(3) considering both low and high blowing ratios

Hybrid URANS/LES Modeling
In the LES approach, the large, energy carrying, dynamically
important, and flow-dependent eddies are solved directly, leaving

only smallest scale of turbulence with very low energy and supposedly universal behavior (assumed isotropic) to be modeled.
The application of LES has been particularly successful in nonequilibrium flows in free shear layers, and in massively separated
flows in which the accurate simulation of regions near the wall is
not of primary importance [18]. However, one of the major
obstacles to the use of LES in complex industrial flows, such as
film cooling, is the modeling of the near-wall region [19]. The dynamics of the flow near the wall are strongly anisotropic even at
small scales and turbulence production in this region is associated
with an upscale energy cascade (energy transfer from smaller
eddies near the wall, where turbulence production is maximum, to
larger eddies away from the wall) that is largely dominant over
the commonly assumed downscale energy cascade (energy transfer from larger to smaller eddies which eventually results in dissipation of the turbulent kinetic energy (TKE)), presenting
elsewhere. Also, the large eddies that must be captured on the
computational grid to perform an accurate LES shrink in size and
are not isotropic as one approaches the wall, leading to excessive
computational costs [18]. In fact, the small eddies are not isotropic
near a wall either. Current LES modeling approaches require that
either the near-wall region be adequately resolved (using a direct
numerical simulation (DNS)-like grid near the wall which makes
it inapplicable for industrial flows), or that an LES wall-model
(e.g., a two-layer model [20] or a dynamic wall model [21]) be
used, which to date has not provided accurate results in relatively
complex flows such as a nonequilibrium high Reynolds number
boundary layer [22] and an airfoil near stall condition [23]. Contrary to the LES, the weakness in the RANS simulations is that
the resolved large scale unsteady motions are often damped out by
the turbulence model. The near-wall modeling, on the other hand,
is often quite accurate since all these models are developed and
calibrated for near-wall flows.
Hybrid URANS/LES methods aim at combining the best of
RANS and LES turbulence models. In this approach, the URANS
models are employed in the near-wall region, while LES treatment
is applied to regions away from the wall. The most common type
of hybrid URANS/LES models is the DES [16]. The term “DES”
comes from the fact that this model is meant to resolve the large
eddies far from any boundary, i.e., “detached eddies,” in an LESlike manner. Implementation of the DES model is very simple. It
is based on using the same background URANS model with different length scales depending on the local grid resolution. A general definition of the DES length scale, compatible with any
RANS model can be written as [24]

Test Case Description. The majority of the previous experimental [34,35] and numerical [5–7,12–15,17] studies on the film
cooling flow have been performed under low (1%) FST. However, the flow in a gas turbine engine typically has FST on the
order of 10–20% in the regions where film cooling is employed,
and turbulence in the free stream is highly anisotropic. FST introduces additional length scales, compounding an already complex
problem. It is shown that correctly predicting flows under high
FST is a challenge even in relatively simple geometries [36].
Therefore, a test case with more realistic level of FST, namely a
turbulence intensity of 10% at cooling holes, is chosen for this
study. It is worthy to mention here that there are very few studies
in the literature where enough information is supplied for a good
simulation, especially under high FST.

The three-dimensional test case for the present study is chosen
according to the experiments of Mayhew et al. [37], which has
been numerically investigated by Yavuzkurt and Hassan [8] and
Yavuzkurt and Habte [9]. A schematic view of the test setup is
shown in Fig. 1. It consists of a single-row of three jet holes on a
flat surface, which forms the top surface of the test section, with a
30 deg inline injection angle. The diameter of the film cooling
hole is D ¼ 12.5 mm, and holes have a length-to-diameter ratio of
4.6 and a pitch-to-diameter ratio of 3. Experiments were performed in a wind tunnel with a free stream flow velocity and temperature of 25 m/s and 298 K, respectively. The FST intensity was
10% at the upstream edge of the film cooling holes. The “coolant”
fluid which is actually heated air in these experiments was
injected from a supply plenum as shown in Fig. 1. Experimental
measurements of the film cooling adiabatic effectiveness g were
documented by Mayhew et al. [37] for three blowing ratios of 0.5,
1.0, and 1.5 with injection jet temperatures of 323 K (typical) and
307 K (only for the case of M ¼ 0.5).

In practice, the cooling process is strongly influenced by turbulent fluctuations in the mainstream which become more important
under high FST. Ziefle and Kleiser [39] compared two cases of
film cooling flow simulations, with and without including the
effect of crossflow turbulence, and concluded that unsteady inflow
fluctuations yield a more realistic reproduction of the flow structure and mixing behavior. Considering the higher level of FST in
the present case compared to the case investigated in Ref. [39], it
is important to include the effect of unsteady turbulent fluctuations in the mainstream. In this study, the influence of inflow turbulent fluctuations is taken into account by applying a perturbed
inlet boundary condition in unsteady simulations. A practical
approach to obtain perturbed inlet condition is to superimpose random noise perturbations on the mean velocity profiles. Imposing
fluctuations as close as possible to those present in the real flow is
crucial. Unphysical randomness will be damped rapidly, hence inhibiting the purpose of the method. To generate a time-dependent,
perturbed inlet condition, a random 2D vortex method developed
by Mathey et al. [40] is used in this study. In this method, a perturbation is added to the mean velocity profiles via a fluctuating vorticity field. This vorticity field is 2D in the plane normal to the
mean flow direction. In each time step, the vortex positions are
given randomly at the inlet plane. The vortices are then given a
random rotation sense such that the total TKE of vortices becomes
the same as the mean value specified by the inlet TKE profile.
These vortices then induce a fluctuating velocity based on the
Biot–Savart law. The use of this method requires the specification
of the mean TKE and dissipation rate profiles at the inlet of the
computational domain, which are available in the present case.
The accuracy of this method has been investigated using several
test cases [40,41].

A detailed grid sensitivity analysis is carried out using several
grid configurations and the optimized configuration is chosen for
the numerical simulations. In steady simulations, increasing number of cells from the optimized grid by about 50% results in less
than 3% change in predictions of film cooling effectiveness. Furthermore, attention is paid to make sure that this grid is also
within the range of the guidelines for DES grids [32]. In general,
in hybrid URANS/LES simulations, more cells expectedly may
lead to a better solution; however, the required computing
resource and time significantly increases. The final computational
grid is fully structured and consists of 1,427,640 hexahedral cells.
The cells are arranged in such a way that they are aligned with the
anticipated flow direction to reduce numerical diffusion. This will
greatly reduce computation time and prevent solution instabilities.

The plenum inlet velocity is varied so as to impose the required
blowing ratio M, and main free stream and plenum inlet temperatures are set according to Mayhew et al. [37]. The no-slip and adiabatic conditions are imposed on the walls (i.e., film-cooled
surface, jet hole, and plenum walls) and zero gradient conditions
are used at the outflow boundary. The computational extent in the
y direction is far enough (10D) from the near-field region such
that a slip condition with zero normal gradients could be applied
on the upper boundary with confidence. A periodic boundary condition is applied on the midpitch planes located at z/D ¼ 61.5,
since it is stated in the experimental study [37] that the flow has
an equal distribution through all holes.
Simulations are performed for low (M ¼ 0.5) and high
(M ¼ 1.5) blowing ratios using both RANS and hybrid URANS/
LES models in ANSYS-FLUENT 14.0 [29]. The steady RANS
simulations are terminated when two criteria are met simultaneously. First, the solution residuals should drop at least four orders
of magnitude. As the second criterion, the mass-weighted average
temperature (the mass-weighted average of a quantity on a surface
is computed by dividing the surface integral of that quantity multiplied by the surface mass flux by the integral of the surface mass
flux) is monitored in the y–z plane at the streamwise position
x/D ¼ 10. When this average temperature remains constant (less
than 0.1% change) by increasing iterations, the steady solution is
considered to be converged. In the URANS/LES simulations, the
physical time step size is taken as 105 s. This time step is used to
ensure that the courant number (CFL number) is below unity in
all simulations. The maximum CFL number is found to be approximately 0.75 in the present unsteady simulations. The convergence
criterion of the residuals for each time step is set to three orders of
magnitude drop or maximum 30 subiterations. Unsteady simulations are carried out for about 15 through-flow time (the time
requires by the mean flow to pass through the domain once), corresponding to 25,000 time-steps. Each time-step takes about 48 s
on an eight-processor LINUX cluster.

Figure 9 illustrates the streamwise variations of centerline film
cooling effectiveness. Time-averaged DES results are compared
to steady RANS results as well as experimental data [37]. The
RANS simulations overpredict the centerline effectiveness in the
near-field region, which is directly related to the underprediction
of mixing between the jet and crossflow in this region. The similar
observation has been made by several previous researchers who
used RANS models [5,7]; however, the discrepancy level between
RANS simulations and data is larger here comparing to the previous studies under low FST [5,7,38]. The inadequacy of RANS

models is more obvious in this case with high FST. Applying a
hybrid URANS/LES model and perturbed crossflow velocity, the
enhanced level of mixing between the jet and freestream is better
predicted both in the vertical and spanwise directions and results
of the centerline effectiveness are improved considerably (40% on
average) as shown in Fig. 9. Far downstream of the injection hole
(x/D > 15), where FST is dissipated and the jet is well mixed with
the crossflow, RANS and DES simulations give similar results
which are in very close agreement with the experimental data.
This implies the relative importance of the turbulence closure
model in simulations of the near-field region. The distributions of
the spanwise-averaged film cooling effectiveness are shown in
Fig. 10, where the RANS and the DES results are compared. No
experimental data are available for the spanwise-averaged effectiveness in Mayhew et al. [37]; however, this figure is included to
compare simulation results of the two turbulence closure
approaches. Both models predict a peak value for the spanwiseaveraged effectiveness in the near-field region (around x/D ¼ 5)
which is associated with maximum overall coverage of the surface. Very near the hole, the DES predicts higher effectiveness
which is attributed to predicting higher spanwise mixing just
downstream of the hole. The coolant is predicted to be spread

In order to compare the ability of RANS and hybrid URAN/
LES models in turbulent heat transport (velocity–temperature correlations) predictions, turbulent heat flux vector ui h is investigated. Figures 15–17 show the predictions of the streamwise,
wall-normal, and spanwise distributions of nondimensional turbulent heat flux vector at the hole centerline plane and for different
streamwise locations, i.e., x/D. RANS results are based on the
reconstruction of ui h components using Eq. (3), while DES results
are the combinations of resolved and unresolved parts. As x/D
increases, the temperature fluctuations and gradients reduce due to

the mixing between the coolant air and the mainstream, thus, the
turbulent heat flux components decrease.
Figure 15 displays the distributions of the dimensionless
streamwise component of turbulent heat flux uh. It has a positive
sign near the wall and a negative sign in the outer region. This is
due to the fact that the temperature gradient inside the boundary
layer in the streamwise direction is negative while it is positive
outside. (Note the negative sign in Eq. (3) and the fact that in the
case of Mayhew et al. [37], the jet has a higher temperature than
the mainstream). The RANS and DES models show relatively
similar trends for this component. However, the DES model predicts considerably higher values. The wall-normal component of
the turbulent heat flux vh, as represented in Fig. 16, has a negative
sign near the wall where the jet is (partially) detached and a positive sign farther away (between the jet core and far-field away
from the wall). The discrepancy between the RANS and DES predictions is relatively small for this component, confirming the fact
that RANS simulations are known to be adequate in predicting the
mixing in the wall-normal direction. The distributions of the spanwise component of the turbulent heat flux wh are illustrated in
Fig. 17. Again, DES predicts higher levels for this component
compared to RANS. It can be concluded, in general, that applying
the hybrid URANS/LES model causes an increase in the predicted
components of turbulent heat flux, resulting in enhancing the heat
transfer between the coolant air and mainstream.
The centerline effectiveness obtained using time-averaged DES
and steady RANS simulations are plotted in Fig. 18 in comparison
with experimental data [37]. Obviously, the centerline effectiveness is reduced in comparison with low blowing ratio (Fig. 9) due
to the jet lift-off and stronger CRVP. The RANS simulations not
only cannot predict enough mixing between the jet and crossflow
in spanwise direction, they also fail to capture the jet lift-off phenomenon in this case. This results in overprediction of the centerline effectiveness. Nevertheless, this overprediction remains even
farther downstream due to the higher momentum within the jet
core and RANS simulations being unable to model the correct
momentum transport of a jet in cross flow. The results obtained
using DES model show improvements in the near-field region due
to enhanced jet penetration simulations both in spanwise and
wall-normal directions. However, it seems that the extent of the
jet lift-off is overpredicted by DES, resulting in an underprediction of g between x/D ¼ 2 and x/D ¼ 6. Farther downstream where
the jet is predicted to be reattaching to the surface, the centerline
effectiveness is overpredicted, although the results are improved
compared to the steady RANS case. The distributions of the
spanwise-averaged effectiveness for the case of M ¼ 1.5 are illustrated in Fig. 19, showing a distinctly different behavior in comparison to the case of M ¼ 0.5. Here, the effectiveness is low
immediately behind the hole and increases toward downstream of
the hole. Again, no experimental data is available for this parameter in Ref. [37]; however, the overall trend of the predictions is
consistent with observations in experimental studies with high
blowing ratio [34].

Considering the results shown in this paper, it is concluded that
predictions can be improved by using hybrid URANS/LES models
in comparison to the RANS simulations. However, there still exist
discrepancies with the experimental data, which need to be thoroughly investigated. Using the DES model introduces various
sources of error. One issue to be aware of is the one associated
with the interfacing the RANS and the LES regions in DES
model. In the interface region, also called the “gray area” [44], the
disparity between the scales imposed by the RANS and the
smaller eddies required by the LES to support the momentum
transport may result in errors. This error becomes severe in cases
including both attached and separated boundary layers. In the
regions immediately after the separation, the LES should be carried out and the majority of the TKE should be resolved. However, this resolved turbulence is not convected from the solution
of the RANS region. Therefore, when the DES switches to the

LES mode, the lack of initial resolved turbulence may result in a
solution in the gray area that is neither RANS nor LES. The extent
and severity of this problem is highly case-specific. In massively
separated flows such as bluff body wakes and airfoils at high angle
of attack, this problem is negligible. However, in film cooling
applications, where the boundary layer separates from and reattaches to the surface this becomes an important source of error in
predictions. Figure 20 shows the distributions of LES and RANS
regions in DES simulations on a spanwise plane located at
x/D ¼ 5. Contours of the instantaneous dimensionless temperature
(defined in Eq. (7)) are also shown in this figure. The boundary

between the RANS and the LES solutions is clearly seen.
As expected, RANS is applied near the wall and LES is carried out
in the outer region. The region around the switching boundary is
the gray area which may affect the DES results. The net effect is
known to be the overestimation of the gradients in the interface
region [45], which may explain the overprediction of the mixing
seen in the DES simulations. Another issue associated with the
DES model is that it does not include a clearly defined subgrid
scale model when it switches to the LES mode [46]. The model
returns a Smagorinsky-like model only if a local equilibrium is
assumed in both the k and e equations, which is unlikely to happen
in a complex flow such as film cooling which is far from the equilibrium conditions. It has been argued [46] that the basic idea of
DES (Eq. (1)) should be applied to all turbulence variables and not
only to the turbulent length scale in the dissipation term of k (as in
Eq. (4)). Kok et al. [47] proposed a model named Extra LES where
the length scale modification is applied to both dissipation term of
k as well as the eddy viscosity expression. Finally, it should be
noted that the physics of the jet–crossflow interactions is quite
complex under high blowing ratio and high FST. In this case, the
high FST entrains some of the coolant that penetrates into the main
flow and mixes it again with the air near the surface [37]. Part of
these phenomena occurs near the wall where the hybrid URANS/
LES model acts as a URANS model. Besides, it has been shown
that large scale turbulent eddies of the free stream can penetrate to
within yþ ¼ 15 of the wall [48]. Therefore, the role of the RANS/
URANS part in the hybrid URANS/LES modeling is quite important in the film cooling predictions, specifically under high FST.
Hence, also modifications/improvements of RANS models for better prediction of the near-wall region within the hybrid URANS/
LES framework have future prospects. Studies in all these directions to improve hybrid URANS/LES predictions are continuing.

models. The emphasis is on the evaluations of turbulence closure
models in predicting the near-field region where the injected jet
interacts with the main stream. Three main distinctive traits of the
present simulations are the inclusion of (1) high FST (10% turbulence intensity at cooling holes), (2) fluctuating inlet boundary
conditions, and (3) low (M ¼ 0.5) and high (M ¼ 1.5) blowing
ratios. It is shown that under high FST and low blowing ratio,
RANS simulations underpredict jet and crossflow mixing both in
vertical and spanwise directions. This results in overprediction of
centerline effectiveness in the near-field region. Applying hybrid
URANS/LES models (DES in the present paper), enhances the
level of mixing, as well as unsteady nonsymmetric features of the
flow can be captured properly. The contours of adiabatic effectiveness obtained from DES simulations show better agreement
with those given by experimental measurements, while RANS
simulations overpredict the length of the jet “footprint” on the
film-cooled surface by as much as two film-hole diameters. Furthermore, predictions of the centerline effectiveness are improved
by on average 40% using DES model. The difference between experimental data, time-averaged DES, and steady RANS simulations decreases toward downstream of the plate. The case with
high blowing ratio exhibits jet lift-off and detachment, and strong
CRVP, captured by the DES model. The CRVP decrease the film
cooling effectiveness by bringing the mainstream underneath the
jet near the wall (entrainment). Utilizing the hybrid URANS/LES
model causes an increase in the predicted turbulent heat flux. This
results in enhanced turbulent heat transfer between the coolant
and mainstream and consequently lowers (corrects) the values of
centerline film cooling effectiveness. Nevertheless, correct prediction of the near-filed region with high blowing ratio and high FST
is quite challenging even using hybrid URANS/LES models. Various sources of error associated with the DES model is discussed
in this paper. Therefore, further investigations of these models in
film cooling applications are still required and should be considered as future steps for simulations of this type of flows.
